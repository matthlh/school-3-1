## Clarifications (checked against the posted slides, `lectures/01-course_introduction.pdf`, 23 slides)
- **Verified.** Sep 10 is "Introduction" on the schedule. The monster exercise is not on the slides; it was an in-class activity. Your read is right: it set up the course thesis, it was not content.
- **The one line to know (slide 8):** *"Software engineering is the discipline of managing what a change costs as a system keeps growing."* Every unit is this at a bigger radius.
- **210 vs 310 (slides 4, 6).**
  - 210: small code, single version, solo, one prompt.
  - 310: large codebase, multi-version, collaborative, and **changing requirements**. The course calls this "socio-technical".
- **The definition quoted (slides 3 and 5, reader intro):** "The process of transforming a mental plan of desired actions for a computer into a representation that can be understood by the computer." That is Hoc & Nguyen-Xuan.
- **Three fluencies (slide 12, Holmes 2026):** decomposition, requirements, validation. Slide 11's punchline: with AI agents doing "source code assistance" and "vibe coding", engineers must know *requirements, design, and validation*. Writing code is the part that got cheap.
- **Roadmap (slide 15).**
  - Weeks 2–5: inside a codebase you own. Why is this change expensive here and cheap there?
  - Weeks 6–7: at its boundaries. Where should the boundaries be, and what happens once you publish one?
  - Weeks 8–13: across a system and team. How does a team change one codebase without breaking each other?
- **Reader for this lecture:** Introduction (What is SE) and the Analytical Code Design intro. Terms that only appear in the reader:
  - SE is "specify, invent, design, build, validate, deploy, maintain, research, improve". Programming is only the *build* step.
  - **Code** is short, isolated, run once. **Software** is large, connected, longstanding.
