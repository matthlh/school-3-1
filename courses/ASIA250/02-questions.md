## Lec 1 — deck-only claims (logged 2026-09-15)

### Q: Give the course definition of "religion" in full, then name the three kinds of supernatural idea it rests on with one example each.
**Topic:** Defining religion, myth and ritual  **Lec:** 1  **Type:** recall
**A:** A set of articulated and reenacted solutions (narratives and rituals) to the human predicament, premised on the ideas of and interaction with the supernatural, adhered to by a community in some institutionalised form. The three kinds are supernatural agents (gods, spirits, souls), supernatural states (nirvana, heaven, hell, afterlife), and cosmic justice (karma, redemption, damnation).

### Q: Hyon Gak says the world is an illusion, only the true self is real and never born or dies, that self is the Buddha mind, and attachment to the illusion leads to suffering. Sort those claims into supernatural agent, supernatural state, and cosmic justice, and say what that shows about his own argument.
**Topic:** Defining religion, myth and ritual  **Lec:** 1  **Type:** apply
**A:** The Buddha mind that is never born and never dies is a supernatural agent. Nirvana as liberation from illusion is a supernatural state. Attachment leading to suffering is cosmic justice. His own teaching meets every clause of the course definition, so by that definition Buddhism is a religion and his "no creator God" test is the wrong test.

### Q: Who is Hyon Gak? Give his birth name, origin, how he came to Buddhism, which tradition he belongs to, and where he teaches now.
**Topic:** Defining religion, myth and ritual  **Lec:** 1  **Type:** recall
**A:** Born Paul Muenzen in New Jersey in 1964. He converted from Catholicism while a graduate student at Harvard, spent over a decade as a monk in Korean temples, belongs to Korean Seon (Zen), and now teaches Buddhist meditation in Germany.

### Q: State the course definition of "myth", then say what myths express (two things) and what they are not.
**Topic:** Defining religion, myth and ritual  **Lec:** 1  **Type:** recall
**A:** Myth is a form of allegory: a story, poem or pictorial description that needs to be interpreted to reveal a hidden meaning, typically moral or political. Myths express the WHY (origins, place in the world, identity: a story a people tell themselves about themselves) and WHAT IS TRUE (profound truth claims in symbolic form). They are not historical or scientific descriptions of the world.

### Q: The Buddha is born from his mother's side, walks seven steps, and announces "I alone am the holy one". Give the lecture's allegorical reading of each element.
**Topic:** Defining religion, myth and ritual  **Lec:** 1  **Type:** apply
**A:** Birth from the side means freedom from attachment to the world. The seven steps mean the future Buddha is a god born as a human. The announcement is a retrospective claim that the Buddha is superior to all the Hindu gods and stands outside the illusory world, telling an Indian listener that Buddhism is better than India's other religions.

### Q: Name the two orientations for interpreting myth and say which one the lecture prefers and why.
**Topic:** Defining religion, myth and ritual  **Lec:** 1  **Type:** recall
**A:** The first disregards content and locates the meaning elsewhere, which is reductive (the myth rests on mistaken biology or history). The second takes the literal content seriously and works to put its bizarre elements in context. The lecture works in the second mode, because the strange elements are where the meaning is, and interpretation resolves the dissonance they create.

### Q: Define ritual as the lecture does, then name its three components and say what each one is.
**Topic:** Defining religion, myth and ritual  **Lec:** 1  **Type:** recall
**A:** Ritual is a complex of behaviour and language whose performance brings about an effect, transforming the participants or their environment, with a before, during and after. Rituals follow a script (pronouncements acted out as if really happening, bringing a story to life), have a purpose (a narrative destination: separation from the past and acceptance of a new future), and work within a cosmology (the worldview in which every participant finds a place).

### Q: Define liturgy, panentheism, and sacrifice as the lecture uses them, and say which myth illustrates all three.
**Topic:** Defining religion, myth and ritual  **Lec:** 1  **Type:** recall
**A:** Liturgy is the reading of a religious text as a ritual. Panentheism is the worldview that God is greater than the universe and includes and interpenetrates it. Sacrifice is a basic religious ritual that grew as a metaphorical extension of tribal gift-exchange economies, gifts to supernatural agents to propitiate them and gain blessings. The Rigveda Purusha creation myth illustrates all three: the primordial being disintegrates into the universe and the gods and humans keep sacrificing him.

### Q: What is a paracosm, whose term is it, and how does religious practice produce one?
**Topic:** Aesthetic experience and the faith frame  **Lec:** 1  **Type:** recall
**A:** A paracosm is the everyday world transformed so that the supernatural comes to life in it. The term is Luhrmann's (2020). Deliberate religious practice produces it by ritualising every aspect of life as a reminder of the connection with the supernatural. The paracosm in turn produces the faith frame through narratives, rituals and religious objects.

### Q: Two drivers are stuck in traffic at sunset. One curses the jam and drives aggressively; the other admires the colours, pulls into the slow lane and plans to frame a photo. Which is having an aesthetic experience, and trace the chain from that experience to behaviour using the lecture's terms.
**Topic:** Aesthetic experience and the faith frame  **Lec:** 1  **Type:** apply
**A:** The second driver. An aesthetic experience is putting a mental frame over an object and observing it for an extended time. That experience leads to value formation, and values (Schwartz 1994: preferred ways of acting or being that serve as guiding principles) drive behaviour. The second driver's peace of mind produces calm driving; the first is hijacked by emotion. A hypothetical third driver who reads the jam as God's punishment is having an aesthetic experience through a faith frame.

### Q: What is axiology, and what does "axiological aesthetics" claim about religious experience?
**Topic:** Aesthetic experience and the faith frame  **Lec:** 1  **Type:** recall
**A:** Axiology is value theory. Axiological aesthetics studies how values are constructed as a result of experiencing things. Its claim is that religious experience is a form of aesthetic experience in this sense, and that it is not limited to art: anything in the world can be the object of the aesthetic gaze.

### Q: What makes a religion a "world religion" by the lecture's definition, and give the lecture's four figures on Buddhism's size and distribution.
**Topic:** Buddhism as a world religion and the early schools (arhat vs bodhisattva)  **Lec:** 1  **Type:** recall
**A:** A world religion has a diasporic presence and travels across state borders. Buddhism has about 500 to 535 million followers, over 7% of the world's population, over 99% of them in the Asia-Pacific region, and it is a minority religion today in its homeland of Nepal and India.

### Q: Contrast the conservative and progressive schools of Buddhism as they stood by the first century CE on: the goal of the follower, the number of Buddhas per universe, what a Buddha creates, and which one each became.
**Topic:** Buddhism as a world religion and the early schools (arhat vs bodhisattva)  **Lec:** 1  **Type:** recall
**A:** Conservative: the disciple aims at nirvana, liberation from the cycle of births and deaths, and becomes an arhat; one universe has only one Buddha at a time; nothing is created; this became modern Theravada. Progressive: the follower declares as a bodhisattva, a future Buddha, to lead others to the same realisation; there are multiple universes, so bodhisattvas can become Buddhas quickly elsewhere; a Buddha creates his own Buddha Land where bodhisattvas are reborn to train; this became most of the Buddhist world.

### Q: What is an arhat, and what are the three poisons an arhat is free from? Say which poison the lecture glosses as the illusion of one's own self.
**Topic:** Buddhism as a world religion and the early schools (arhat vs bodhisattva)  **Lec:** 1  **Type:** recall
**A:** An arhat is a liberated follower of the Buddha who does not get reborn. The three poisons are anger, passion and ignorance. Ignorance is glossed as the illusion of one's own self.
