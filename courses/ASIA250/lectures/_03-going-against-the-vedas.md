## What the deck claims

### The Brahmanical order the shramanas rejected

- The Rig Veda hymn that names the four castes is late in the collection, because the earliest
  hymns describe a polytheistic, nomadic society instead.
- The four castes are brahmins (priests and teachers), kshatriyas (nobles and warriors), vaishyas
  (merchants) and shudras (labourers and artisans).
- The brahmanical priesthood solidified as a hereditary male lineage of ritual specialists who
  intermarried among themselves and then encoded the four castes into the Vedas.
- Each person is supposed to stay in their caste and not switch their way of life, which is what
  the Sanskrit word **dharma** ("duty") means here.
- Each caste has its own duty: brahmins perform rituals and teach, kings and nobles keep order and
  fight intruders, merchants make profit while paying taxes and giving donations, and artisans,
  labourers and peasants follow their vocations while also paying taxes and donating.

### The Bhagavadgita as the brahmanical answer

- The Bhagavadgita is a chapter of the Mahabharata, an epic tale from the 3rd century BCE.
- Arjuna hesitates to massacre his teachers, friends and relatives on the enemy side, and wants to
  withdraw from the world and become a wandering ascetic instead of accruing the bad karma of war.
- Krishna tells him he must fight because he is a kshatriya and fighting is his duty.
- Krishna's second argument is that the souls of all living beings (**atman**) are indestructible,
  so Arjuna will not really be killing anybody.
- The deck calls this the brahmanical view encoded into the epic.
- Arjuna later repents and dies of exhaustion on a pilgrimage to the Himalayas. Krishna is shot in
  the heel by a hunter who mistakes his foot for a deer, blesses the hunter, and ascends to
  Vaikuntha heaven; other scriptures send him to Goloka Vrindavan, the divine realm of cows, where
  Mirabai and his other devotees hope to go.
- The deck's reading of the ending is that there is no escape from bad karma, but that recompense
  for good karma by following one's duty awaits in the afterlife. It calls this cosmic justice.

### The two kshatriyas who answered differently

- Siddhartha Gautama (ca. 500 BCE, founder of Buddhism) and Vardhamana Mahavira (his older
  contemporary, founder of Jainism) were both born warriors and royal princes.
- Siddhartha was of the Shakya clan and renounced the world at 29; Mahavira was of the Naya clan
  and renounced at 30.
- Their religious views differed, but both held that killing produces so much bad karma that it is
  better to renounce the world and become an ascetic (**shramana**).
- Siddhartha left the palace with his charioteer Channa after encountering sights of suffering,
  cut his hair, took off his princely garb and became a wandering ascetic.

### The shramana movement and the ascetic norms

- The shramana movement begins around the 6th century BCE, but the Vedas already show people
  seeking to renounce the world to escape suffering and pursue spiritual goals.
- Rig Veda hymn 10.136.1–2 describes the long-haired **muni** girdled with wind, wearing
  soil-coloured garments and following the wind's course where the gods have gone.
- The modern **sadhus** ("practitioners of austerities") of South Asia are the descendants of the
  Vedic munis.
- Cutting one's hair symbolised cutting ties with the world, and yellow patched robes symbolised
  worn-out fabric losing its colour in the sun. Mahavira went completely naked.

### World-affirming and world-renouncing in Hinduism

- The three modes of human life (**purusha-artha**) are dharma (piety, society, duty), artha
  (profit, success, domination) and kama (pleasure, sex, desire), pictured as a tripod in which no
  goal can be neglected.
- The deck asks whether the order dharma → artha → kama is a ranking by importance or a set of
  steps toward a better goal.
- The four stages of life (**ashramas**) are brahmacarya (youth, celibacy, learning dharma),
  grihastha (adulthood, householder, marriage and family), vanaprastha (middle age, retirement,
  "way to the forest") and sannyasa (old age, forest dwelling, renunciation and moksha).
- The first three are world-affirming; only sannyasa is world-renouncing.
- Both founders answered that renunciation is best begun early, because human life is a precious
  opportunity to escape samsara and the path takes effort that old age makes difficult. Siddhartha
  practised austerities for 6 years before enlightenment; Mahavira for 12 before omniscience.

### Why Hindus call Buddhism and Jainism heterodox

- Neither accepts the authority of the Vedas.
- Both hold that the Vedic gods are not beyond suffering: they live long and happy lives but still
  die, and may then be reborn in lower worlds once their good karma is exhausted, so divine rebirth
  does not solve suffering.
- Neither has a creator god. The universe is eternal but cyclical, each cycle beginning from
  previous karma, with beings reborn and dying until they liberate themselves.
- Both are against caste. Siddhartha said it is better to be of any caste than a brahmin, because
  brahmins have distorted views, and that anyone of proper spiritual conduct is a "brahmin" — so it
  is not a caste. Mahavira is said to have been miraculously transferred as a fetus from a brahmin
  woman's womb into a kshatriya woman's.
- Mahavira becomes a **Tirthankara** ("Ford Maker", the ford being protection from the world of
  suffering); Siddhartha becomes the **Buddha** ("Awakened One") and attains nirvana. Both are
  called **Jina**, the Conqueror of suffering.
- Both project their knowledge into the past: Mahavira names 23 earlier tirthankaras, the Buddha 27
  earlier Buddhas. The deck says this move legitimised each tradition by making its teachings older
  than the Vedas, and that there is zero archaeological evidence for the claim.
- Both started renunciant communities.

### Where Jainism and Buddhism differ

- Jains believe in a self (**jiva**) similar to the Vedic atman; the Jain permanent soul is
  enmeshed in the substance of karma, as purusha is in prakriti in Samkhya.
- Jain cosmology puts the liberated world on the top tier of the cosmos, above the Vedic gods,
  while Buddhist nirvana is a state completely outside the world.
- Jainism takes asceticism to the extreme with body-mortifying practices: giving up possessions,
  going naked in some sects, and abstaining from sex, sleep and eventually food to the point of
  death (**sallekhana** or **santhara**), on the premise that complete non-violence toward the
  world liberates one from karma.

### Asceticism as a category (Schmalz 2007)

- The word comes from the Greek **askesis**, "to exercise, train".
- Schmalz finds asceticism in all religions to some extent, with examples from Christianity,
  Judaism, Islam, Hinduism and Buddhism.
- It involves controlling, and ideally eliminating, self-indulgence, desire and sensual pleasure.
  Celibacy is a subtype.
- It is negative because it involves denial — avoidance, mortification, self-violence, cutting or
  suppressing something in the self.
- It is positive because it produces something — virtue, purity, consciousness, an intangible
  power. The Sanskrit term is **tapas**, "inner heat", a spiritual power produced by ascetic
  practice.
- What it does: it consecrates (makes one pure and dedicated to a purpose), separates (makes one
  different from ordinary people), transforms or atones (changes one's nature for spiritual gain),
  and in combination with other methods produces altered states of consciousness.

### Why the Buddha's life is only traditional lore

- There are no external sources for the life of the Buddha: writing was not invented in South Asia
  until around 300 BCE, no rival tradition mentions Siddhartha Gautama, and no archaeological
  discovery proves his historicity.
- Everything known comes from the oral Buddhist tradition, written down several centuries after his
  death, so what survives is the traditional lore of the Buddha's "lives".
- The deck's conclusion is that Buddhist teachings are collective memory that changes with time and
  gains depth as the tradition develops.

### Buddhism as the religion of numbers

- The early community used numbers as a mnemonic device to keep important teachings in memory,
  transmitted them orally, and later wrote those numbered sets into the scriptures.
- The tradition names 27 Buddhas before Gautama, who is number 28; the next will be **Maitreya**
  (Pali Metteyya), number 29, expected many thousands of years from now, with some sources saying
  5.67 billion years. Maitreya is now a god in Tushita Heaven.
- Gautama's journey began not in his 5th-century-BCE life in Lumbini but four asamkhyeyas and one
  hundred thousand kalpas ago. One asamkhyeya is 10^140; one kalpa is the time from the creation of
  one universe to the next.
- At that time he was a brahmin ascetic named **Sumedha**, who heard the teaching of Buddha number
  4, **Dipankara**, and aspired to become a Buddha himself. Someone with that aspiration is called
  a **bodhisattva**. Sumedha is then reborn countless times, giving his life and resources to
  others.

### The ten perfections and the Jataka tales

- The tradition singles out ten of the bodhisattva's previous lifetimes to show how the man born as
  Siddhartha came to possess the ten virtues.
- The ten are generosity, morality, renunciation, wisdom, energy, patience, truthfulness,
  resolution, loving-kindness and equanimity.
- The stories of the past lives are the **Jataka** tales. The earliest complete Pali canon has 547;
  the most important ten are the **dasajati jataka**, "The Stories of Ten Rebirths", often painted
  on temple murals.
- **Vessantara Jataka** (generosity): the bodhisattva is born as Prince Vessantara and gives away
  all his possessions, including his wife and children, to help living beings. This is the reading
  in the four YouTube parts.
- **Prince Bhuridatta** (morality): the bodhisattva is a naga captured by a snake charmer and
  forced to perform; his family finds him, he greets his brother and sister with affection, and the
  charmer releases him. The deck reads it as obedience and family values.
- **Prince Temiya** (renunciation): the prince pretends to be deaf and mute to avoid becoming king;
  a charioteer is ordered to kill him, the prince lifts the chariot in a show of strength, the
  frightened charioteer lets him go, and he becomes an ascetic in the forest.

### The twelve great deeds

- Having accumulated his virtues, the bodhisattva is born in Tushita heaven as a god named
  Shvetaketu ("White Banner"), then willingly descends to become Siddhartha, Buddha number 28.
- Every one of the 29 Buddhas accomplishes the same **12 great deeds**: descent from Tushita
  Heaven, entering his mother's womb, miraculous birth in the garden, training in the arts and
  sciences, victory in sports competition, enjoying the palace and marriage, renunciation, six
  years of ascetic practice and relinquishing it, victory over Mara, enlightenment under the Bodhi
  Tree, turning the Wheel of Dharma, and passing into parinirvana.
- Deed 1: he remembers his vow made before Dipankara, abandons heavenly pleasures willingly and
  chooses rebirth in the human world.
- Deed 2: Queen Maya dreams a white elephant entered her side and conceives miraculously, not by
  intercourse. Maya means divine illusion, and the deck reads the bodhisattva as entering a human
  realm of deeper illusion than the divine world.
- Deed 3: Maya gives birth standing and holding a tree branch; Siddhartha comes out of her side
  rather than the birth canal, signifying freedom from passion. He takes seven steps at once and
  says "In the heavens above and the earth below only I am the holy one", which the deck reads as
  his supremacy over the Vedic gods, humans and the rest of the cosmos. **Vesak**, his birthday,
  falls on the full moon of the fourth lunar month, usually around May, and involves the ritual
  bathing of the baby Buddha.
- Deed 4: he grows up in the palace of the King of the Shakya clan. His birth prophecy says he will
  become either a great monarch or a great renunciant saint. He learns the kshatriya arts — archery,
  wrestling, statecraft, poetry, music, mathematics — and excels at all of them.
- Deed 5: he wins the sports competitions to ask for Princess Yashodhara's hand, which the deck
  says underscores his virility and prowess.
- Deed 6: he marries and leads a palace life full of pleasure. His father, King Shuddhodana ("Pure
  Generosity"), fears he will renounce the world and so surrounds him with pleasures, banning any
  sign of suffering from the palace.
- The deck closes on the title of the primary source: **Lalitavistara**, where lalita means play or
  divine sport and vistara means extensive or full.
