## What the deck claims

### Deeds 7 and 8: renunciation and ascetic practice
- Every Buddha is said to accomplish the same 12 Great Deeds. This lecture picks up at deed 7.
- The Lalitavistara's title means "the extensive play": lalita is play or divine sport, and vistara is extensive.
- In deed 7, Siddhartha and his charioteer Channa leave the palace after he sees suffering. In the forest he cuts his hair, removes his princely clothes and becomes a wandering ascetic, a shramana.
- The Bhagavadgita shows the brahmanical counter-view. Krishna tells the warrior Arjuna to fight because it is his caste duty, his dharma, and because the atman cannot be destroyed.
- The four stages of life (ashramas) are brahmacarya (youth and study), grihastha (householder), vanaprastha (retiring to the forest), and sannyasa (renunciation). The first two affirm the world, and the last renounces it.
- Siddhartha leaves his wife and his newborn son, whom he names Rahula, meaning "stumbling block".
- In deed 8 he studies under two teachers. Under Alara Kalama he masters the four meditative absorptions (dhyanas or jhanas).
- The fourth absorption gives a fully equanimous mind, but it is not liberation. It causes rebirth in the world of form, where gods called Brahma live.
- Keep Brahma (a god), Brahmin (a priest) and Brahman (the absolute) apart.
- Under Udraka Ramaputra he learns subtler states that lead into the formless realm. Together these are eight stages of absorption.
- The eight stages resemble samadhi in Classical Yoga. Patanjali's Yoga Sutras (about the 2nd century BCE) define yoga as the cessation of the fluctuations of the mind, which lets the quiet mind reflect the atman.
- Siddhartha then spends six years in strict asceticism and nearly starves.
- Two stories explain why he turns to the middle way: the lute string that snaps when too tight and makes no sound when too loose, and muddy water that clears only when left to settle.

### Cosmology and the victory over Mara (deed 9)
- Buddhist cosmology can be mapped onto the meditator's body, with Mount Meru as the backbone.
- The realms rise from the desire realms ("pleasure") to the form realms ("clarity") to the formless realms ("space"). Meditation was like a shamanic journey through them.
- Mara, also called Kamadeva, is the god of passion and sexual desire. His name means "death", and he rules the desire realm from its highest heaven.
- Mara's daughters personify the passions, and his army personifies anger.
- The Buddhist story reworks the Hindu myth in which Shiva burns Kama with his third eye. Siddhartha, by contrast, conquers Mara without killing him.
- Bertolucci's film Little Buddha (1993) shows the battle as a story told to children within the film.

### Awakening (deed 10)
- He sits under the sacred fig (the Bodhi tree) and awakens at dawn.
- First, he remembers all his previous lives, which yields the teaching of the 29 Buddhas.
- Second, after stopping his mind in the eighth absorption, he realises that there is no atman.
- What exists instead is physical matter made of five elements (earth, water, fire, air and space) and conscious matter made of five skandhas (form, sensation, perception, volition and cognition).
- The skandhas are impermanent and pass from body to body like fire jumping from branch to branch. On this view, Hinduism mistakes the fire's changes for a permanent substance.
- Third, he realises that only nirvana, literally "blowing out", is final peace. Final nirvana comes when the last body dies.
- The Dhammapada (153–154) verse addresses the "house-builder", craving, as seen and defeated.
- The three marks (lakshanas) in this deck are anatman (no soul), anitya (impermanence) and nirvana. Nirvana is the end of dukkha. Note that the standard list gives dukkha as the third mark, not nirvana.
- He stays under the tree for 49 days, the same number of days the mind of the dead lingers before rebirth. He is 35 and is now the Buddha, "the awakened one".

### Teaching and death (deeds 11 and 12)
- "Turning the wheel" (the dharmachakra) is the metaphor for teaching.
- Dharma now means the truth spoken by the Buddha, not caste duty. In the plural, dharmas are the constituents of existence, like atoms.
- He teaches for 45 years, to people of every caste. His followers form four groups: monks (bhikshus), nuns (bhikshunis), laymen and laywomen.
- Buddhists had no writing for over 200 years. The Pali Canon is in Pali, a dialect of western India from about the 3rd century BCE.
- The first Pali inscriptions date to about 25 BCE, more than 400 years after the Buddha. The full canon took its current form in the 5th century CE.
- He dies after eating spoiled pork given as an offering. Buddhists hold that he chose to die in order to teach impermanence.
- His cremated bone fragments became relics (sharira), which were enshrined in stupas.
