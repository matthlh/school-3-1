## What the deck covers

### Hook: Hyon Gak
- Hyon Gak was born Paul Muenzen in New Jersey in 1964, converted from Catholicism while a graduate student at Harvard, spent over a decade as a monk in Korean temples, and now teaches meditation in Germany.
- He belongs to Korean Seon, the Korean form of Zen.
- His argument runs: religion is believing in something outside oneself such as a God; Buddhism has no creator God; therefore Buddhism is not a religion.
- The lecture rejects his premise, not his facts.

### Defining "religion"
- Abstract concepts resist definition. The thought experiment is to define "love": every definition falls short of the experience and has exceptions. Poetic language such as "love is a rose" captures it better, which is why myths have power over us.
- A first attempt, narratives about the supernatural plus rituals interacting with it, fails because it cannot be told apart from "worldview" or "cultural knowledge".
- The evolutionary framing: humans became self-aware and aware of mortality, suffering, evil and injustice, which is the human predicament. They answered it by conceptualising the supernatural in three forms.
  - Supernatural agents such as gods, spirits and souls, which often persist beyond death.
  - Supernatural states such as nirvana, heaven, hell and the afterlife, experienced after death or transcending suffering.
  - Cosmic justice such as karma, redemption and damnation, often enforced by the agents.
- The course definition: religion is a set of articulated and reenacted solutions (narratives and rituals) to the human predicament, premised on the ideas of and interaction with the supernatural, adhered to by a community in some institutionalised form.
- Applied to Hyon Gak's own teaching: the Buddha mind that is never born and never dies is a supernatural agent, nirvana as liberation from illusion is a supernatural state, and attachment leading to suffering is cosmic justice. So Buddhism fits the definition.
- Religious practice is the sustained effort to base behaviour on the ideas and personal experiences of the supernatural, by internalising the narratives and by ritualising daily life according to what the narratives prescribe.

### Narrative and myth
- Story and narrative are synonyms: a meaningful, interrelated sequence of events. Narrative-making is selective, so every narrative reinforces and recommends particular values.
- Religious narratives include the material culture that supports them, such as books, art and objects, which act as memory aids and make the intangible tangible.
- Myth is defined for this course as a form of allegory: a story, poem or picture that must be interpreted to reveal a hidden meaning, usually moral or political.
- Myths express the WHY, meaning origins, place in the world and identity, "a story a people tell themselves about themselves". They also express WHAT IS TRUE, meaning truth claims in symbolic form. They are not historical or scientific descriptions.
- Mythology is the study of myth or a group's collected myths.
- Two orientations in interpreting myth. The first disregards content and finds the meaning elsewhere, which tends to be reductive. The second takes content seriously and works to put the bizarre elements in context.
- Powell (Classical Myth, 1995) is quoted on Greek intellectuals being disturbed by the irrational and immoral content of their tales. Interpretation resolves that dissonance.
- The Buddha's birth story has three supernatural elements and an allegorical reading of each.
  - He emerges from his mother's side: freedom from attachment to the world.
  - He walks seven steps at birth: the future Buddha is a god born as a human.
  - He declares "in the worlds above or below, I alone am the holy one": a retrospective claim that the Buddha is superior to all Hindu gods and stands outside the illusory world, telling the Indian listener that Buddhism beats the other religions of India.
- The Rigveda creation myth: the primordial being Purusha disintegrates into the universe, including the gods and humans who then keep sacrificing him to maintain cosmic order. Why he sacrifices himself and why his offspring keep sacrificing him is deferred to lecture 2.
- The reading of a religious text is itself a ritual called liturgy. In the Buddha's lifetime, sacrifices were accompanied by liturgical recital and modelled on a feast where guests from another tribe are fed and reciprocate with allegiance.
- The Purusha myth is an example of panentheism: God is greater than the universe and includes and interpenetrates it.
- The Vedic sacrificial system of ritual, repeated recitation and artifact-making maintains the faith frame, Luhrmann's 2020 term.
- Sacrifice is a basic religious ritual that grew as a metaphorical extension of tribal gift-exchange economies: gifts to supernatural agents to propitiate them and win blessings.

### Ritual
- Ritual is "a complex of behavior and language whose performance brings about an effect, transforming the ritual participants or their environment". There is a before, a during and an after, like a journey.
- Rituals follow a script, have a purpose, and work within a cosmology.
  - The script is pronouncements acted out "as if really happening", bringing a story to life.
  - The purpose is the narrative destination: separation from the past and acceptance of a new future.
  - The cosmology is the worldview in which every participant finds a place.
- Ritual studies is a field in anthropology and religious studies. There is no agreement on what counts. Examples offered: oaths, ceremonies for the deceased, rites of passage, greetings and etiquette, music, social norms, hospitality.
- The speaker notes add that a rite of passage moves a person from one state to another, and that marriage is mapped onto union with the divine.
- The purpose of deliberate religious practice is to ritualise every aspect of life as a reminder of the supernatural. This turns the everyday world into a paracosm, Luhrmann's term, in which the supernatural comes to life.

### World religions and Buddhism's numbers
- A world religion is one with a diasporic presence that travels across state borders.
- Around 500 to 535 million people identify as Buddhists, over 7% of the world. Over 99% live in the Asia-Pacific region. In the homeland, Nepal and India, Buddhism is a minority religion today.

### Buddhism experientially: axiological aesthetics
- Religious experience is a form of aesthetic experience in the axiological sense. Axiology is value theory. Axiological aesthetics studies how values are constructed by experiencing things.
- An aesthetic experience is putting a mental frame over something and sustaining attention on it for an extended time. Art is the obvious case, but anything can become the object of the aesthetic gaze.
- The two-drivers example: Driver A curses the traffic and gets hijacked by emotion; Driver B admires the sunset, pulls into the slow lane and plans to frame a photo; a hypothetical Driver C reads the jam as God's punishment. B is having the aesthetic experience.
- The chain is aesthetic experience, then value formation, then behaviour. Values, after Schwartz (1994), are preferred ways of acting or being that serve as guiding principles for a person or group.
- Religious art is only part of the story; axiological aesthetics looks at all objects and experiences.
- The faith frame is produced by a paracosm built from narratives, rituals and religious objects, all attempts to detect the supernatural in the natural world.
- Jones (1979) lists facets of aesthetic experience: formal (colour, texture, shape), primeval or archetypal (hero, lover, outlaw), affective (emotion), sentimental ("that's cute"), extrinsic ("this would look great in my bedroom"), iconic (a symbol), cognitive ("this reminds me of X") and sterile or technical ("this technique was also used by X").
- The same facets seen through a faith frame become supernatural agents and states, embodiment in a monk or shaman, suffering and ecstasy, group bonding, asking a deity for something, a symbolic gesture, fitting a philosophical concept, and knowing what religion Y does on such a day.
- The meme: a Buddhist nun sees Jesus in her latte and says she is surprised it is not the Buddha.

### Early schools by the first century CE
- Conservative schools, surviving as modern Theravada: the disciple aims at nirvana, liberation from the cycle of births and deaths. An arhat is a liberated follower of the Buddha who is not reborn and is free of the three poisons, anger, passion and ignorance, where ignorance is the illusion of one's own self. One universe has only one Buddha at a time.
- Progressive schools, now most of the Buddhist world: the follower declares as a bodhisattva, a future Buddha, in order to lead others to the same realisation. A Buddha is free of rebirth and the three poisons and also creates a Buddha Land where bodhisattvas can be reborn to train. There are multiple universes, so bodhisattvas can become Buddhas quickly in other unenlightened universes.
