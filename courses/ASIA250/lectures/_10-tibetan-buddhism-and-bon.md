# ASIA 250 — Lecture 10: Tibetan Buddhism, the assimilation of Bon and the domestication of Tantra

Staged from the posted deck (55 slides) on 2026-11-27, before the recording was watched. Mini-Quiz 10 locks Mon Nov 30.

## Three keys to Tibetan Buddhism (slide 2)
- The deck unlocks Tibetan Buddhism with three concepts: Buddhism's interaction with the local religion of Tibet (Bon), Buddhist monastic scholasticism, and the domestication of Tantra.

## Bon, the local religion (slides 3 to 21)
- Buddhism reached Tibet about the 7th century CE and met **Bon**, a religion with many shamanic elements: shamanic journeys, contact with spirits through drumming, possession and control of spirits, white and black magic.
- Bon's worldview divides the cosmos into upper, lower and human worlds that are similar and contiguous.
- The name comes from the verb *bon pa*, "to chant incantations", and now means the oral and written teachings of the tradition. The Cham dance is an offering to the gods, like Japanese kagura.
- Tibet had an oral culture before Buddhism, and its script comes from Sanskrit alphabets, so Bon texts use Buddhist terminology and absorbed Buddhist concepts. Original Bon is hard to separate from Buddhism.
- A Bon lama looks like a Buddhist lama apart from blue patches on the robe. The Nechung Oracle, a shaman the Dalai Lamas consult, found a place inside Buddhism.
- What stays distinct is Bon's narratives: an **asexual corporal cosmogony** in which the formless creator god **Trigyal Khugpa** condenses five primordial lights (space blue, air green, water white, fire red, earth yellow) into a luminous cosmic egg; a black egg of chaos produces the demons; humans and animals arise from the friction between the two. Possible Zoroastrian and Chinese influences.
- The first Tibetan kings came from the light and returned to it.
- The **rainbow body**: advanced Bon (and some Buddhist) practitioners dissolve the body into the five elements at death, leaving only hair and nails. Always third-person accounts. The Bon lama **Shardza Tashi Gyaltsen** (1859 to 1933 or 1935) is believed to have achieved it. **Dzogchen** (great perfection) is the cycle of teachings for it, found in Bon and in Buddhism; lecture 11 covers it.
- Bon has **nine ways** (cycles of teaching), some borrowed from Buddhism and some exported into it.
- **Tapihritsa** (7th to 8th century) is the archetypal Bon practitioner: initiated into an unbroken lineage from the world of light, subdued evil spirits and converted them to Bon, then dissolved into light.
- **Shenrab Miwo** is the legendary founder of Bon in the human world, said to have lived 18,000 years ago and to come from Tazik (perhaps Tajikistan). His life retells the Buddha's (royal birth, renunciation), so he is in effect a Bon Buddha, and Bon is sometimes called the fifth school of Tibetan Buddhism.
- **Chod** combines the nine ways: the practitioner visualizes springing out of the body as a deity (khandroma) who chops the body up and offers it to gods and demons to clear debts and grudges, with song, drum, bell and sometimes a thighbone flute, then dissolves the divine body into light in Dzogchen meditation.
- Buddhists have Chod too. The nun **Machig Labdron** (1055 to 1149) developed it in a Buddhist setting; she is depicted as a three-eyed naked dakini with bell (emptiness) and damaru drum. Chod blends Buddhist tantric transformation with the Bon offering of the body to spirits.
- Chod practitioners live semi-nomadically on cemetery grounds, like Indian tantrikas, but their focus is Tibetan and shamanic: pacifying spirits and paying karmic debts.
- Summary: Buddhism borrowed the spirit world and the pacification of spirits from Bon; Bon borrowed exoteric and esoteric Buddhism wholesale; the differences left are the shamanic worldview and the creation cosmology; Chod and Dzogchen are fully blended.

## Monastic scholasticism (slides 22 to 39)
- **Kalu Rinpoche** (1905 to 1989) met the Korean Zen master **Seungsahn** (1927 to 2004), who came with an entourage wanting a dharma battle. Seungsahn raised an orange and asked "What is this?" three times. Kalu Rinpoche finally asked his translator whether they have no oranges in Korea. Seungsahn bowed in concession and left, and the audience laughed.
- The deck's point: a Zen monk would have eaten and shared the orange to show kyogai (direct experience of emptiness, compassion without boundaries), but Tibetan dharma argument goes straight to the concepts of emptiness and compassion, with no Daoist distrust of words.
- The **Samye debate** (792 to 794): the Indian monk **Shantarakshita** (725 to 788) debated the Chinese Zen monk **Moheyan** (8th century, identity not firmly established) before King **Trisong Detsen** (742 to 804), who wanted to know which Buddhism to import as a diplomatic tool.
- Moheyan summarized his teaching with a koan, "all you need to do is forget about thinking". Shantarakshita took it literally: then the fish in the pond would already be enlightened. Zen officially lost, and 8th-century Indian Buddhism (developed Mahayana philosophy plus Vajrayana tantra) was imported.
- Tibetan monks debate with ritual gestures and hand claps. The curriculum is gradual: Abhidharma (**Sarvastivada**), its refutation (**Sautrantika**), then **Madhyamaka** and **Yogacara** (Vijnanavada), then Tantra.
- **Sarvastivada** ("everything exists"): dharmas exist in past, present and future; their temporary combinations make the perceived world; knowing the dharmas (the Abhidharma lists) is how one reaches nirvana and arhatship. Analogy: atoms and chemistry.
- **Sautrantika** ("sutras are enough"): everything exists only momentarily; we have no access to the world, only inaccurate after-the-fact mental representations; no need to hunt for dharmas, just follow the Buddha's teaching on impermanence (momentariness). Analogy: quantum mechanics.
- **Madhyamaka** (the Middle Way), started by **Nagarjuna** (150 to 250 CE), who retrieved the emptiness sutras from the nagas: nothing is independent of its parts, causes, conditions and the classifying mind, so everything is empty of independent existence; a bodhisattva deconstructs subject and object in meditation and directly experiences emptiness.
- **Yogacara/Vijnanavada** (mastery of the yoga of consciousness), developed by **Asanga** and **Vasubandhu** in the 4th century CE: meditating on one's own consciousness is enough for liberation, discovering emptiness as the absence of subject and object duality.
- Yogacara's **three natures**: imagined (dualistic delusion), dependent (endless causes and conditions behind seeming objects, Madhyamaka's emptiness) and perfected (direct experience that all objects are the mind's magic show). Zen says it as mountains are mountains, then no longer mountains, then mountains again.
- Tibetan Buddhism domesticates Tantra to turn the three natures into a practical program: impure vision, pure vision and ineffable enlightened vision.
- **Impure vision**: the same river appears as fire and ice to hell beings, blood and pus to hungry ghosts, home to some animals, a river to humans, weaponizable energy to demigods and nectar to gods. Appearance depends on the karma-shaped mind.
- **Pure vision**: the cosmos as pure lands, the world as a mandala palace of jewels and light, oneself as the deity at the centre after initiation by the guru; every movement a mudra, every word a mantra, every appearance the deity's play of light.
- **Two stages of tantric practice**: the generation stage builds the deity and mandala visualization until it is spontaneous (samadhi); the completion stage dissolves them into light, and whatever the eyes then see has the quality of pure vision. The completion stage equals Yogacara's third nature. Sand mandalas built over days and swept away show the two stages to laypeople, who gain merit by watching.
- The monastic curriculum is gradualist and scholastic: memorize Asanga, Vasubandhu, Nagarjuna and the Vinaya first, then Tantra. Domestication means the openly transgressive methods that break the Vinaya are done only ritually, in visualization, not in fact (no actual sexual union or killing of demons), so Tantra becomes the tool that puts philosophy into practice.

## Padmasambhava, guru devotion and the tulku system (slides 40 to 55)
- Trisong Detsen also invited **Padmasambhava**, an 8th-century tantra master and miracle worker. Shantarakshita and Padmasambhava together stand for the pairing of monastic scholasticism with tantric practice.
- Padmasambhava subdued Tibet's demons and turned them into Buddhist guardian deities (Bon says he got only the unimportant ones). This is the other side of domestication: tantric ritual doing the shamanic tasks of Bon.
- He did it by becoming the wrathful **Guru Dragpo**, scaring the demons into submission. **Nyingma** (the old school), the earliest Tibetan Buddhist tradition, venerates him as founder, and the Guru Dragpo practice turns anger into awakening.
- Bhakti in exoteric Buddhism goes to cosmic bodhisattvas such as Avalokiteshvara; in esoteric Buddhism it goes to one's guru, the source of all the deities.
- **Kagyu** ("whispered transmission") was founded by **Marpa the Translator** (1012 to 1097), who studied under the Indian yogin **Naropa** (1016 to 1100). When the deity Hevajra appeared, Marpa bowed to it first; Naropa waved it away, Marpa bowed to Naropa, and only then received the Hevajra initiation. The guru outranks the deity.
- Guru devotion is double-edged: a genuine guru may act antinomian to open students' minds; an opportunist abuses students under that cover. Since Tibetan Buddhism reached the West in the 1960s there have been many scandals of sexual abuse and embezzlement.
- Guru devotion also produced the **tulku system**. Tulku translates Sanskrit **nirmanakaya**, the manifestation body of an enlightened being; tantric Buddhists hold that the historical Buddha was a nirmanakaya of the cosmic Buddha Vairochana.
- The idea borrows from Hinduism: a tulku is like an avatar of Vishnu, such as Krishna.
- Monasteries used it to legitimize their heads as reincarnated lamas, called **Rinpoche** ("treasure"): advanced bodhisattvas who reincarnate to teach. The Dalai Lamas are reincarnations of Avalokiteshvara and of each other, from the first to the current 14th.
- A bodhisattva can have several manifestations at once: there are two 17th Karmapas, once disputed, now accepting each other, after one 16th Karmapa (1924 to 1981).
- Only Tibet has tulkus because training a tantric master takes so long. Usually talented rich boys are recognized, adding to the monastery's wealth and power; they leave their families as children for training so strict it borders on child abuse, and they often know nothing of wider society.
- Kalu Rinpoche (1905 to 1989) decided to reincarnate; his reincarnation was born in 1990. Slide 52 is a video (the young Kalu Rinpoche's 2011 disclosure of sexual abuse in the monastery, which the quiz says he made while visiting UBC).
- Traditionally there are no female tulkus, though that is changing in the West. **Jetsunma Ahkon Lhamo** (born Alyce Zeoli, 1949) is a former New Age leader recognized as a tulku who sponsored a community of 70 Tibetan monks.
