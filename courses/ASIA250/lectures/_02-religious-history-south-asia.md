## What the deck covers

### Absorption and the faith frame (slides 2 to 4)
- The lecture reopens with the Jones facets seen through a faith frame, then adds a new axis: absorption, the volume of the religious experience.
- Luhrmann ch. 3 says some people are naturally good at absorption into the paracosm, and that the same absorption can be reached through religious training.

### Shamanism as proto-religion (slides 5 to 14)
- Mircea Eliade held that shamanism was the proto-religion of the first human societies. It developed techniques for altering consciousness to solve social problems: healing, death rituals, contact with spirits.
- The shaman's faith frame: beyond this world is a world of spirits. Spirits of the dead and gods can affect this world for good or ill. The shaman communicates with them, sacrifices to them and propitiates them to heal a person or a community.
- Shamans appear in hunter-gatherer and agricultural societies and inside religions such as Buddhism, Shinto and Bon. They are men, women and transgender persons. In Japan and Korea most practitioners are women. Eliade took Siberian groups to be the original shamans.
- Techniques: rhythmic song, dance, formulaic repetition in meditation, intoxicants, and ascetic acts such as fasting and sleep deprivation, all producing an altered state of consciousness or trance. Healing, individual and social, is the primary function.
- The shaman's story: one becomes a shaman through an initiatory ritual narrated as a journey to the spirit world, starting from a psycho-physical crisis, often illness or a near-death experience. In Korean shamanism this begins with a "divine sickness" (shin byeong) that can only be cured by apprenticing to a shaman and being initiated into the possessing spirit.
- Healing rituals either send the shaman to the spirit world or summon spirits here. In many forms the boundary is porous, which is the paracosm again. The basic myth of shamanism is the account of travel to and from the spirit world.
- Neuroscience aside: activation of temporal-lobe circuitry, as in epileptic seizures, can cause altered states that feel religious. V. S. Ramachandran calls this part of the brain "the God module". The experiences are ambiguous and need a structured narrative to give them meaning.
- Key concepts: ecstasy and enstasy. Both are altered states of consciousness (ASC) provoked by specific techniques. Transcendence is the sensation of leaving the physical body, sometimes with the body dismembered in the vision and rebuilt with new parts.
  - Ecstasy (Greek ex-stasis, "stand outside oneself") is travel directed outward: the border between self and world disappears and the person becomes the universe, a drop dissolving into the ocean. In shamanism it is travel to a spirit world away from the body.
  - Enstasy (en-stasis, "stand inside oneself") is directed inward: the universe breaks into the contours of the body, as if the infinite put on VR goggles to experience itself as subject and objects, seeing an ocean in one drop. In shamanic terms, the spirit possesses the shaman.
- Reference given: Winkelman 2005, "Shamanism: An Overview", Encyclopedia of Religion, 2nd ed.
- As societies grew complex, shamanism gave rise to religions; shamans became hereditary priests; rituals and narratives grew more complex; the ASC techniques remained. The question posed: can we trace South Asia back to these shamanic roots?

### The Vedic period and a brief introduction to Hinduism (slides 33 to 39)
- We cannot say much about IVC religion, so the lecture turns to the later Vedic or Brahmanical religion.
- The Vedic period runs roughly 1500 to 500 BCE, when the Vedas were composed. Early Vedic is about 1500 to 1000 BCE, Later Vedic about 1000 to 500 BCE. It ends with the decline of Vedic activity and the rise of classical Hinduism and new cities.
- "Hinduism" is an umbrella term for a group of religious traditions from South Asia, linked to modern yoga, meditation and sects such as ISKCON.
- Ultimate reality (dharma) is personified either as deities with personality and form (saguna) or as formless deities (nirguna). Some traditions see it as a formless principle beyond the world, and understanding it brings liberation (moksha). In traditions with personified deities, liberation comes through devotion to a deity.

### The Purusha hymn answered (slides 40 to 45)
- Same three slides as lecture 1 on the Purusha cosmology, liturgy and panentheism, then the answer that was deferred.
- Macdonell (1917) read the hymn as gods sacrificing Purusha, which the lecture calls a paradox he did not think through: the gods in the hymn are Purusha himself as he partitions into gods, people, animals and the inanimate world.
- Correction slide, to memorise: pantheism means the universe is God. Panentheism means the universe is a part of God's body but part of God extends beyond the universe. The Hymn of Purusha is panentheism.
- The hymn's account of the four castes shows it is a late hymn in the Rig Veda. Brahmins are priests and teachers, Kshatriyas nobles and warriors, Vaishyas merchants, Shudras labourers and artisans. The earliest hymns reflect a polytheistic, nomadic society.

### Vedic gods (slides 46 to 49)
- The Vedic cosmos is tripartite: the gods' Heaven, the spirits' Earth, and an intermediate space created when Indra separates them, where life occurs. The lecture says this is very similar to the shamanic systems.
- Agni is the fire god who sustains life and carries sacrifices to the gods. Vayu is the wind god animating the cosmos.
- Indra and Rudra are both personifications of the storm, modelled on nomadic warriors who come and go unexpectedly. Indra is benevolent, the good storm for agriculture. Rudra is belligerent, the destructive storm.
- In the post-Vedic period, around 200 BCE, Indra and Rudra combine with non-Aryan deity forms to become Shiva. Shaivas hold Shiva to be Purusha.
- Vishnu was a minor Vedic god, a friend of Indra who sets the sun in motion. He becomes very popular post-Vedic, and Vaishnavas hold him to be the true Purusha.

### The purpose of sacrifice and the meaning of karma (slides 50 to 51)
- In early polytheism and shamanism, sacrifice placates deities and spirits in a gift economy. In the early Rig Veda it seeks worldly gains and immortality after death, birth among the gods.
- Soma is both a god and the elixir of immortality, probably an early psychedelic plant. Priests sacrifice hoping to receive it as the gods' gift. Rig Veda 8.48.3 is quoted: "We have drunk the soma; we have become immortal."
- In the late Rig Veda the meaning shifts. The primordial God's self-sacrifice sets the universe in motion and produces living beings. Sacrifice maintains universal order; without it the world returns to chaos. This solidifies the position of hereditary Vedic priests, the brahmins.
- "Karma" originally meant the sacrificial actions that sustain the universe. By the time of the Upanishads (800 to 200 BCE) it broadened to all actions that produce effects, good and bad. Sacrifice becomes tied to generosity, which brings good karma.

### Rebirth, karma, and the character of Hindu deities (slides 54 to 64)
- Rebirth: individual beings are reborn again and again. Deities also participate, as when Vishnu is reborn as Rama and Lakshmi as his consort. The world itself cycles through creation, flourishing, decay and destruction; these are ages (yuga), and the world's suffering depends on where it sits in the cycle.
- Karma means action: of body, speech and mind, and also ritual, sacred speech and gesture. It affects future rebirths and the chances of liberation, "you reap what you sow". Proper ritual and action can reshape the world.
- Kama is the god of desire, embodying erotic pleasure including forbidden pleasures. Iconography: beautiful, in his prime, eyes half closed as if intoxicated, a sugarcane bow and five flower-tipped arrows, vehicle a parrot or sparrow. He is compared to Eros or Cupid and personifies the creative force of desire in the universe.
- The Hindu pantheon includes erotic beings; erotic desire has power, explained through karma, and the Creation Hymn has desire causing the world to appear. The ideal human life depends, for some traditions, on caste, station and gender; other traditions aim to escape samsara and reach moksha, treating desire as the core problem.
- Rati is Kama's consort and feminine aspect, with matching iconography. In the myth, Shiva, an ascetic after losing his wife Sati, is shot by Kama's arrow so he will fall for Parvati, Sati's reincarnation. Enraged, Shiva burns Kama to ash with his third eye. Read one way this is asceticism defeating eroticism; read the other way Kama wins, because Shiva does fall in love and marry Parvati. Rati petitions for Kama's rebirth; he returns formless but still powerful. A son of Shiva was needed to defeat a demon.
- The Trimurti: Brahma the creator, Vishnu the sustainer, Shiva the destroyer. The Tridevi: Sarasvati with Brahma, Lakshmi with Vishnu, Parvati with Shiva, each the consort or the female manifestation of the god. The pairs are joined sexually or are masculine and feminine sides of one principle.
- Deities have wrathful and peaceful aspects: Kali versus Parvati. A single goddess has many aspects, for example Parvati as Kali, Durga and Gauri; Kali connotes darkness, the end of time and death, with weapons, bloody tongue and a necklace of heads. Sometimes two forms, sometimes three, sometimes nine (the Nine Forms of Durga).
- The divine androgyne: Shiva as Ardhanarishvara, "the Lord who is half woman", split vertically with Shiva on the dominant right side and Parvati on the left.
- Shakti is divine energy, considered feminine, of the earth and the goddess. As Adishakti, primal energy, she stands over the three gods and her true nature is formless (nirguna). Shaktism places the divine feminine at the centre.

### Upanishads and the post-Vedic schools (slides 65 to 69)
- The Upanishads (800 to 200 BCE) posit a formless (nirguna) ground of being, Brahman, at the foundation of the universe. Brahman is the source of the individual soul (atman), the imperishable part of humans and animals that is reborn until liberation (moksha). Some Upanishads say atman is identical to Brahman (non-dualism), others keep a distinction (dualism). The Purusha question returns: are the shards the same as Purusha?
- Samkhya ("rational examination"): purusha names the individual spirit or soul that animates a living being. It interacts with inanimate matter, prakriti, which produces the world. Mind, ego, the six senses and intellect are all prakriti; only purusha, beyond life and death, makes prakriti seem alive. We suffer because we are unaware of our inner core, which is ignorance (avidya), and we deepen suffering through passion and anger, producing bad karma. The goal is to become aware of purusha by introspection, giving liberation at death. The allegory: purusha is the passenger in a chariot, the charioteer is intellect, ego and mind, the horses are the six senses, the chariot is the body. Samkhya's open problem: why does purusha get entangled in prakriti, and where does prakriti come from?
- Yoga ("yoke", taming body and mind) puts Samkhya into practice. Keeping the body still while concentrating on one object stops the mind's fluctuations and brings a deep trance (samadhi) in which purusha shows itself like the moon on still water. Gods (Ishvara) serve as perfect objects of concentration because they represent purushas that never got lost in prakriti. Yoga inherits Samkhya's problems.
- Vedanta ("conclusion of the Vedas") comes in two forms. Advaita (non-dual): there are no individual souls, only Brahman, the universal consciousness; individuals are like the one sun reflected in many mirrors, and separate selfhood is illusion. Its problem: why does Brahman become ignorant of itself? Dvaita (dual): God, for example Vishnu, is the eternal purusha or Brahman who created individual souls out of goodwill so they would seek union through devotion. The lecture says this is the dominant Hindu theology today and solves most of the other schools' problems.
- The desktop analogy: thoughts come and go but consciousness continues, like a desktop and its contents. Is there one desktop or many (atman versus Brahman)? Is it separate from the hardware (purusha versus prakriti)? Or are both produced by a benevolent intelligence (Vedas and Dvaita Vedanta)?

### Sects and Bhakti (slides 70 to 85)
- Hindu sects are often devoted to one major deity: Shaivism to Shiva, Vaishnavism to Vishnu and his incarnations, especially Krishna.
- Bhakti, "devotional love", is falling in love with God, with romantic love as the model for spiritual ecstasy. Devotionalism appears in Hinduism, Islam, Sikhism, Jainism, Buddhism and Christianity. As a South Asian path it has intellectual, textual, performative and communal dimensions. Optional reading: Pechilis 2007, "Bhakti".
- Krishna is a manifestation of Vishnu, an avatar, meaning an incarnation born as a human, while Vishnu resides in a heavenly realm.
- In the Mahabharata (3rd century BCE), in the Bhagavadgita chapter, Krishna tells Arjuna (BG 9.28) that dedicating all works to him frees the devotee from the bondage of karma and from samsara.
- Who is this God? The Vedas are polytheistic and hold that all souls come from the impersonal Brahman, allegorically Purusha. Buddhism keeps the same Vedic gods but declares them mortal and bound to samsara, denies a permanent soul (anatman), and leaves cosmogony unanswered. Classical Hinduism develops devotion to a transcendent God who personifies Brahman and is also immanent in avatars.
- The Bhakti movement (7th to 17th centuries CE) takes one manifestation as most important among the divinities, which is henotheism, like a lover choosing one beloved. It develops in opposition to over-ritualised Vedic Hinduism and later to Tantra.
- The Bhagavata Purana holds the most popular Krishna stories; its tenth book tells Vishnu's incarnation as Krishna. Purana means "that which took place previously". Here Krishna is the absolute and Vishnu is his partial manifestation, reversing the usual ranking of human and divine. Devotees long to join the play (lila) of his childhood in Braj.
- The preference for the human form parallels Buddhism: before the Buddha became human he was a celestial bodhisattva in a heaven, and he awakens in human form, which matters more than his divine past. The difference is that Krishna is a form of the one creator God, while the Buddha is only an awakened being. Hinduism also counts the Buddha as an avatar of Vishnu.
- Love of God mirrors human relationships: lover to beloved, parent to child, servant to master, friend to friend. The mother-to-child mode uses the butter-thief stories of Krishna's childhood in Vrindavan or Braj (Bryant, p. 112).
- Maya is divine illusion, sometimes personified as the goddess Yogamaya. Yashoda looks in Krishna's mouth for dirt and sees the whole universe, becomes enlightened and can no longer mother him, so Krishna covers her again with maya and she forgets (Bryant, p. 116). Krishna does not want to be God all the time.
- Two key terms. Lila (play) is an allegory for creation as an act of God's charity, with good and bad karma as educational tools leading creatures toward union with God. Maya (illusion) is God hiding from creatures while revealing himself through avatars. Together they are an educative game of hide-and-seek. Liberation is seeing through the veil, and in truth there is no separation and no samsara.
- The lover-to-beloved mode uses the gopis (cowherd girls) and Radha. Their love is romantic, characterised by surrender (prema), equal in union and unequal in separation.
- Sherma's distinction: kama is erotic love based on desire; prema is pure love based on surrender and sacrifice, which includes and transcends kama and is one way to love God. Devotional poets use union (ecstasy) and separation (pain and longing) to show the devotee as equal to God in union and unequal in separation. Optional reading: Sherma 2007, "Romantic Love in Hinduism".
- The rasa lila, "play of emotion" or "dance of divine love": one autumn evening Krishna plays his flute, the gopis abandon chores, homes, husbands and families, and each dances and makes love with Krishna for a night that lasts billions of years (Schweig, p. 449, in Bryant). Joining this night is the highest human experience.

### Puja and bhakti-yoga (slides 86 to 89)
- Puja, worship or honouring, is a ritual of devotion, and the lecture reuses the lecture 1 definition of ritual with script, purpose and cosmology. Its cosmology is the divine play, its purpose is to connect with Krishna and nurture bhakti.
- Offerings: sips of water, flowers, food, mantras, devotional songs, waving lighted camphor before the image, hospitality, bathing and dressing the image. Much simpler than Vedic ritual. Ritual cultivates a physical and emotional response.
- Domestic puja bathes and dresses the infant Krishna as a beloved friend present in the home. Temple puja is the same with more of everything, in a special room, performed by ritual specialists.
- The nine processes of bhakti-yoga (adapted from Bryant, p. 117): hearing about Krishna, singing about him, remembering him, serving him, worshipping him, offering obeisance, dedicating all one's actions to him, confiding in him as a friend, and offering one's body and belongings to his service. Hearing, singing and meditating on his lila head the list.

### Mirabai (slides 90 to 91), the subject of Optional Assignment 1
- Born around 1500 CE to a high-caste, wealthy ruling family, a princess with a childhood love of Krishna after receiving an image of him from a visiting Brahmin.
- Married against her will, she refused wifely, household and religious duties, refused to bow to her mother-in-law and to have sex with her husband, and instead joined the devotees at the local temple.
- Her family tried to confine, stop and punish her, then made several attempts to kill her, including poison. She began to wander, visiting the places of Krishna's biography, the Braj and the rasa lila.
