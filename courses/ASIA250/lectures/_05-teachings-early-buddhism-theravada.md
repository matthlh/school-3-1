## Slides, organized

### How the teachings were transmitted (slide 2)
- The content of the Buddha's teaching varies from school to school.
- For over 200 years Buddhists had no writing system.
- Several canons developed, first orally and then in writing.
- One early canon is the **Pali Canon**, named for **Pali**, an Indo-Aryan dialect of western India from about the 3rd century BCE.
- The first (legendary) Pali inscriptions date to about 25 BCE, over 400 years after the Buddha.
- The full canon was recorded in its current form in the 5th century CE, 1000 years after the Buddha.
- The other canons survive only in fragments and in Chinese and Tibetan translations.
- Comparing the canons gives the common elements of what the historical Buddha might have taught.

### Eight common characteristics of early Buddhism (slide 3)
- The deck lists eight features shared across the canons that point back to the origin of Buddhism:
  - the uniqueness and rarity of the founder, the **Buddha** ("The Awakened One"), for example the 29 Buddhas;
  - a Buddha perfects his virtues over countless lifetimes of selfless service to all living beings (the **Jatakas**);
  - the Buddhas come to the human world (the 12 Deeds) and also teach gods;
  - they teach the true doctrine, the **Dharma**, marked by 3 (or 4) **lakshanas**;
  - the **Middle Way** free from extremes (the tuning parable);
  - a cluster of ideas: causality (**karma**), rebirth (**samsara**), rejection of Brahmanical rituals and of the supremacy of gods;
  - the attainments of the Buddha's disciples: the **Arhats**, and the Bodhisattva **Maitreya** (Pali Metteyya);
  - the supramundane **Eightfold Path**.

### The 29 Buddhas (slide 4)
- The Buddha named his 27 predecessor Buddhas from time immemorial, so the story of the Buddhas is "beginningless".
- Gautama (Pali Gotama), the current Buddha, is number 28.
- Maitreya (Pali Metteyya), the future Buddha, is number 29.
- The numbered ones worth knowing: 1 Tanhankara, 4 **Dipamkara** (Dipankara), 14 Sumedha, 19 Siddhattha, 27 Kassapa, 28 Gautama, 29 Maitreya.
- The full list in order: Tanhankara, Medhankara, Sarananakara, Dipamkara, Kondanna, Mangala, Sumana, Revata, Sobhita, Anomadassi, Paduma, Narada, Padumuttara, Sumedha, Sujata, Piyadassi, Atthadassi, Dhammadassi, Siddhattha, Tissa, Phussa, Vipassi, Sikhi, Vessabhu, Kakusandha, Konagamana, Kassapa, Gautama, Maitreya.

### When the journey began: asamkhyeyas and kalpas (slide 5)
- Siddhartha Gautama began his journey toward enlightenment not in this life (about the 5th century BCE, a prince born in **Lumbini**) but four **asamkhyeyas** and one hundred thousand **kalpas** ago.
- One asamkhyeya is 10 to the power 140.
- One kalpa is the time from the creation of one universe until the creation of the next.
- In his life as a brahmin ascetic named **Sumedha** he vows, in front of Buddha number 4, **Dipankara**, to become a Buddha himself.

### The ten virtues and the Vessantara Jataka (slides 6 to 8)
- The **Bodhisattva** (the future Buddha) spends countless lifetimes perfecting his virtues.
- The tradition singles out 10 previous lifetimes to show how he came to possess the virtues.
- The 10 virtues needed to become a Buddha, in the deck's order:
  - 1 Generosity, giving help and benefit to other living beings;
  - 2 Morality, living an ethical life;
  - 3 Renunciation, giving up worldly pleasures;
  - 4 Wisdom, right understanding of life and the world;
  - 5 Energy, persistent effort that is not discouraged by failure;
  - 6 Patience, accepting life's ups and downs;
  - 7 Truthfulness, honesty in all things;
  - 8 Resolution, unwavering determination to progress on the path;
  - 9 Loving-kindness, benevolence and compassion to all things;
  - 10 Equanimity, perfect mental equilibrium.
- The **Vessantara Jataka** illustrates generosity: Prince Vessantara gives away all his possessions, including his wife and children, to help living beings.
- Having accumulated his virtues, the Bodhisattva is born in **Tushita** heaven as a god named **Shvetaketu** ("White Banner").
- He then willingly descends to the human world to become Siddhartha, Buddha number 28.

### Why the human world (slide 9)
- The human world is neutral: it has both suffering and bliss.
- The divine worlds have very little suffering and mostly pleasures.
- The lower worlds (animals, ghosts, hells) have too much suffering and too short a life span, so there is no possibility of following the teachings on liberation.

### Ghosts and Yama: the Vedic idea and the Buddhist twist (slides 10 to 12)
- Ghosts are **pretas** in Sanskrit and **petas** in Pali.
- In the Brahmanic Hinduism of the Buddha's time, people became ghosts when they died.
- A son conducted ritual sacrifices to feed the dead ghost so it could grow an immortal body and go to the ancestral land of **Yama**.
- In Vedic mythology Yama was the first living being who died.
- Buddhist cosmology echoes this but twists it on two fronts.
- First, there is a **Yama Heaven**, one of the pleasant worlds, where good people are born as Yama gods.
- Second, Yama himself becomes the Ruler of Hell.
- As ruler of hell Yama is a **vemanikapeta**, a type of ghost who experiences both heavenly pleasures and the torments of the hells.
- He judges the "baddies" and decides the type of torment for their punishment.
- Yama is the personification of death and the King of Hells.
- In Buddhism pretas live as ghosts not because they have no son to feed them (the Vedic reason) but because they had excessive greed.
- They are reborn with big bellies and tiny throats that cannot pass food, so they are "hungry" ghosts (the deck's comparison is Smeagol from The Lord of the Rings).

### Cosmology and the Buddha over the gods (slides 13 to 17)
- In the cosmology diagram the god **Indra** lives in a heaven of the desire realm, and Siddhartha's previous life was in Tushita.
- The Buddha's Dharma is the Truth.
- One of the Buddha's honorific titles is **Shasta Devamanushyanam**, Teacher of Gods and Humans.
- That title underscores the Buddhist claim that the Buddha is supreme over the Vedic gods.
- Almost all Vedic gods worship the Buddha, including Indra and the Brahmas.
- Kama, that is Mara, opposes the Buddha but is conquered.
- The Buddha's mother **Maya** ("divine illusion") died soon after Siddhartha came out of her side and was reborn in Indra's heaven.
- The Buddha visits her in heaven to teach her the 3 lakshanas.

### The three lakshanas (slide 18)
- The three marks of the Buddha's experience, the **three lakshanas** (marks of existence), as the deck lists them:
  - 1 **Anatman** (Pali anatta), no indestructible soul;
  - 2 **Anitya** (Pali anicca), impermanence of the self and the world;
  - 3 **Nirvana**, the direct experience of 1 and 2, the only final peace, salvation from suffering (**dukkha**) and the permanent resolution of the human predicament.
- Some traditions give dukkha as a separate, fourth lakshana.
- Dukkha is elaborated in the Buddha's teaching on the Four Noble Truths.
- Nirvana is the end of dukkha.

### Karma as causality and the six realms (slides 19 to 20)
- Causality is karma, an overarching theme in Buddhism.
- Everything that comes into existence does so due to causes and conditions, and once those no longer hold the thing ceases to exist.
- The deck's example: the cause is a seed falling into the ground; the conditions are soil, water and sunshine.
- Rebirth works the same way, each realm having its own cause:
  - hells, due to anger, aversion and killing;
  - hungry ghosts, due to avarice and hoarding;
  - animals, due to ignorance;
  - humans, due to sexual desire;
  - demigods (**Asuras**), due to jealousy;
  - gods, due to pride.
- Hells, hungry ghosts and animals are the 3 lower realms; humans, demigods and gods are the 3 higher realms.

### Anatman and the Asuras (slides 21 to 22)
- Anatman (Pali anatta) means there is no indestructible soul.
- For Buddhism the self is a combination of five conscious domains, like the moon reflected in water.
- Hinduism and Jainism hold instead that there is an **atman**, an indestructible soul or pure consciousness.
- The deck's image of an Asura is from Kofukuji in Japan, dated 734 CE.
- Asuras are highly intelligent but less capable than the gods, of whom they are jealous and with whom they often fight, and always lose.
- Asuras cannot get all the way up to Yama heaven, which is why that heaven is described as a peaceful domain, harking back to the Vedic idea of a peaceful afterlife with Yama and the ancestors.

### Feeding the hungry ghosts: Maudgalyayana and the Ullambana festival (slide 23)
- Buddhism borrows from Brahmanical Hinduism the idea that feeding the hungry ghosts, relieving their suffering, is a meritorious deed that secures good karma.
- This is the **Ullambana** festival, **O-bon** in Japan.
- A man named **Maudgalyayana** found out that his mother had been reborn as a hungry ghost.
- He sought the Buddha's advice; the Buddha explained that individual efforts were not enough and instructed that offerings be made to the assembled monastic community on the 15th day of the seventh lunar month.
- Maudgalyayana himself becomes the Buddha's disciple.
- The Buddha, his Dharma and his disciples, the **Sangha**, are of ultimate value in Buddhism and the source of the highest merit.
- Maudgalyayana's offerings to the Buddha and the monks served as a cause of a better rebirth for his mother.
- Brahmanical rituals are rejected in early Buddhism and reincorporated later.

### The ten epithets (slide 25)
- The Buddha had great charisma and sometimes converted people by his mere appearance.
- The tradition describes him as a very manly, charismatic figure possessing great inner peace.
- The **10 Epithets** underscore his character and his (supposed) achievements:
  - 1 **Tathagata**, Thus Come One;
  - 2 **Arhat**, worthy of worship;
  - 3 Samyak-sam-buddha, the one who has attained complete enlightenment;
  - 4 Vidya-carana-sampanna, perfect accordance of knowledge and conduct;
  - 5 Sugata, well-departed;
  - 6 Lokavid, knower of the world;
  - 7 Anuttara, unexcelled, unsurpassed one;
  - 8 Purusha-damya-sarathi, trainer of men;
  - 9 Shasta Devamanushyanam, teacher of gods and humans;
  - 10 Buddha-Bhagavat, Awakened World-Honored One.

### No creator god, and the castes again (slides 26 to 27)
- By the Buddha's time Vedic panentheism was becoming popular (recall the Purusha Hymn).
- The Buddhist tradition ridicules the creator idea: at the beginning of a kalpa Brahma wakes up first from non-being and wishes for companions; just as he has that thought other heavenly beings wake up and appear, and Brahma thinks he created them.
- The gods are deluded and cannot save us from suffering.
- Samsara is the infinite regress problem in Buddhism.
- The origin of the four castes shows that the Purusha Hymn is fairly late in the Rig Veda: Brahmins (priests and teachers), Kshatriyas (nobles and warriors), Vaishyas (merchants), Shudras (labourers and artisans).
- The earliest hymns reflect a polytheistic and nomadic society.

### The Arhats (slide 28)
- The Buddha converted thousands of people over 45 years, including his cousins, wife, son, relatives and former servants, the ascetics he had trained with, and people he met on his constant journey.
- Many of them are considered to have reached nirvana and became known as the **Arhats**.
- Arhats differ from the Buddha: although liberated, they did not possess all the Buddha virtues and attained liberation by following the Buddha's teachings.

### Notable disciples (slides 29 to 34)
- **Ashvajit** (Pali Assaji): his father was one of the Brahmins who predicted that baby Siddhartha would become either a universal monarch or a Buddha; he is one of the 5 ascetics who practised austerities with Siddhartha, who become his first disciples and arhats; he converts Shariputra and Maudgalyayana.
- **Shariputra** (Pali Sariputta): an ascetic and a friend of Maudgalyayana; hearing Ashvajit's summary of the teaching he attains initial awakening, and several weeks later, after meeting the Buddha, he becomes an arhat; known for his **wisdom**.
- Ashvajit's summary is the **Verse of Causality** (Pratityasamutpada gatha, "ye dharma hetu"), perhaps the most famous line of early Buddhist teaching, found in all traditions: those dharmas which arise from a cause, the Tathagata has declared their cause, and that which is the cessation of them; thus the great renunciant (shramana) has taught.
- **Maudgalyayana** (Pali Moggallana): known for his **supernatural abilities**, which let him see his mother as a preta.
- According to some accounts both Shariputra and Maudgalyayana die voluntarily before the Buddha, not wishing to witness his final Parinirvana: Maudgalyayana is beaten to death, and Shariputra dies after converting his mother.
- **Ananda**, "the man who remembered everything": the Buddha's cousin; phenomenal memory; the Buddha's assistant; known for his **compassion** but also for lingering attachments; asked the Buddha to ordain women into the sangha; attained arhatship only after the Buddha's death, because he had focused on service; a "crypto-bodhisattva" and a model for later traditions; celebrated for the phrase "Thus have I heard" that opens all Buddhist sutras.
- Ananda died of old age: by one source he corrected a young monk who was reciting a line wrongly, the monk's teacher said Ananda had become senile, and on hearing that Ananda decided to pass into final nirvana.
- **Devadatta**: another cousin of the Buddha; joined for selfish reasons; created a **schism** by inventing stricter rules than the Buddha's; the Buddha sent Shariputra and Maudgalyayana to bring back the monks who had left with him; later tried to kill the Buddha, failed, and fell into the deepest hell, literally swallowed by the earth.
- Devadatta shows sectarianism within early Buddhism: the Chinese traveller **Faxian** (337 to 422 CE) reports Devadatta's followers in India, who worshipped all the other Buddhas except Siddhartha.
- **Maitreya**: likely a lay follower named **Ajita** during the Buddha's lifetime; the future Buddha; now a god in Tushita Heaven; will become human in the distant future and repeat the 12 Deeds; a bodhisattva who, like Siddhartha, spent endless lifetimes practising the 10 virtues; a mirror projection of the current Buddha into the future; a model for later Buddhists who discarded the ideal of arhatship.

### Conservative versus innovative Buddhism, and Theravada by country (slides 35 to 37)
- **Conservative Buddhism**, which includes **Theravada**, accepts arhatship as an ideal, does not aspire to Buddhahood, holds that there is only one Buddha and one bodhisattva at a time, and believes in the finality of nirvana.
- **Innovative Buddhism** holds the ideal of Buddhahood for all, innumerable Buddhas and Bodhisattvas at a given time, arhatship not as an ideal, and nirvana not as final liberation; it is the subject of the next lectures.
- Theravada is about 30% of all Buddhists, about 150 million in Southeast Asia.
- Theravada share by country: Sri Lanka 70%, Cambodia 97%, Laos 67%, Myanmar 80%, Thailand 90%, and in Vietnam the Khmer Krom ethnic group.

### Asceticism and the Middle Way (slide 38)
- Siddhartha did extreme fasting during his 6 years of ascetic practice in the wilderness before his enlightenment, almost never slept, and meditated all the time.
- After an enlightening experience in meditation he declared that extreme asceticism does not free one from suffering.
- He then established and taught the Middle Way, free from the extremes of indulgence and complete self-deprivation.

### The Four Noble Truths and the three poisons (slides 39 to 44)
- The Buddha began teaching by explaining the **Four Noble Truths** to the group of ascetics he had practised with.
- First Noble Truth: life is ultimately suffering and sorrow (Pali **dukkha**); seeking pleasures and avoiding pain only leads to more suffering later.
- Second Noble Truth: suffering is caused by the **three poisons**:
  - **ignorance** of the impermanence of everything that comes into existence, including one's own self, depicted as a **pig**;
  - **craving** and desire (passion), depicted as a **chicken**: pleasure cannot last and leads only to rebirth and more suffering;
  - **aversion** to pain (hatred), depicted as a **snake**, which only causes more pain.
- Third Noble Truth: there is an end to suffering, because everything that comes into existence is bound to end, including suffering itself; when all suffering ends the person achieves nirvana, the permanent end of the three poisons and of rebirth, and the only ultimate peace and end of conditioned existence (samsara).
- The deck illustrates the Third Noble Truth with a clip from The Matrix: Agent Smith is the allegory for samsara, and Neo for the future buddha.
- Fourth Noble Truth: the way to end desires is a moderately ascetic path with three aims:
  - extinguish ignorance (pig) with direct knowledge of impermanence in meditation and observation of the world, including decomposing corpses;
  - extinguish desire (chicken) by giving up one's possessions and sex as a practice of generosity;
  - extinguish aversion (snake) by practising loving-kindness (metta), compassion (karuna), co-rejoicing (mudita) and equanimity (upekkha).

### The Wheel of Life (slide 41)
- Buddhist temples often depict the cycle of life and death as the **Wheel of Life** (**Bhavacakra**).
- The three poisons are at the centre of the wheel.
- Next come the good and bad deeds: white and ascending, black and descending.
- These lead to the six planes of existence, shown bottom to top clockwise: hells, animals, humans, gods, demigods, hungry ghosts.
- The lord of death, Yama, the personification of existence tainted by the poisons, holds the wheel as a mirror to show us reality.
- The only way to escape the circle is to follow the Buddhist path.

### Extinguishing ignorance: corpse meditation (slides 45 to 48)
- Ignorance is extinguished by experiencing impermanence, for example by meditating on the stages of decomposition of a corpse.
- This practice is found in the **Satipatthana Sutta** of the Theravada canon.
- Practitioners face or visualise a corpse in stages from freshly dead to skeletal remains and reflect that their own body is subject to the same process.

### Extinguishing desire: giving up possessions and sex (slides 49 to 51)
- The monastic rules are summarised as giving up worldly possessions and sex to live as a monastic.
- Young men and women leave their families to become monks and nuns dedicated to the Path.
- The path is structured by rules and ascetic practices, including celibacy, thought to free the person from addiction to desire and the suffering it causes.
- The key point: giving up possessions and giving up sex are both **giving**, the opposite of craving (the chicken), so both are a form of self-sacrifice toward escaping the cycle of births and deaths for oneself and potentially for others.
- **Sex negativity** is a view of sex (broadly understood) as spiritually dangerous or detrimental to religious goals.
- **Sex positivity** is a view of sex as helpful to spiritual development, to achieving religious goals, or as an ultimate goal in itself.
- Buddhism is sex negative: according to **Paul Numrich**, the human predicament "begins with our desire for life's pleasures", which leads to the cycles of suffering, births, sickness, old age and death, and sex "epitomizes" the human predicament.

### The three baskets (slide 52)
- The Pali Canon is divided into 3 baskets, the **Tipitaka**:
  - the **Vinaya Pitaka**, 5 books of monastic rules;
  - the **Sutta Pitaka**, 5 collections of many suttas;
  - the **Abhidhamma Pitaka**, 7 books.

### Sudinna and the first rule (slides 53 to 54)
- **Sudinna** joins the order of monks to live a religious life.
- On a visit home his parents and former wife convince him to father a son so the family will continue without him.
- He agrees and has sex with his former wife three times in a secluded place in the forest; at the time there was no official rule against sex.
- He feels remorse, knows what he did is not in keeping with the religious life, and confesses; the other monks and then the Buddha chastise him.
- The Buddha's point: the Dhamma is to overcome craving, yet Sudinna set his heart on craving, and he "neither inspires faith in the faithless nor increases the faithful".
- The Buddha proclaims the **first rule: no sex**.

### Structure of the Vinaya and the Patimokkha rule counts (slide 55)
- The **Suttavibhanga** contains the basic training rules for **bhikkhus** (monks) and **bhikkhunis** (nuns), with the origin story for each rule.
- The rules are summarised in the **Patimokkha**: 227 rules for bhikkhus and 311 for bhikkhunis.
- The rule classes, with counts for monks then nuns:
  - **Parajika**, rules entailing expulsion from the Sangha ("Defeat"): 4 and 8;
  - **Sanghadisesa**, rules entailing an initial and subsequent meeting of the Sangha: 13 and 17;
  - **Aniyata**, indefinite rules: 2 and 0;
  - **Nissaggiya pacittiya**, forfeiture and confession: 30 and 30;
  - **Pacittiya**, confession: 92 and 166;
  - **Patidesaniya**, acknowledgement: 4 and 8;
  - **Sekhiya**, rules of training: 75 and 75;
  - **Adhikarana samatha**, rules for settling disputes: 7 and 7.

### Misogyny in Theravada (slide 56)
- In most Indian traditions of the Buddha's time women were considered inferior to men.
- A female birth was believed to be caused by a more pronounced accumulation of poison number 2 (passion) and other bad karma.
- The female body was thought less robust than a male's and prone to more pain (menstruation).

### The sex rules in the Vinaya (slides 57 to 62)
- After Sudinna, further origin stories close the loopholes: sex with humans, with non-human animals (a female monkey), with non-humans (naga, peta, yakkha), with dead bodies or parts of them, any penetration to the extent of a sesame seed, oral, anal and genital sex, and penetrating oneself.
- The rule is that a bhikkhu who engages in any form of sexual intercourse is Defeated.
- The first parajika for monks: voluntary sexual intercourse (genital, anal or oral) with a human, non-human or animal, by a monk who has not renounced the training or declared his weakness, is a parajika offence.
- Bhikkhuni parajika 5 and 8 defeat a nun who, lusting, consents to a lusting man's touching her between the collar-bone and the knees, or takes his hand, lets him touch her robe, or goes to a rendezvous or hidden place with him for that purpose.
- The first five sanghadisesa for monks cover deliberately emitting semen (except in dreams), lustful bodily contact with a woman, lewd words to a woman, praising to a woman the ministering to his sensuality, and acting as a matchmaker; the touching that only requires meetings of the Community for a monk defeats a nun.
- The bhikkhuni sanghadisesa include travelling alone or staying away a night alone, accepting food from a lusting man while lusting, telling another nun to take such food, and conveying marriage or liaison proposals.
- The two aniyata rules for monks concern sitting alone with a woman on a seat secluded enough for intercourse, or secluded enough for lewd words, as a trustworthy laywoman might report it.
- The nuns' confession rules include genital slapping, a dildo, ablution deeper than two finger joints, standing or speaking with a man, bathing naked, and two bhikkhunis sharing a bed, blanket or sleeping mat.
- Further misconduct for monks includes masturbation, having non-relative nuns wash one's robes, lying down in the same building or boat as a woman, teaching a woman or nun alone, giving or sewing robes for a non-relative nun, and travelling alone with or trying to be alone with a woman or nun.

### Extinguishing aversion: the four immeasurables (slides 63 to 64)
- Aversion and hatred are abandoned by meditating on the **four immeasurables**:
  - 1 loving-kindness (Pali **metta**), wishing happiness to all living beings;
  - 2 compassion (**karuna**), loving-kindness in the face of suffering, the practical wish for all beings to be free from suffering;
  - 3 co-rejoicing (**mudita**), rejoicing at the happiness beings experience as the result of their good deeds, however fleeting, by wishing them the fruits of their good acts;
  - 4 equanimity (**upekkha**), the wish for all living beings finally to abandon the three poisons and attain nirvana, without preference for anyone, friends and enemies alike.
- Theravada mostly recommends the four immeasurables to lay followers, which sets it apart from the Mahayana Buddhism of the next module.
- The deck closes with **Avalokitesvara** (Guanyin), the deity of compassion in Mahayana Buddhism.
