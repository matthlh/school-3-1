## Slides, organized

### Buddhism reaches China (slides 3 to 5)
- Around the 1st century CE Buddhism spread out of the Indian subcontinent into Central Asia and reached China slowly along the **Silk Road**, carried peacefully by monks and merchants.
- **Emperor Ming** of the Eastern Han (28 to 75 CE) dreamed of a golden figure riding a white horse from the west who helped him wipe out the **Xiongnu** nomads.
  - The golden figure was taken to be the Buddha.
  - He founded the **White Horse Monastery** in Luoyang in 68 CE.
  - Most later emperors became patrons of Buddhism as a state-protecting religion.
- In China, Confucianism and Daoism were both state ideologies and religions, so Buddhism adapted by borrowing heavily from both and adding ideas and practices of its own.

### Confucianism as a religion (slides 6 to 12)
- Confucianism is often called philosophy or practical ethics, but the deck says it is a religion: it presupposes supernatural forces and an afterlife that mirrors this world.
- It is an **immanent** religion, not a transcendent one, because it is about order in this world: the state, the home and the family.
- It is religious in three ways.
  - Ideals: this-worldly harmony, an ideal person and an ideal society reached through self-cultivation.
  - Ritual efficacy: ritual lets the living family feed its dead ancestors.
  - Afterlife: a world populated by ancestors.
- **Confucius** (Kongzi, 551 to 479 BCE) is a founder figure. His sayings were collected as **the Analects** (Lunyu): pithy, dialogical, didactic, often quoting the Book of Odes, and the most important classic of Chinese civilization.
- The key concept is **de**, "virtue" or "moral power", a mystical religious power.
  - De is inherited. Kings get it from their ancestors' spirits, ancestors get it from **Heaven** (tian, a deity), and retainers get it through loyalty and filial piety toward their own ancestors.
  - Rituals, sacrifices, dances and music keep de flowing from its source.
  - Every vocation has its own de. **Wu** (martial power) is the de of brave warriors, and fallen heroes who show it can become gods, such as **Guan Yu**.
- Confucius systematized the Zhou rituals; he did not invent them.

### The self-cultivation program (slides 13 to 15, 20 to 26)
- The path to de starts with **filial piety** and learning: be filial at home, respectful in public, then study the civil arts (Analects 1.6).
- Filial piety extends to ancestor worship, and maintaining the sacrifices makes the people return to virtue (Analects 1.9).
- **Li** (ritual) means religious rites and also everyday etiquette. Rulers govern by ritual, not by violence.
- Besides de, Confucianism develops **qi**, vital energy. Mencius (about 371 to 289 BCE) speaks of "flood-like qi" that is born from an accumulation of rightness and starves without it (Mencius 2A2).
  - His agrarian metaphor: do the right thing and take your time, and do not pull on the sprouts to make them grow.
  - The sprouts of virtue are the innate goodness of human nature, as when an evil king pities an ox led to slaughter.
- The **five relationships** are emperor and minister, father and son, elder and younger brother, husband and wife, and friends. Extended, they stand for all of society.

### The Five Classics and the golden age (slides 16 to 19)
- The **Five Classics** come from the Zhou (1046 to 256 BCE), were systematized in the Han from 202 BCE, and their selection is attributed to Confucius: the Book of Changes, the Classic of History, the Classic of Poetry, the Records of Rites and the Spring and Autumn Annals.
- The early Zhou (1046 to 771 BCE) is the ideal age, and its aristocratic, patrilineal family system is the ideal family, kept up through ancestral rites of food, wine and prayer (Ebrey).
- The Classics have four overlapping concerns: individual, familial, political and cosmic.
- **Ritual masters** lead worship of deities, natural principles and ancestors at public temples.

### Ancestor veneration, family and women (slides 27 to 39)
- The kinship structure, extended beyond death, was the medium of religious practice.
- At ancestral rites a **personator** (shi) stands in for the dead: ideally the grandson for a dead grandfather, and the grandson's wife for the grandmother. The son and daughter-in-law cannot be personators, because they must show the most reverence.
- Ebrey describes the Confucian family as heteronormative, patriarchal, patrilineal and patrilocal, putting filial duty above romantic love. Marriage was obligatory for men, men could take concubines, and sons were needed to continue the rites.
- **Ban Zhao** (48 to 112 CE), a Han scholar, wrote **Admonitions for Women** (Nujie): humility, harmony, obedience to the mother-in-law, submission to the husband, gentleness and no remarriage. A later Classic of Filial Piety for Women puts her in Confucius' place as the teacher.

### Civil and martial (slides 40 to 61)
- The Zhou culture is split into **wen** (civil) and **wu** (martial).
  - **King Wen** (1152 to 1050 BCE) preferred negotiation and is credited with the 8 trigrams and the 64 hexagrams used in divination (the Book of Changes).
  - **King Wu** (died 1046 BCE) conquered the Shang at the Battle of Muye by his martial virtue.
- Wen means discerning patterns to predict the future: divination, omens, fengshui, history and the civil arts.
- Wu means displaying the charisma of a cultivated character to punish the wicked; the six arts are ritual, music, calligraphy, math, archery and charioteering.
- **Wu (martial) is not bing (military)**. Wu is honorific and religious, a posthumous noble title and a virtue (wude); bing is the plain word for soldiers, weapons and strategy.
- **Sunzi** (Sun Tzu, 544 to 496 BCE) wrote the Bing fa, "Maxims of Warfare" (the Art of War): win by any means when virtue is lacking. The Mahabharata makes the same move when Krishna tells Arjuna to use cunning in the degenerate age (kali yuga).
- Both camps agree the best battle is won without a fight. For Confucians martial might comes from self-cultivation; for strategists it is a deterrent. From the Spring and Autumn period (770 to 481 BCE) martial virtue was the ideal and strategy the realpolitik.
- In the **Zuo Zhuan**'s Battle of Bi, the King of Chu refuses to feast on victory; the character for wu is read as "stop" plus "halberd".

### The Confucian hero journey (slides 62 to 82)
- The **myth of Pangu**: mold chaos into order, take on as much responsibility as you can, produce offspring, and if you die in battle without heirs you become a god for all posterity. It resembles the Purusha Hymn but is pantheistic and late (about 200 CE).
- After the Zhou fell in 256 BCE, Heaven became a metaphor for universal order, and the Confucian Way became taking on social roles of growing responsibility: filial piety, then loyalty, then martial power in war.
- The Great Learning chains it: investigate things, extend knowledge, be sincere, rectify the heart, cultivate the person, regulate the family, order the state.
- To check the military aristocracy, the Han raised the loyal **scholar official**, and the Sui and Tang created the **imperial examinations** (605 to 1905), which tested the Confucian classics; about 1 in 100 passed, after a decade or more of study.
- Confucian core value: **ren**, humaneness or benevolence, with effortless action (wu wei). Analects 2.4 is Confucius' life stages, from setting his mind on learning at 15 to following his heart's desires without overstepping at 70.
- **Yue Fei** (1103 to 1142) is the military hero: a peasant who passed the military exam, tattooed "Serve the Country with Utmost Loyalty" on his back, fought the Jin and was killed with his emperor's consent.
- **Huang Chao** (835 to 884) shows when it goes wrong: he failed the exam, smuggled salt, rebelled during famine, took Chang'an in 881 and declared the Qi dynasty, and was killed in 884. Dropouts more often became Daoist hermits or Buddhist monks.

### Daoism (slides 83 to 98)
- **Laozi** ("Old Master", assumed 400s BCE) is the founder figure; the **Daodejing** ("Classic of the Way and its Power") is also called the Laozi. The earliest communities date from the later Han.
- Daoism means living in harmony with the **dao**, the way underlying all things, without resistance and effortlessly.
- Cosmogony: primordial chaos (dao) splits into qi, qi splits into **yin and yang**, and yin and yang give the **five phases** (wuxing).
- Matter is in constant flux, with yin and yang as its opposing aspects.
- Kohn: people are containers of qi that can be manipulated through breath control, healing exercises, sexual hygiene, diet and meditation to live longer.
- The body mirrors the cosmos: heaven, human and earth match the head, heart and pubic region, and practice keeps qi flowing. A person in perfect harmony with the dao becomes **immortal**.
- Immortal figures: the God of Longevity, the Eight Immortals, the **Queen Mother of the West** (Xiwangmu) on Mount Kunlun, who gives the elixir of immortality that **Chang'e** stole before fleeing to the moon, and the peaches of immortality.

### Three ways to selflessness (slides 99 to 110)
- Confucian: sincerity in embodying role models. Buddhist: revealing the illusion of self through meditation. Daoist: becoming immortal by accumulating qi and returning to innate simplicity.
- Confucian qi grows by constant refinement, "carving and polishing". Daoist qi grows by returning to nature, the **uncarved block**.
  - The uncarved block is the calm mind of meditation on space: the use of a wheel, a pot or a room lies where there is nothing (Daodejing 11).
  - It is also stillness found by watching things return to their root (Daodejing 16).
- Daoist qi cultivation uses the image of an **embryo**: gather qi in the lower abdomen, transform the raw essence (jing) as it rises, and let spirit (shen) transform it at the crown into an immortal.
- The Daoist apotheosis is control of the five states of energy: metal, water, wood, fire and earth.
- Rewards of the three journeys: Confucian spiritual immortality as ancestral de; Buddhist Buddhahood or rebirth as a bodhisattva; Daoist physical immortality and control of the five elements.

### Daoist gods and martial arts (slides 106 to 118)
- By the Han, Laozi was considered an immortal (later the claimed ancestor of the Tang), and the **Yellow Emperor** was claimed as the Han's ancestor.
- Ge Hong (283 to 343 CE) described a vision of Laozi seven feet tall with a long head, the archetype for drawing immortals.
- Commoners propitiate immortals with food, incense, liturgy, music, dance and impersonation in trance, and receive talismans and protective qi.
- **Xuanwu** (Zhenwu), the Dark or True Martial Deity of **Mount Wudang**, washed his own stomach and intestines in a river to atone, was made immortal, and subdued the turtle and snake they became. Emperor Yongle credited him for ending the Jingnan Rebellion (1399 to 1402).
- Temple combat performances in trance, retold in Qing wuxia stories, gave rise to Wudang "martial arts". **Gongfu** means mastery of any skill; **wushu** became the 20th-century term for martial arts. Beijing Opera refines these temple performances.
- **Guan Yu** is a guardian bodhisattva for Buddhists, a celestial general for Daoists, and under the Qing the Martial Saint beside Confucius the Civil Saint.
