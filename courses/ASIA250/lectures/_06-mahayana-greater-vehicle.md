## Slides, organized

### After the Buddha's final nirvana (slides 2 and 3)
- Tradition puts the Buddha's final nirvana at the Vesak full moon (4th lunar month) of 483 BCE, though closer to 400 BCE is more likely.
- Buddhism then spread around the Indian subcontinent, and by the time of **King Ashoka** (304 to 232 BCE), when writing came into use, there were many schools.
- Two trends ran through them.
  - **Conservation**: elaborating the Buddha's teaching on how to become an arhat, as in the **Sthaviravada** (Pali Theravada, "The Way of the Elders"), with emphasis on listing the **dharmas**, the building blocks of existence, in collections called **Abhidharma**.
  - **Innovation**: for example the **Lokottaravada** (Transcendentalism), which held that the Buddha was a supramundane being outside samsara.

### The conservative schools and their two extremes (slides 4 to 7)
- **Sarvastivada** ("everything exists") is one extreme.
  - Dharmas exist in the three times: past, present and future.
  - Their combinations are temporary and make the world we perceive.
  - To reach nirvana and become an arhat you must understand the dharmas (the Abhidharma lists) and how they make the temporary world.
  - The deck's analogy is atoms in physics combining into elements.
- Abhidharma lists differ in length: Theravada has 82 dharmas, and some Mahayana schools have 100.
- **Sautrantika** ("the sutras are enough") is the other extreme.
  - Everything exists only for a moment.
  - We have no access to the ordinary world, only after-the-fact, inaccurate and impermanent mental representations of it.
  - There is no need to search for dharmas; follow the Buddha's teaching on impermanence (momentariness).
  - The deck's analogy is quantum mechanics: atoms are not indivisible, and dividing them leads to an infinite regress.
- Most conservative schools, including modern Theravada, took the middle position: dharmas do not exist independently, because they arise from causes and conditions, but the schools still built Abhidharma lists and rejected radical momentariness.

### The innovationists (slides 8 to 11)
- The **Mahasamghikas** ("Great Assembly") and their sub-school the **Lokottaravada** split from the Sthaviravada because they thought arhatship was not a sufficient goal and looked to the Buddha for salvation.
  - Over time the Buddha became ever more splendid, a transcendent being (**lokottara**) with powers such as creating a **Buddha Land** where he lives with his true followers, a positive reframing of nirvana.
  - The elders, some counted as arhats, looked like ordinary people: they got sick, grew old, could die violently and had wet dreams, so becoming like them did not seem worth pursuing.
  - Toward the 1st century CE these groups gave rise to the Mahayana traditions.
- The Lokottaravadins spread the belief that the Buddha's body bore the **32 Marks of a Great Man**.

### What "Mahayana" means (slides 12 and 13)
- **Mahayana** means "the Great(er) Vehicle".
- Mahayana Buddhists called Theravada and the other conservative schools **Hinayana**, the "Smaller Vehicle", a polemical, pejorative term the lecturer asks students not to use for Theravada.
- The two forms compared:

| | Theravada | Mahayana |
|---|---|---|
| Stance | Conservative | Innovative, "progressive" |
| Schools | The only conservative school surviving today (others, such as Mahasamghika and Pudgalavada, died out) | Many schools and regional varieties |
| Scriptures | Pali Canon | Sanskrit, then Chinese, then Tibetan |
| Region | "Southern Buddhism", Southeast Asia | "Northern Buddhism", Nepal, Tibet, East Asia |

### The biggest difference: the goal (slides 14 and 15)
- Conservative position: a monk or nun is a **sravaka** ("listener", follower) who follows the Buddha's instructions to become an **arhat** ("worthy of worship"), who attains nirvana and completely severs the three poisons (ignorance, passion, aversion).
- Innovative position, developing about 100 years after the Buddha:
  - The Buddha was far more than an arhat, because he practised self-sacrificial giving over many lives and gathered infinite merit (conservatives agree).
  - Arhats are not fully liberated and keep a residue of the three poisons (conservatives disagree).
- The arhats' failings, one per poison:
  - Ignorance residue: they realised there is no self (anatman) but have not directly experienced the impermanence of the phenomenal world.
  - Passion residue: they can still have nocturnal emissions, a sign of subconscious desire.
  - Aversion residue: they lack enough compassion, because of excessive world-denying practice such as corpse meditation and strict rule-keeping.
- So, the progressives say, a Buddhist should aim to become a Buddha, whose awakening is perfect, not an arhat.

### How one becomes a Buddha (slides 16 to 19)
- Both sides agree it takes countless acts of compassion and self-denial over many lifetimes to gather merit.
- Those past-life acts are the **Jataka** tales ("rebirth stories"), and the Buddha before awakening is the **bodhisattva** ("awakening being").
- **Vessantara Jataka**: as Prince Vessantara, the bodhisattva gives away all his possessions, even his wife and children (generosity, abandoning possessions).
- **Prince Sattva Jataka**: seeing a starving tigress and her cubs, the prince jumps off a cliff and offers his body as food (self-sacrifice, abandoning the ego).
- The virtues a bodhisattva must perfect:

| Theravada: 10 paramis (Pali) | Mahayana: 10 paramitas (Sanskrit) |
|---|---|
| Generosity | Generosity |
| Morality | Morality |
| Renunciation | Patience |
| Wisdom | Energy |
| Energy | Meditation |
| Patience | Wisdom |
| Truthfulness | Skillful means (generosity and wisdom) |
| Resolution | Resolution |
| Loving-kindness | Power |
| Equanimity | Knowledge |

- In the Mahayana list the first six are essential, and the last four are built from them.

### The Mahayana goal and the multiverse (slides 20 to 22)
- In Mahayana the arhat, an ascetic who attains nirvana for himself, is not the goal; the bodhisattva, shown in princely dress helping all beings, and the Buddha are the path and the goal.
- The practitioner takes the **Bodhisattva Vow** from day one: to practise the Buddha's teaching, become a Buddha in the future and save all living beings.
- The problem: in Theravada there can be only one Buddha in a universe while his teaching lasts.
- Mahayana's answer is infinite universes, a multiverse, in some of which Buddhas are teaching, in some the teaching endures after them, and in some there is no Buddha, so bodhisattvas can be reborn where there is none and become Buddhas there.

### What Mahayana keeps and what it adds (slides 23 to 27)
- Kept from conservative Buddhism: the **Vinaya** monastic rules (no possessions, no sex), the Four Noble Truths, and the Jatakas with the ten perfections (six core, four derived).
- Added:
  - **Bodhisattva precepts** on top of the Vinaya, sometimes instead of it.
  - Visualising cosmic Buddhas such as **Amitabha** and travelling to their **pure lands** in meditation or at death; worshipping powerful cosmic bodhisattvas such as **Guanyin**; techniques of instantaneous awakening (**Zen**).
  - New sutras describing these.
- The **Brahmajala Sutra** ("Brahma's Net Sutra") lists ten major precepts for beginner bodhisattvas, close to Theravada's; the third forbids inappropriate sexual behaviour (sex without consent or harmful to beings), and monastics still abstain from sex entirely.
- The third major precept's text uses male-oriented language and calls encouraging promiscuity a **Parajika** offence.
- Buddhist morality rests on **sentience**: it forbids what harms sentient beings rather than following divine commands, and the monastic rules and bodhisattva precepts add ascetic self-sacrifice and generosity.
- **Precept 20**: every male being has been my father and every female being my mother, so a bodhisattva must rescue animals about to be killed (compare last lecture's loving-kindness meditation).

### Emptiness (slides 28 to 33)
- For Theravada the poison of ignorance is failing to see that everything is impermanent.
- Mahayana adds that everything is also **interdependent**: each thing depends on its parts, on causes and conditions (a seed needs water, soil and sun), and on our minds, which divide and classify the world.
- That interdependence is **emptiness** (**sunya**, 空): nothing exists independently of its parts, causes and the observing mind.
- Where arhats and Theravadins observe impermanence to detach from the world, Mahayanists say nothing in the cosmos is independent of us.
- The image is **Brahma's Net**, which gives the Brahmajala Sutra its name: each knot holds a jewel that reflects all the others, each unique yet connected.
- So the division into self and others is artificial and causes suffering; whoever experiences emptiness first-hand sees through the eyes of the infinite Buddhas and becomes a Buddha, without having to become a monk or travel to Buddha Lands.
- Realising selflessness through altruism, detachment and monastic life is believed to tap the universe's limitless power, producing miracles such as levitation, multiple bodies and mind-reading; this true state is also called **Buddha nature**.
- Mahayana's ambivalence: everyone can become a Buddha by experiencing emptiness, but that is hard, so you still walk the path, take the Vinaya and bodhisattva precepts and practise to remove the three poisons.

### Conservative and progressive summed up (slide 34)

| Conservative | Progressive |
|---|---|
| The disciple aims for nirvana, release from births and deaths | The follower declares themselves a bodhisattva, to lead others to the same ideal |
| The arhat is a liberated follower, not reborn, free of the three poisons | The Buddha is free of rebirth and the poisons and also creates a Buddha Land where bodhisattvas study |
| One universe, one Buddha at a time | Many universes, so bodhisattvas can become Buddhas quickly elsewhere |
| Modern Theravada | Most of the Buddhist world |

### Meditation from an axiological perspective (slides 35 to 42)
- The lecturer's framework: meditation is an aesthetic experience that constructs values, which in turn shape behaviour.
- **Axiology** has two branches: **aesthetics** (embodied experience, affect, valuing X over Y, memory and repeated exposure) and **ethics** (values activated in daily life, choosing to do X rather than Y).
- The chain: embodied experience, then affect, then cognition, then retention of the value, then behaviour.
- Two types of meditation:

| | Conservative | Progressive |
|---|---|---|
| Experience | Impermanence (無常) | Emptiness (空) of self and phenomena |
| Steps | Breathing, calming the mind, scanning sensations as they rise and pass | Breathing or an object, emptying the mind, watching all sensations like a magic show in the theatre of consciousness until subject and object disappear |
| Value | **Detachment** (出離), distancing from pleasant and unpleasant alike | **Compassion** (慈悲), empathy for beings still enchanted by the show |
| Ethical outcome | Cessation of desires, renunciate precepts (proscriptions), monastic life, a monastic community apart from the world | The bodhisattva ideal, bodhisattva precepts and positive virtues, the monk-lay line matters less (the **Vimalakirti Sutra**), a multi-universe community of bodhisattvas |

- In art, detachment is the forest-dwelling **arhat** (羅漢), bald, in mendicant robes; compassion is the cosmic **bodhisattva** with many heads and arms, wearing jewellery and holding weapons that stand for the power of knowledge.
- China kept both: novices learn some renunciation, while experienced monastics and lay people focus on compassion.

### Wisdom and skillful means (slides 43 to 47)
- Mahayana says Theravada's "this too shall pass" is indirect, deduced; meditation on emptiness gives the direct, unconditioned experience called **wisdom** (**prajna**), present from the start but hidden by samsara.
- Egoless compassion turns the other two poisons, anger and passion, into **skillful means** (**upaya**): anger becomes displays of power that push beings toward awakening, and desire becomes charisma that attracts them.
- Power: **Vajrapani** (modelled on Indra) and **Mahasthamaprapta** ("arrival of the great strength", shown subduing a lion), equated in some traditions such as the Tibetan, personify skillful means that turn anger toward the path.
- Attraction: **Sarvanivaranaviskambhin** ("the one who blocks all obstacles"), shown as an androgyne with a bare chest, removes the five hindrances (desire, hostility, laziness, distraction and worry, doubt) and turns passion toward the path.
- **Avalokiteshvara** (Chinese **Guanyin**), male in India, was feminised in China, where compassion is a feminine (yin) quality.

### The great bodhisattvas (slides 48 to 55)
- **Maitreya**, the future Buddha of early Buddhism, was in the Buddha's lifetime a layman named **Ajita**, vowed long ago to become a Buddha, now lives as a god in **Tushita** heaven and will come as the next Buddha; with the Jatakas he is the prototype of the cosmic bodhisattvas.
- **Manjushri** personifies wisdom; his sword cuts through the illusion of samsara.
- **Samantabhadra** ("All Good") stands for selfless service (bhakti) through his ten vows, from paying homage to all Buddhas to dedicating all merit to beings' Buddhahood; he is the model of the Mahayana path, and the deck compares his vows to the nine processes of Hindu **bhakti-yoga** toward Krishna.
- **Kshitigarbha** ("Essence of the Earth") began as a Brahmin woman who freed her mother from hell through devotion and dedicating merit, then vowed to free all beings, and now wanders the hells as a monk.
- **Akashagarbha** ("Space Essence") symbolises a mind embracing emptiness, equanimous as space and purifying negative karma; important in Esoteric Buddhism (Vajrayana, lecture 9).
- Together these are the **8 Great Bodhisattvas**: Maitreya (benevolence), Manjushri (wisdom), Avalokiteshvara (compassion), Vajrapani or Mahasthamaprapta (power), Samantabhadra (conduct), Sarvanivaranaviskambhin (blocking obstructions), Kshitigarbha (earth, merit) and Akashagarbha (space, purifying transgressions); they turn the three poisons into wisdom and skillful means.
- Buddhist devotion to a bodhisattva answered the growing popularity of Hindu bhakti and spread in the early centuries CE.
- Example: **Kukai** (774 to 835), pioneer of Esoteric Buddhism in Japan, recited Akashagarbha's eulogy as a novice; the bodhisattva told him in a dream to sail to Tang China, and he brought Esoteric Buddhism back to Japan.

### The Mahayana follower as hero (slides 56 to 64)
- The vow to become a Buddha to free all beings in the multiverse is **bodhicitta** ("enlightening mind"), modelled on Siddhartha's renunciation after seeing sickness, old age and death.
- It is a heroic journey rather than early Buddhism's withdrawal, started by monastics but spreading to the laity, who become bodhisattvas rather than donors, which is why "greater vehicle" fits.
- **Joseph Campbell**'s **monomyth**, *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949): the hero leaves the known for the unknown, faces a monster and brings back a treasure; a bodhisattva bridges our world and the multiverse of Buddhas, creating a paracosm the community shares.
- Followers join the great bodhisattvas' journey by chanting scriptures, such as the most-chanted text in East Asia, chapter 25 of the **Lotus Sutra**, on the miracles of Guanyin.
- The **Gandavyuha Sutra** ("A Series of Episodes"): the youth **Sudhana** meets 53 teachers, including Maitreya and Manjushri but also a slave, a child, a physician and a ship's captain, 20 of them women (the courtesan Vasumitra, the Buddha's wife and mother, a queen, a princess, goddesses).
- In the **Tower of Maitreya** Sudhana experiences emptiness and the interconnected multiverse.
- The sutra ends with **Samantabhadra** telling him to put his wisdom into practice as skillful means; Sudhana ends where he began, the same yet transformed, a metaphor for realising emptiness.
- The deck asks how **Ko Un**'s modern rendering (the novel *Little Pilgrim*) compares with the traditional account.
