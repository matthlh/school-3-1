## Slides, organized

### The Brahmanical order
- The Rig Veda hymn that names the four castes is late in the collection; the earliest hymns describe a polytheistic, nomadic society.
- The four castes are **brahmins** (priests and teachers), **kshatriyas** (nobles and warriors), **vaishyas** (merchants) and **shudras** (labourers and artisans).
- The priesthood became a hereditary male lineage of ritual specialists who married among themselves and encoded the castes into the Vedas.
- Here **dharma** means duty: each person stays in their caste and does its work.
- Brahmins perform rituals and teach.
- Kings and nobles keep order and fight intruders.
- Merchants make profit, pay taxes and give donations.
- Artisans, labourers and peasants follow their vocations, pay taxes and donate.

### The Bhagavadgita as the Brahmanical answer
- The Bhagavadgita is a chapter of the epic Mahabharata, which the deck dates to the 3rd century BCE.
- Arjuna does not want to kill his teachers, friends and relatives on the enemy side, and wants to become a wandering ascetic instead of gaining the bad karma of war.
- Krishna's first argument is that Arjuna is a kshatriya and fighting is his duty.
- Krishna's second argument is that the soul of every living being, the **atman**, is indestructible, so Arjuna will not really kill anyone.
- At the end Arjuna repents and dies of exhaustion on a pilgrimage to the Himalayas.
- Krishna is shot in the heel by a hunter who mistakes his foot for a deer; he blesses the hunter and ascends to Vaikuntha heaven, or in other scriptures to Goloka Vrindavan, the realm of cows where Mirabai hoped to go.
- The deck's reading is "cosmic justice": bad karma cannot be escaped, but doing one's duty is rewarded in the afterlife.

### Two kshatriya princes who renounced
- **Siddhartha Gautama** (about 500 BCE) founded Buddhism; he was of the Shakya clan and renounced the world at 29.
- **Vardhamana Mahavira**, his older contemporary, founded Jainism; he was of the Naya clan and renounced at 30.
- Both were born warriors and royal princes.
- Both held that killing produces so much bad karma that it is better to renounce the world and become an ascetic, a **shramana**.
- Siddhartha left the palace with his charioteer Channa after seeing sights of suffering, cut his hair, took off his princely clothes and became a wandering ascetic.

### The shramana movement and ascetic norms
- The shramana movement begins around the 6th century BCE, but the Vedas already show people renouncing the world to escape suffering.
- Rig Veda hymn 10.136 describes the long-haired **muni**, girdled with wind and wearing soil-coloured garments.
- Modern **sadhus** ("practitioners of austerities") descend from the Vedic munis.
- Cutting the hair symbolised cutting ties with the world.
- Yellow patched robes symbolised worn-out cloth fading in the sun.
- Mahavira went completely naked.

### World-affirming and world-renouncing in Hinduism
- The three goals of human life, the **purusharthas**, are dharma (piety, society, duty), artha (profit, success, domination) and kama (pleasure, sex, desire).
- The deck pictures them as a tripod in which no goal can be neglected, and asks whether the order is a ranking or a set of steps.
- The four stages of life, the **ashramas**, are brahmacarya, grihastha, vanaprastha and sannyasa.
  - Brahmacarya is youth: celibacy and learning dharma.
  - Grihastha is adulthood: householder, marriage and family.
  - Vanaprastha is middle age: retirement, "way to the forest".
  - Sannyasa is old age: forest dwelling, renunciation and moksha.
- The first three stages are world-affirming; only sannyasa is world-renouncing.
- Both founders said renunciation should start early, because human life is a rare chance to escape samsara and old age makes the effort hard.
- Siddhartha practised austerities for 6 years before enlightenment.
- Mahavira practised austerities for 12 years before omniscience.

### Why Hindus call Buddhism and Jainism heterodox
- Neither accepts the authority of the Vedas.
- Both hold that the Vedic gods are not free of suffering: they live long, happy lives but still die, and can be reborn lower once their good karma runs out.
- Neither has a creator god; the universe is eternal but cyclical, each cycle starting from previous karma.
- Both reject caste. The Buddha taught that anyone of proper spiritual conduct is a "brahmin", so the word does not name a caste.
- Mahavira is said to have been moved miraculously as a fetus from a brahmin woman's womb into a kshatriya woman's.
- Mahavira becomes a **Tirthankara**, "Ford Maker", the ford being a crossing out of the world of suffering.
- Siddhartha becomes the **Buddha**, "Awakened One", and attains nirvana.
- Both are called **Jina**, "Conqueror" of suffering.
- Mahavira names 23 earlier tirthankaras and the Buddha names 27 earlier Buddhas. The deck says this made each tradition older than the Vedas, and that there is no archaeological evidence for it.
- Both founded renunciant communities.

### Where Jainism and Buddhism differ
- Jains believe in a permanent self, the **jiva**, similar to the Vedic atman, which is enmeshed in karma as a substance.
- In Jain cosmology the liberated live on the top tier of the cosmos, above the Vedic gods.
- Buddhist nirvana is a state completely outside the world.
- Jainism takes asceticism to the extreme: giving up possessions, nakedness in some sects, and giving up sex, sleep and finally food until death, called **sallekhana** or **santhara**.
- The Jain premise is that complete non-violence toward the world frees one from karma.

### Asceticism as a category (Schmalz 2007)
- The word comes from the Greek **askesis**, "to exercise, train".
- Schmalz finds asceticism to some extent in all religions, including Christianity, Judaism, Islam, Hinduism and Buddhism.
- It means controlling, and ideally eliminating, self-indulgence, desire and sensual pleasure; celibacy is one kind.
- It is negative because it denies: avoidance, mortification, cutting or suppressing part of the self.
- It is positive because it produces something: virtue, purity, consciousness or power.
- The Sanskrit word for that power is **tapas**, "inner heat".
- Asceticism consecrates (makes one pure and dedicated), separates (sets one apart from ordinary people) and transforms or atones (changes one's nature for spiritual gain).
- Combined with other methods it produces altered states of consciousness.

### Why the Buddha's life is traditional lore
- Writing did not exist in South Asia until about 300 BCE.
- No rival tradition mentions Siddhartha Gautama, and no archaeological find proves he existed.
- Everything comes from the oral Buddhist tradition, written down several centuries after his death.
- The deck concludes that Buddhist teachings are collective memory that changes over time and gains depth as the tradition develops.

### Buddhism as the religion of numbers
- The early community used numbered lists to memorise teachings, passed them on orally, and later wrote them into scripture.
- Gautama is Buddha number 28, after 27 earlier Buddhas.
- The next Buddha is **Maitreya** (Pali Metteyya), number 29, now a god in Tushita Heaven; some sources say he comes in 5.67 billion years.
- Gautama's path began four asamkhyeyas and one hundred thousand kalpas ago. The deck gives one asamkhyeya as $$10^{140}$$ and a kalpa as the time from the creation of one universe to the next.
- He was then a brahmin ascetic named **Sumedha**, who heard Buddha number 4, **Dipankara**, and vowed to become a Buddha.
- A being with that aspiration is a **bodhisattva**.

### The ten perfections and the Jataka tales
- Ten of the bodhisattva's past lives show how he gained the ten virtues: generosity, morality, renunciation, wisdom, energy, patience, truthfulness, resolution, loving-kindness and equanimity.
- Stories of the past lives are the **Jataka** tales; the Pali canon has 547.
- The ten most important are the **dasajati jataka**, "Stories of Ten Rebirths", often painted on temple murals.
- **Vessantara Jataka** (generosity): Prince Vessantara gives away everything, including his wife and children. This is the YouTube reading.
- **Bhuridatta** (morality): the bodhisattva is a naga captured and made to perform by a snake charmer; his family finds him and the charmer releases him. The deck reads it as obedience and family values.
- **Temiya** (renunciation): the prince pretends to be deaf and mute to avoid becoming king, lifts the chariot of the charioteer sent to kill him, is let go, and becomes a forest ascetic.

### The twelve great deeds
- Before his last birth the bodhisattva is a god named **Shvetaketu** ("White Banner") in Tushita Heaven.
- Every Buddha performs the same **twelve great deeds**, in this order:
  1. Descent from Tushita Heaven.
  2. Entering his mother's womb.
  3. Miraculous birth in the garden.
  4. Training in the arts and sciences.
  5. Victory in sports competition.
  6. Enjoying the palace and marriage.
  7. Renunciation.
  8. Six years of ascetic practice, then giving it up.
  9. Victory over Mara.
  10. Enlightenment under the Bodhi Tree.
  11. Turning the Wheel of Dharma.
  12. Passing into parinirvana.
- Deed 1: remembering his vow before Dipankara, he gives up heavenly pleasure and chooses a human birth.
- Deed 2: **Queen Maya** dreams that a white elephant enters her side and conceives without intercourse. Maya means divine illusion, and the deck reads the human realm as a deeper illusion than the divine one.
- Deed 3: Maya gives birth standing and holding a tree branch, and he comes out of her side, which signifies freedom from passion.
- He takes seven steps and says that in the heavens and on earth only he is the holy one, which the deck reads as supremacy over the Vedic gods.
- His birthday, **Vesak**, falls on the full moon of the fourth lunar month, usually around May, and includes bathing the baby Buddha.
- Deed 4: a birth prophecy says he will be either a great monarch or a great renunciant. He masters the kshatriya arts: archery, wrestling, statecraft, poetry, music and mathematics.
- Deed 5: he wins sports competitions for the hand of Princess **Yashodhara**.
- Deed 6: his father, King **Shuddhodana** ("Pure Generosity"), fears he will renounce, so he surrounds him with pleasure and bans every sign of suffering from the palace.
- The primary source is the **Lalitavistara**: lalita means play or divine sport, and vistara means extensive.
