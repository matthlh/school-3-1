## Pure Land Buddhism

### Three problems in China and the Buddhist answers (slides 2 to 4, 10)
- When Buddhism reached China it met three problems.
  - Chinese culture had no concept of reincarnation.
  - **Confucians** attacked Buddhists as unfilial and asocial, because monks were celibate renunciants.
  - **Daoists** ridiculed them for cutting their hair, for not cultivating qi, and for disrespecting the immortals.
- Buddhists answered in three ways.
  - They downplayed human reincarnation and preached karma as leading to a next life of rewards (heavens) or punishments (hells and hungry ghosts).
  - They built rituals around Confucian ancestor veneration: freeing hungry ghosts (the **Maudgalyayana** story, the Obon and Ghost festivals) and worship of the bodhisattva **Kshitigarbha** for liberation from hell.
  - They preached immediate rebirth in a Buddha's **Pure Land**, escaping suffering altogether.
- Pure Land addresses what happens after death, a gap in the Confucian account.
- It offers an easier path than the Daoist pursuit of immortality through qi cultivation and appeals to immortals such as **Xiwangmu**.
- A person reborn there becomes an advanced bodhisattva and can bless their relatives. This answers the concern about filial piety.
- A pure land also resembles a Daoist paradise.

### Devotion and the idea of a Pure Land (slides 11 to 14)
- Mahayana has many forms of devotion (**bhakti**): supplicating and making offerings to a Buddha or bodhisattva in the hope that a higher power rescues you.
- Through visions of cosmic Buddhas in meditation, Mahayana monks decided that Buddhas who reach nirvana leave samsara (the negative side, as in early Buddhism) but also create mystical spaces outside it, the Pure Lands (the positive side, likening Buddhas to the creator gods of Hinduism).
- Unlike Hindu heavens, Pure Lands are free from suffering, and bodhisattvas are reborn there by the grace of a Buddha's powers.
- Why the idea arose: the Buddha would not describe nirvana beyond "no more rebirth", "the end of conditioned existence" and "absence of the three poisons", and since a body is the product of karma and the three poisons, nothing objective seemed to remain. Innovators held that the Buddha's mind and body dwell on in a pure form, as light, in a land of imperishable subtle substances described as shining jewels.
- The **Pure Land sutras** describe cosmic Buddhas and their lands. Visualizing the land and chanting the Buddha's name lets practitioners experience it in meditation or be born there after death.
- **Amitabha** is the cosmic Buddha shown welcoming the faithful to his Pure Land.
- Bodhisattvas born there are exclusively male, the sexist view of the female body shared with conservative Buddhism, so the Pure Land is **homosocial**.
- In a Pure Land you find its creator Buddha, visiting Buddhas from other worlds (reflections in the jewels of **Brahma's Net**), and cosmic bodhisattvas such as **Avalokiteshvara** (Chinese **Guanyin**, compassion, who helps anyone calling his name, to Amitabha's right) and **Mahasthamaprapta** (power). They can appear as apparitions in any world to guide the living and the dead.

### The Pure Land as a hotel (slides 16, 20)
- For ordinary beings a Pure Land is like a magical hotel: they check in to practise under the Buddha while resting from samsara, and check out when they are mature bodhisattvas ready to become Buddhas somewhere.
- While there, their previous karma, good and bad, is suspended. When they leave, it takes them somewhere in samsara again, but the good karma earned there usually gives a good rebirth, which mostly implies a male one.
- So the Pure Land path is **gradual**: you fall out of samsara temporarily, grow into a powerful bodhisattva slowly under a Buddha's guidance, then return to save others and eventually become a Buddha.

### Shinran and Shin Buddhism (slides 17 to 19)
- Pure Land Buddhism mostly keeps Theravada's gradualism and its view of female inferiority, yet it gives equal, even preferential, access to people supposedly burdened with bad karma, including women.
- **Shinran** (1173 to 1263), a Japanese monk, put it as a paradox: "Even if a good person can reach the Pure Land, how much more so an evil person!"
- His reasoning: Amitabha, whose Pure Land lies far to "the west", made so much merit that saying his name with faith even once guarantees rebirth there. Good people rely on their own effort and lack faith in Amitabha's grace; evil people (and, by the misogynist implication, women) admit their powerlessness and rely on his grace wholeheartedly.
- The ethical result: Shinran lived with the nun **Eshin-ni** and fathered six children with her.
- **Shin Buddhism**, the movement he started, keeps neither the Vinaya nor the bodhisattva precepts; it centres on gratitude and faith in Amitabha. It is now Japan's largest Buddhist denomination, with 22 million believers, and is sex-positive and non-celibate.

## Chan and Zen

### Non-gradual (subitist) Buddhism (slides 21 to 27)
- **Subitist** Buddhism holds that awakening, the direct experience of emptiness, happens in a flash (Latin *subito*). The path is direct mind-to-mind transmission of the experience from master to disciple, not step-by-step training in virtues: "a finger pointing to the moon".
- **Chan/Zen** is one example. All of Zen's teachings are the finger; once you see the moon (experience emptiness) you let go of the finger and carry on as you are, as monk, nun or lay person.
- Monastic discipline is one finger among many, so a lay person can be enlightened, the opposite of Theravada, where only monastics become arhats.
- "Zen is like a mandarin orange" (**Shigematsu Soiku**): it comes from China, it is a product of cultivation (rituals, practices, methods), and you will not know the taste until you try it, which is Zen's big claim.
- The word: Sanskrit *dhyana*, Chinese *Chan*, Korean *Seon*, Japanese *Zen*, Vietnamese *Thien*, all meaning meditation.
- Zen is **syncretic** (a combination of elements from different sources): Daoist distrust of verbal truths, the Confucian patriarchal system, and the Buddhist idea of awakening (*wu*).

### Paradox, words and awakening (slides 28 to 33)
- **Zhuangzi**'s butterfly dream: "Is it I who dreamed of being a butterfly, or the butterfly now dreaming of being me?" The paradox points to a reality words fall short of.
- Paradoxes are comic in spirit and temporarily suspend rational thinking so the world is experienced without borders, with subject and objects gone.
- What is wrong with words: the sign is not the thing (the finger is not the moon), and language is built on the subject-object duality that Buddhist practice aims to transcend.
- Zen uses paradox to jam rational thinking and cause a temporary "system failure" in dualistic seeing. The moment the intellect fails, the world is experienced "as it is", and this is **awakening** (Japanese *satori*, Chinese *wu*).
- **Hara Tanzan** (1819 to 1892) carried a woman across a muddy patch; his younger companion later criticized him for breaking the Vinaya rule against touching women. Tanzan: "I left the woman at the shore, while you are still carrying her." The younger monk awakened.
- A **koan** (Chinese *gong'an*, "public case", like a court hearing) is a dialogue between master and disciple that captures a moment of awakening. Koans are comic and paradoxical, and both monastics and lay people meditate on them, but in East Asia women masters never teach male students.

### Bodhidharma and the Zen lineage (slides 34 to 46)
- Zen is a **patriarchal** system. Its legendary founder **Bodhidharma** (Japanese *Daruma*) was an Indian monk, a Brahmin or a prince, who came to China in the 6th century CE.
- He met the Buddhist devotee **Emperor Wu** of Liang (464 to 549) and told him to doubt his achievements. Wu misunderstood and sent him off to **Shaolin**, a small temple in the middle of nowhere; he is said to have crossed the Yangtze on a reed. Hagiographies use the miraculous to legitimize the lineage.
- At Shaolin he sat facing a wall in a cave for nine years and his legs atrophied.
- **Huike** asked to be his disciple, was refused, and cut off his left arm to show his dedication. "Teacher, I want to free my mind." "Show me your mind and I will set it free." "I cannot find it anywhere." "Well then, I have freed your mind!" Huike was enlightened.
- Bodhidharma's four-line verse, the one poem to memorize in Zen:
  - A separate transmission outside scripture (*kyoge betsuden*).
  - Not founded on words and letters (*furyu monji*).
  - Point directly at the human mind (*jikishi ninshin*).
  - See your nature and become Buddha (*kensho jobutsu*).
- Words can express the subject but cannot exhaust it, as with explaining the taste of a mandarin; enlightenment is the taste.
- The Buddha held up a flower and **Mahakashyapa** smiled back: mind-to-mind transmission (*ishin denshin*).

### Monastic life and the unified experience (slides 47 to 57)
- In the monastery everyone does the same things in the same traditional pattern: begging, eating, meditating, chanting, sleeping, working.
- Does shared behaviour unify the thinking? Yes and no: members can have different motives for the same output, as with vegetarianism (not killing animals, disgust at raw meat, mad cow disease).
- A master guides disciples toward satori and can recognize their progress because he was once a novice. Bodily training (posture, movement) embodies the Confucian model and goes beyond words.
- Meditation: calm the mind, then mindfulness of the present moment, then the master gives each disciple a koan.
- A master's expression is his **kyogai**, literally "boundary": his inner state seen through his face, posture, voice and behaviour (the portrait of **Chikotsu Dai'e**, 13th century).
- Koan practice has three steps: think of the meaning and visualize the scene (duality); the doubt whether you can solve it intensifies (non-duality); become the koan, so the object is the doubting mind itself.
- "Strive to save yourself through practice, like a madman whose hair's caught fire!"
- Three stages: mountains are mountains (ignorance); mountains are no longer mountains (emptiness in meditation); mountains are mountains again (awakening, emptiness after meditation), shown with a landscape by **Fan Kuan** (about 960 to 1030).

### Rinzai and Soto, and the two meditations (slides 61 to 62)
- **Rinzai** (Chinese *Linji*) stresses meditating on koans; once a koan is "solved", the "theater of consciousness" arises naturally.
- **Soto** (Chinese *Caodong*) meditates on the theater of consciousness itself rather than on koans, and reads koans in temple study to check its experience. *Fancy Dance* shows this.
- The two types of meditation from lecture 6 return:
  - Progressive meditation leads to **compassion** through the emptiness of self and phenomena: concentrate, calm the mind, observe all sensations as magical apparitions in the theater of consciousness until subject-object duality disappears; when duality returns after meditation, you see others still enchanted by the magic show and wish to share what you know.
  - Conservative meditation leads to **detachment** through the aesthetic experience of impermanence: concentrate, calm the mind, scan sensations and mental states as they rise and pass away, and distance yourself equanimously from pleasant and unpleasant alike.

### Celibacy and antinomianism (slides 63 to 66)
- Japan abolished monastic celibacy at the end of the 19th century, so non-celibate clergy are not only Shin Buddhists.
- Korea, under Japanese rule from 1910 to 1945, also has married clerics, but after WWII most of Korean Buddhism returned to celibate monasticism. *Mandala* shows this.
- **Antinomianism** is the technical term for precept-breaking in Zen: masters sometimes break monastic precepts or act as if free from all laws, including the culture's moral standards, to cause awakening in students or in themselves. Many koans are antinomian ("a dog has no Buddha nature"); it works as shock therapy for people ready for it.
- **Linji** (Japanese *Rinzai*, died 866 CE) was famous for shock therapy: "If you meet the Buddha, kill the Buddha" (from the *Linjilu*, Japanese *Rinzairoku*, misspelled "Rinsairoku" in Mishima).
- Killing the Buddha would be the greatest sin, so the tradition adds a safety valve: kill the Buddha and the world is doomed; do not kill him and there is no salvation. The answer to "why kill the Buddha?" is "sit and meditate on it".
