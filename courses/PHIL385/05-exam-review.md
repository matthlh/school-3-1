# PHIL 385 — Exam 1 review (Fri Oct 2, 14:00, Buchanan A 201)

Two questions, 500 words each, 50 minutes, typed in Respondus. Each has an agree-or-disagree part. Scope from Kraal's Sep 26 note: the Preface, The Unhappiest One, Crop Rotation, Ancient Tragedy's Reflection in the Modern. A question may span all four or sit on one.

## The frame that ties the four together

- Every text is A speaking, the aesthete, not Kierkegaard. An essay that says "Kierkegaard argues…" about any of these is already wrong.
- Kraal's three-part recipe is the exam summary of A. On the aesthetic view, life satisfaction is best served by a pessimistic baseline (The Unhappiest One), by having no deep lifelong commitments (Crop Rotation), and by seeing your fortune or misfortune as beyond your control (Ancient Tragedy). The Preface is the frame that lets A say this without a verdict.
- "Aesthetic" has two senses. The narrow one is the artistic category: what counts as beautiful, tragic, comic, interesting. The wide one is the aesthetic life-view: living for immediacy, moods and the interesting, judging a life by what it is like to experience rather than by duty or God.
- Kraal's distinction: A does not promise happiness, he promises never being bored and never being trapped. Satisfaction is not happiness.
- The standing objection you can use on any question is Judge William's, from volume 2. Commitment is what lets first love last, a life of moods has no self, and the ethical is the more beautiful life. Your own note from lecture 7, that there is meaning in the struggle and an exit at the first difficulty is a slippery slope, is the Judge almost word for word.

## Preface (Victor Eremita)

### What it claims
- It opens by doubting Hegel's formula that the outer is the inner and the inner the outer. People carry secrets their outside never shows.
- The desk story acts the doubt out. A desk Eremita knew from outside had a hidden compartment he never suspected, sprung by a hatchet blow in 1836.
- Hearing became his dearest sense because the voice reveals an inwardness the eye cannot measure. The confessor behind the grille hears only, so no contradiction arises.
- A's papers are aesthetic essays on vellum. B's are letters to A, ethical, on legal-style paper. A is a name Eremita invented. B is Wilhelm, a court assessor, hence Judge William.
- The title is Either/Or: A Fragment of Life, a jab at Hegel's complete system.
- The aim: when the book has been read, A and B are forgotten and only the life-views stand facing each other, expecting no decision in particular persons. Eremita calls it good fortune that nothing is settled, because in ordinary novels one character converts the other and the reader is handed a result instead of a view.
- He could not give up the thought that A and B might be one man who went through both movements, and that gave him the title.

### Likely agree-or-disagree
- Is it a strength or an evasion that the book refuses to decide between A and B?
- Does the outer reveal the inner, or is Eremita right that it does not?

### Trap
- Do not write "there is no hard truth" or "balance the two views." The book withholds a verdict so you have to make one, and B argues that balancing is exactly the evasion. The title is Either/Or, not Both/And.

## The Unhappiest One

### What it claims
- A grave in England inscribed "the unhappiest one" was opened and found empty. The real source is the Miserrimus slab in Worcester Cathedral. The empty grave and the stone rolled away echo Mark 16.
- The speaker is A addressing the Symparanekromenoi, the fellowship of the already dead, at a Friday meeting. Anyone who counts death the greatest misfortune is shut out. The fellowship knows a worse misfortune, living. The man who cannot die (the Wandering Jew) is raised and rejected. Death is the common good fortune of all. The title goes to a class, not a person.
- Definition to memorise: the unhappy one has his ideal, his life's content, outside himself. He is always absent from himself, never present to himself. You can only be absent in the past or the future. A takes only the boundary from Hegel's unhappy consciousness, not its content.
- Grammar: the pluperfect and the future perfect have no present in them. Pluperfect is the rememberer, future perfect the hoper.
- Merely hoping or remembering is not strict unhappiness while you are present in the hope or memory. So one blow can never make the unhappiest. Job lost everything but not at one blow, and he had possessed it.
- The hoper becomes strictly unhappy when he hopes for what he knows cannot be realised. The rememberer, when he remembers a past that never had reality for him (the teacher who never had a childhood).
- Memory is the proper element of the unhappy. The unhappiest is found among the rememberers, because hope's disappointments are the more cheerful kind.
- The combination: he hopes for what should be remembered and remembers what should be hoped. His life is back-to-front in two directions. He came into the world too soon and so always arrives too late. Kraal's illustration is Gatsby.
- The gallery, in order: a jilted girl, Niobe, Antigone, Job, the prodigal's father (the only hoper), a lapsed martyr whose name is forgotten, a second girl who cannot grieve or hope and gets the accessit, then the nameless winner, "the unhappy lover of memory." Everyone who can still grieve is eliminated, because grief proves a real past.
- The ending: A reverses the title to "the happiest," because this is a gift of fortune nobody can give himself and the world cannot take away. Then the four clauses: life is madness, faith is folly, hope is a reprieve (literally a gallows stay), love is salt or vinegar in the wound. The day starts again. No cure is offered.
- Kraal's three words for the ending: doxology, paradox, comfort. In A's own vocabulary the words are "the happiest," "gift of fortune," "envy."

### Likely agree-or-disagree
- Is the unhappiest person really the one who is absent from himself, rather than the one who suffers most?
- Is memory worse than hope?
- Can there be comfort in being the unhappiest?

### Your objection, already on file
- A defines unhappiness as absence and never argues for it. Define it as suffering and Job wins. The essay has no answer to that.
- The insight underneath survives: an ambiguous loss is worse than a clear one, which is why the second girl outranks Niobe.

### Traps
- "The unhappiest one is the one who cannot die" is raised and thrown away.
- Regret is not the definition. A regretter remembers a real past and is present in it.
- Tolstoy's On Life is Kraal's comparison, a convergence, not an influence. Tolstoy has a cure, A does not.

## Crop Rotation

### What it claims
- Full title: "Crop Rotation. An attempt at a theory of social prudence." Epigraph from Aristophanes' Plutus: one finally gets sick of everything. Satiety is the note.
- Principle: all people are boring. Boredom is a root of all evil (the Danish says "a," not "the"). Proof from children: amused they are good, bored they turn unruly.
- History of the world as boredom: the gods were bored and made humans, Adam was bored alone, Babel was built for amusement, Rome was held off by bread and circuses.
- Man is called a social animal but is at bottom a beast of prey. Talk of society is inherited hypocrisy.
- Two classes: those who bore others are the plebs, those who bore themselves are the elect. The busy are the most boring.
- Idleness is not the problem. Work annuls idleness but cannot touch boredom. Idleness is divine as long as one is not bored. Man's destiny is to amuse himself, so boredom is opposed to amusement, not to work. Boredom is "the demonic pantheism," built on the nothing that winds through existence.
- Two kinds of rotation. The vulgar kind changes the soil: new city, new country, America, star to star. A calls it Hegel's bad infinity (Hannay p. 232) and says it cancels itself. The true kind keeps the soil and changes the method. Here lies the principle of limitation, the only saving principle in the world (p. 233). The prisoner with the spider. Intensive against extensive.
- Remembering and forgetting (p. 233): first throw hope overboard, since as long as one hopes one cannot limit oneself. Nil admirari. Never enjoy to the last drop. Forgetting must apply to the pleasant too, because the pleasant, once past, carries the unpleasantness of longing. Forgetting is the scissors under the supervision of memory.
- The three things to avoid, each because it makes you "several" and costs your freedom. Friendship: the superfluous third, always keep more speed than the relationship. Marriage: promising forever is easy and means nothing, and woman is man's ruin once a lasting tie is contracted. An official post: you become a Peter Somebody, a peg in the state machine, with a title you can never shed.
- Arbitrariness (pp. 239 to 240): "the whole secret lies in arbitrariness." Enjoy not the thing but what you arbitrarily read into it. The sweating lecturer. Treat all of life as a wager. Keep changing the eye you see with.

### Likely agree-or-disagree
- Is boredom really the deepest problem of a life?
- Is self-limitation plus arbitrariness a liveable answer, or does it collapse into the bad infinity it attacks?
- Should every relationship keep an exit door?

### Objections ready to use
- The Judge: a life with no commitments has no self, only a sequence of moods, and depends on the world to supply the next one. Your own line: there is meaning in the struggle.
- Internal: A's cure is still rotation, just faster and more inward. The Symparanekromenoi are the end state of this programme, people who experienced everything and count themselves dead. Crop Rotation is where the aesthetic life-view first shows itself failing.

### Traps
- Idleness is not a remedy, it is the neutral state. The remedy is amusement.
- A never embraces boredom or routine. Habit is what he blames.
- "If you truly loved them you would break it off" is not A. The break is self-preservation, not a gift.
- Kraal's "three arts" is his tidy framing. A does not number them.

## Ancient Tragedy's Reflection in the Modern

### What it claims
- Ancient and modern tragedy differ in where the downfall comes from. Ancient: the hero rests in substantial ties, state, family, fate, so action is "as much event as action," with chorus and epic foreground. Modern: the hero is reflected out of every immediate tie and "stands and falls entirely on his own deeds" (p. 143).
- Tragic guilt lies between two extremes. No guilt and the collision goes slack. Absolute guilt and the hero is simply bad: aesthetic guilt has become ethical guilt, and evil has no aesthetic interest. Sin is not an aesthetic element.
- The modern age loads the hero's whole life onto his shoulders as his own deed and will not hear of an unhappy childhood as an excuse. "As the age loses the tragic, it gains despair." The courage to be one's own creator is an illusion.
- Absolute and relative: every individual is the child of God, his age, his people, his family. If inside all that relativity it wants to be the absolute, it is comic. If it accepts being relative, it has the tragic, and "the individual is only happy when it has the tragic." This is about guilt and belonging, not about objective versus subjective knowledge.
- The healing in fate (p. 144): there is "a melancholy and a healing" in the tragic. Aesthetically the tragic is to life what divine grace and mercy are, a motherly love. The ethical is strict and hard. The religious is a fatherly love. The healing is that guilt is shared and inherited, so the individual is not left alone as sole author. Not blame-shifting.
- Guilt (p. 147): ancient guilt is aesthetic, inherited through piety, guilt and yet not guilt, like original sin. Modern guilt is ethical: the isolated individual is his own creator, his guilt is sin, his pain is repentance, and the tragic is cancelled. The spectator's response becomes "help yourself," which is the loss of compassion, and compassion is the real expression of the tragic. A finds modern tragedy aesthetically deficient exactly to the extent that it is ethically dominated.
- Sorrow against pain: ancient tragedy has deeper sorrow and less pain. Modern has more pain and less sorrow. Sorrow has something substantial in it. Pain is reflection on suffering. The image is a child watching an adult suffer. The bitterest pain is repentance, which has ethical, not aesthetic, reality.
- Antigone is the principal example because the house of Oedipus carries inherited guilt. Taken as an isolated clash of sisterly love and a king's ban she would be a modern subject. "Not an individual but a little world goes under." Hamlet is tragic because he suspects his mother's crime, and anxiety belongs to the modern tragic. The Faust in the essay is Grabbe's, A's example of a work built on evil.
- A's own modern Antigone: everything as in Sophocles, except the secret of Oedipus is known only to her. Anxiety is her organ. She falls mortally in love, the beloved presses for the secret, and she can only confess her love in the moment she dies. She is sorrow's bride, and the figure who in The Unhappiest One has turned her back on hope.
- Christ: A says his appearance is in one sense the deepest tragedy, and that his life is never called a tragedy because absolute innocence and absolute guilt together exceed aesthetic categories. "The crucifixion is the turning point from Greek to modern" is Kraal's reading, not A's sentence.
- Kraal's forward link: Nietzsche's amor fati, 1882, forty years later, a parallel not a debt.

### Likely agree-or-disagree
- Does the modern self-created individual lose something the ancients had?
- Is there healing in seeing your fate as beyond your control, or is that an excuse?
- Is modern tragedy aesthetically deficient because it is ethically dominated?

### Objection ready to use
- The Judge again, and the ethical generally: taking responsibility for your own deeds is the condition of having a self at all. A's "healing" is purchased by giving up authorship.
- Turn A's own line back on him: a view that calls despair the price of self-creation has already conceded that the modern self is in despair, which is where volume 2 starts.

### Traps
- Sorrow is not regret. The dictionary pairs them. In A they are opposite sides. Sorrow goes with fate and innocence. Pain and repentance go with reflection and guilt.
- "Absurd" is your friend's word. A's is comic or ridiculous.
- Modern tragedy is a tendency A diagnoses, not a verdict that the protagonist caused everything.

## How the four fit one essay question

If a question spans all four, the spine is this. The Preface sets up a book that refuses to decide between two lives. The three essays are A's case for the aesthetic life: find your satisfaction in a pessimistic baseline, refuse ties that make you several, and rest in a fate you did not author. The Judge's reply, which you are allowed to use as your own view, is that each of these buys relief by giving up a self.

Agree or disagree, but name the view, name the essay it comes from, give A's strongest line, give the objection, and answer it in your own voice. Kraal is marking the evaluation as much as the facts.

## Essay shape for 500 words in 25 minutes

1. Position in one sentence, naming the text and A as the speaker.
2. A's claim in his own terms, with one quoted phrase and the page if you have it.
3. Your argument, numbered, two or three points.
4. The strongest objection, usually the Judge's.
5. Your answer to it, and a closing sentence that restates the position.
