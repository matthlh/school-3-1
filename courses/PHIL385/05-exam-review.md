# PHIL 385 — Exam 1 review (Fri Oct 2, 14:00, Buchanan A 201)

Two questions, 500 words each, 50 minutes, typed in Respondus. Each question asks whether you agree or disagree with a central idea. Texts covered: the Preface, The Unhappiest One, Crop Rotation, Ancient Tragedy's Reflection in the Modern. A question can cover all four or just one.

## Things that apply to all four texts

- All four texts are written by "A", a made-up character. Kierkegaard wrote them, but the views are A's, not Kierkegaard's. If you write "Kierkegaard argues…" you lose marks.
- A lives what the course calls the aesthetic life. Plain meaning: he judges life by what it feels like to experience, not by duty or religion. He wants interesting experiences and moods, and he avoids anything that ties him down.
- Kraal summarised A's view in three parts, one per essay. To be satisfied with life: expect the worst (The Unhappiest One), avoid lifelong commitments (Crop Rotation), and treat your good or bad luck as outside your control (Ancient Tragedy).
- Kraal's point: A does not promise happiness. He promises you will not be bored and not be trapped.
- The main objection you can use against A in any answer comes from "B", Judge William, who writes the second half of the book. B says: committing to things is what makes love last, a life of moods has no real self, and the committed life is the more beautiful one. Your own view from lecture 7, that struggle has meaning and leaving at the first difficulty is a slippery slope, is the same point.

## Preface (written by "Victor Eremita", the editor)

### The facts
- The Preface starts by doubting Hegel's claim that the inside of a person matches the outside. Eremita says people keep secrets their outside never shows.
- The desk story makes the point. Eremita bought an old writing desk, hit it with a hatchet when a drawer stuck, and a hidden compartment full of papers popped out. He knew the outside of the desk and had no idea about the inside.
- He says hearing became his favourite sense, because a voice shows what is inside a person better than looks do.
- The papers came from two unknown men. A's papers are essays about art and life. B's papers are letters to A about ethics. Eremita invented the name A. He learned from the letters that B was called Wilhelm and was a judge, so B is called Judge William.
- The full title is Either/Or: A Fragment of Life. "Fragment" is a dig at Hegel, who claimed to have a complete system.
- Eremita says the book settles nothing on purpose. Once you have read it, you forget A and B and are left with two views of life facing each other. He calls this good luck, because in a normal novel one character wins and the reader is handed the answer. Here you have to decide.
- He also says he could not drop the idea that A and B might be one person who lived both views, and that idea gave him the title.

### Likely question
- Is it a strength or a cop-out that the book refuses to say which view is right?
- Is Eremita right that a person's outside does not show their inside?

### Mistake to avoid
- Do not write "there is no real truth" or "the answer is to balance both views". The book refuses to decide so that you have to. B says balancing the two is exactly the way to dodge the choice. The title is Either/Or, not Both/And.

## The Unhappiest One

### The facts
- A grave in England is marked "the unhappiest one". It was opened and no body was inside. The real source is a grave slab in Worcester Cathedral marked Miserrimus. The empty grave is a deliberate echo of Christ's empty tomb.
- A is giving a speech to a club he invented called the Symparanekromenoi, people who count themselves as already dead. They meet on Fridays.
- Rules of the contest for the grave: anyone who thinks death is the worst thing is excluded. The club thinks living is worse than dying. A considers "the man who cannot die" and rejects him, because everyone dies. The title goes to a type of person, not one individual.
- The definition to memorise: the unhappy person has the content of his life outside himself. He is always absent from himself, never present to himself.
- You can only be absent in two directions: the past or the future. So there are two kinds of unhappy person, the one living in memory and the one living in hope. A borrows the phrase "unhappy consciousness" from Hegel but only the label, not Hegel's meaning.
- Grammar example: the pluperfect tense ("had done") has no present in it, and neither does the future perfect ("will have done"). Those are the rememberer and the hoper.
- Just hoping or just remembering is not enough to be unhappy in A's strict sense, as long as you are really living in that hope or memory. So one disaster, however bad, cannot make you the unhappiest. Job lost everything but not all at once, and he had really had it.
- The hoper is strictly unhappy when he hopes for something he knows cannot happen. The rememberer is strictly unhappy when he remembers a past he never actually had. A's example: a man who never really had a childhood, becomes a teacher, sees what childhood is, and spends his life looking back at one he never had.
- Memory is worse than hope. A says the unhappiest is always a rememberer, because disappointed hope is the more cheerful kind of disappointment.
- The worst case combines both. He hopes for things that are already in the past and remembers things that are still ahead. His life points the wrong way in both directions. A's phrase: he came into the world too soon and so always arrives too late. Kraal's example is Gatsby, who wants a future that is really a repeat of 1917.
- The candidates, in order: a girl whose lover cheated, Niobe, Antigone, Job, the father of the prodigal son (the only hoper), a forgotten man who failed at martyrdom, a second girl who cannot grieve or hope and gets second prize, and then the winner, who has no name and is called "the unhappy lover of memory". Everyone who can still grieve is eliminated, because grief proves you really had something.
- The ending: A corrects himself and calls the winner the happiest, because being the unhappiest is a prize the world can never take from you. Then four lines: life is madness, faith is folly, hope is a reprieve (literally a stay of execution), love is salt in the wound. The speech ends with the day starting again. No cure is offered.
- Kraal's three words for the ending: doxology (a hymn of praise), paradox, comfort. A's own words are "the happiest", "gift of fortune", and "envy".

### Likely question
- Is the unhappiest person really the one who is absent from his own life, rather than the one who suffers most?
- Is memory really worse than hope?
- Can there be comfort in being the unhappiest?

### Objection you can use
- A defines unhappiness as being absent from yourself and never argues for that definition. If you define unhappiness as suffering instead, Job wins. The essay has no answer to this.
- What survives the objection: a loss you cannot make sense of is worse than a clear loss. That is why the second girl, who does not know if she was deceived, ranks above Niobe, who lost twelve children but knows it.

### Mistakes to avoid
- "The unhappiest one is the one who cannot die" is wrong. A brings it up and throws it out.
- Regret is not the definition. Someone who regrets a real past is living in a real memory, so by A's rule he is not the unhappiest.
- Tolstoy's On Life is Kraal's comparison. Tolstoy wrote thirty years later and never read A. Tolstoy offers a cure, A does not.

## Crop Rotation

### The facts
- Full title: "Crop Rotation. An attempt at a theory of social prudence." It opens with a quote from Aristophanes' play Plutus: one eventually gets sick of everything.
- Starting claim: all people are boring. Boredom is a root of all evil. Proof from children: while they are amused they behave, and they act up as soon as they get bored.
- A retells history as boredom: the gods were bored so they made people, Adam was bored alone, the Tower of Babel was built for something to do, Rome was kept going by bread and circuses.
- People are called social animals. A says they are really predators and that talk of community is hypocrisy.
- Two kinds of boring people: those who bore others (the crowd) and those who bore themselves (the few). Busy people are the most boring of all.
- Idleness is not the problem. Work cures idleness but does not cure boredom. Doing nothing is fine, even divine, as long as you are not bored. The opposite of boredom is amusement, not work.
- Two ways to deal with boredom. The bad way is to change your surroundings: new city, new country, America, then the stars. A calls this the bad infinity, Hegel's term for a series that never ends (Hannay p. 232). The good way is to stay where you are and change how you use it. The key rule is self-limitation: the fewer options you have, the more inventive you become (p. 233). A's example is a prisoner who finds a spider endlessly entertaining.
- Remembering and forgetting (p. 233): first give up hope, because as long as you hope you cannot limit yourself. Never enjoy anything to the last drop. Learn to forget the good things too, because a good memory makes you long for it. Forgetting is like scissors that cut away what you cannot use.
- Three things to avoid, because each one ties you to other people and costs your freedom. Friendship: keep enough distance that you can always leave. Marriage: promising love forever is easy and means nothing, and a wife ruins a man. A job with a title: you become a small cog in the state and can never shed the title.
- The whole secret is arbitrariness (pp. 239–240): enjoy not the thing itself but whatever you decide to notice about it. A's example is listening to a boring lecturer and watching the sweat drip off his nose. Treat life as a bet. Keep changing how you look at things.

### Likely question
- Is boredom really the deepest problem in life?
- Is A's method (limit yourself, forget on purpose, be arbitrary) something a person could actually live by?
- Should every relationship have an exit?

### Objections you can use
- B's answer: a life with no commitments has no self, just a string of moods, and depends on the outside world to deliver the next one. Your own line: there is meaning in struggle.
- From inside A's own writing: his method is still a kind of rotation, just faster and more in your head. The club in The Unhappiest One is what this life ends up as: people who have tried everything and now count themselves as dead. This essay is where A's own view starts to break down.

### Mistakes to avoid
- Idleness is not the cure. It is the neutral state. The cure is amusement.
- A never says to accept boredom or settle into routine. Habit is what he blames.
- "If you truly loved them you would break it off" is not A's reason. A breaks things off to protect his own freedom, not for the other person's good.
- Kraal's "three arts" (limitation, forgetting, arbitrariness) is Kraal's tidy version. A does not number them.

## Ancient Tragedy's Reflection in the Modern

### The facts
- A says ancient and modern tragedy differ in where the hero's downfall comes from. In ancient (Greek) tragedy the hero is held by ties to family, state and fate, so what happens to him is partly something done to him, not only something he did. In modern tragedy the hero is cut off from those ties and "stands and falls entirely on his own deeds" (p. 143).
- Tragic guilt has to sit between two extremes. If the hero is not guilty at all, there is no tragedy. If he is completely guilty, he is just a bad person, and A says evil is not interesting as art. Sin does not belong in tragedy.
- The modern age puts a person's whole life on his own shoulders and will not accept an unhappy childhood as an excuse. A's line: "as the age loses the tragic, it gains despair." The belief that you can create yourself is an illusion.
- Absolute and relative: everyone is the child of God, their time, their country and their family. If you claim to be completely self-made inside all of that, you are ridiculous. If you accept that you are shaped by it, you have the tragic, and A says you are only happy when you have the tragic. This passage is about blame and belonging, not about objective versus subjective truth.
- The healing in fate (p. 144): A says there is "a melancholy and a healing" in the tragic. For him the tragic does for life what God's grace does in religion: it is like a mother's comfort. The ethical life is strict and hard. The religious life is like a father's love. The healing is that guilt is shared with your family and your time, so you are not left alone with it. This is not simply blaming someone else.
- Guilt (p. 147): Greek guilt is "aesthetic", meaning it is inherited and not fully your fault, like original sin. Modern guilt is "ethical", meaning the isolated person made himself, so his guilt is sin and his pain is regret. At that point the tragic disappears. The audience's reaction becomes "help yourself", and compassion is lost. Compassion is what tragedy is really about. A's verdict: modern tragedy is worse as art exactly as far as it is dominated by ethics.
- Sorrow versus pain: Greek tragedy has deep sorrow and little pain. Modern tragedy has more pain and less sorrow. Sorrow is deep and unreflective. Pain is thinking about your suffering and your part in it. A's picture: a child watching an adult suffer feels sorrow without pain. The worst pain is regret, and regret belongs to ethics, not art.
- Antigone is A's main example of ancient tragedy because she carries the inherited guilt of Oedipus's family. If you took only her act of burying her brother against the king's order, that would be a modern story. What makes it Greek is that the whole family's fate is in it: "not an individual but a little world goes under."
- Hamlet is modern because he suspects his mother, and suspicion and anxiety are modern. The Faust A mentions is Grabbe's play, not Goethe's. A uses it as an example of a work built on pure evil.
- A then invents his own modern Antigone. Same story, except only she knows Oedipus's secret. Anxiety replaces sorrow. She falls in love, her lover presses her for the secret, and she can only admit she loves him at the moment she dies. A calls her "sorrow's bride". She is the Antigone of The Unhappiest One, who gave up hope and lives on memory.
- Christ: A says Christ's coming is in one sense the deepest tragedy, but his life is never called a tragedy, because being completely innocent and completely guilty at once goes beyond art. "The crucifixion is the turning point from Greek to modern tragedy" is Kraal's reading, not a sentence in the text.
- Kraal's link forward: Nietzsche's amor fati (love of fate), 1882, forty years later. A parallel, not an influence.

### Likely question
- Does the modern self-made individual lose something the Greeks had?
- Is there real comfort in seeing your fate as outside your control, or is that just an excuse?
- Is modern tragedy worse as art because it is about personal responsibility?

### Objection you can use
- B's side, and the ethical view in general: taking responsibility for your own actions is what it means to have a self. A's "healing" is bought by giving up ownership of your life.
- Use A's own line against him. If self-creation brings despair, A has already admitted the modern person is in despair. That is exactly where B's half of the book starts.

### Mistakes to avoid
- Sorrow is not regret. The dictionary says they are synonyms. For A they are opposites: sorrow goes with fate and innocence, regret goes with responsibility and guilt.
- "Absurd" is not A's word. A says ridiculous or comic.
- A says modern tragedy tends to make the hero fully responsible. He does not say every modern hero caused everything.

## If one question covers all four texts

The Preface sets up a book that refuses to pick between two ways of living. The three essays are A's case for his way: expect the worst, avoid commitments, and treat your fate as outside your control. B's reply, which you can use as your own view, is that each of these gets you relief by giving up having a self.

Whichever side you take, do these five things: name the view, name the essay it comes from, quote A's strongest line, give the objection, and answer the objection in your own words. Kraal marks the evaluation as much as the facts.

## Shape of a 500-word answer in 25 minutes

1. One sentence saying whether you agree, naming the text and A as the speaker.
2. A's claim in his own words, with one short quote and the page if you know it.
3. Your reasons, numbered, two or three.
4. The strongest objection, usually B's.
5. Your reply to it, and one closing sentence that restates your position.
