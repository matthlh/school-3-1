## Stages on Life's Way: the 70,000 fathoms

- In this book the religious is articulated in terms of uncertainty or risk. Kraal's board line: "The religious is now articulated in terms of uncertainty or risk. The 70,000 fathoms."
- The image is swimming in an ocean with 70,000 fathoms of water underneath you. "The 70,000 fathoms" became a stock phrase.
- The passage he read asks "whether one has not become joyful in the wrong place". The right place, from the point of view of the religious, is in danger.
- "To be joyful out on 70,000 fathoms of water, many, many miles from all human help" is something great. "To swim in the shallows in the company of waders is not the religious."
- A person who wants certain ground and will not move beyond reason is in the shallow water. The religious water is out in the ocean, where you go by just a word.
- The danger comes from trust. Wherever there is trust there are a thousand possibilities of going wrong.
- Kraal's examples, one per religion:
  - A Christian has the word of Jesus, but only by also trusting the followers who said it was his word. Those are layers of trust, and humans can deceive.
  - A Muslim trusts that Muhammad received revelations in the cave of Hira without having been there, and trusts that Sahih al-Bukhari in the 800s set aside the unreliable hadiths, about 98%, and kept the reliable 2%.
  - Someone who builds a philosophy of life on Vedantic Hinduism has no proof of the Mahabharata.
  - A Jew trusts that Moses received the Torah from God, and that Moses wrote it at all.
- So the Christian, the Muslim, the Hindu and the Jew all make an act of trust, an act of faith. Someone who thinks a religious worldview can be demonstrated like a theorem in Euclid's geometry is mistaken.
- The *Postscript* was to be the defence of this position. In the setting of Christian apologetics it goes through the attempts to give Christian doctrine a rational basis and shows that the most they yield is probability.
- One cannot go from probability to certainty. Since the religious demands everything of a soul, probability is not enough, so there has to be an act of faith. That is the 70,000 fathoms.
- The passage continues: the one who loves the swimmer more than his own life can do no more for him, and has one thing left. He can shout to the beloved, "If you do not become happy now, then know that it is your own fault."
- Kraal's gloss: the only religious message that meets you out there is that it will go very badly for you if you do not go by the promise.
- In the second authorship the prime example is the forgiveness of sins. You cannot know your sins are forgiven in the sense of a proof, but if you do not believe it you perish. The one life ring you are given is something you can only believe in.

## The Concept of Anxiety

- The Danish word is angst, and because of this book it became a standard word in English too. Angst is a little different from the everyday English "anxiety", which is one reason it is often left in Danish.
- The book is about the experience of sin and of falling into sin, and the angst that results. It has a theological framework.
- It is also a gesture towards the religious, as all the books after *Fear and Trembling* are.
- Despite the theology it is one of the most popular Kierkegaard books among psychologists. Kraal named Jung as a great fan.

## Prefaces

- The book is a series of prefaces, eight or nine by Kraal's count, with nothing after them. The whole book is prefaces.
- Its pseudonymous author is Nicolaus Notabene.
- It takes issue with an attitude opposed to the religious one: that modern times have superseded the earlier, religious eras of the human spirit. "But today we know better." First the age of religion, then the age of reason, and we have outgrown the old ways.
- Kierkegaard ties this to certain branches of Hegelianism, which thought they could explain and evaluate whole epochs of human history as if from a God's-eye view.
- The same target is in *Either/Or*. At Hannay p. 487 B speaks of the distasteful sight of young men who can mediate Christianity and paganism and play with the titanic forces of history, yet cannot tell a plain man what he is to do in life.
- It is in *Fear and Trembling* as well. In matters of the soul one cannot rely on earlier generations: "Whatever one generation learns from another, it can never learn from a predecessor the genuine human factor. In this respect, every generation begins afresh."
- That book closes on "one must go further", with a warning. A disciple of Heraclitus wanted to go beyond "you can never go down in the same river twice" by saying you cannot move at all, and so negated Heraclitus's insight altogether.
- The lesson: to go beyond love is to negate love, and the same holds for faith. Against the Hegelians: if you think faith must give way to knowledge, as Hegel says in the preface to the *Phenomenology of Spirit*, you have not understood faith.
- The passage Kraal printed from *Prefaces*: with the help of mediation, the Hegelian synthesis of world history, everyone today gains absolute significance and "becomes the endpoint" to which all past world history tends.
- Notabene says this attitude must collapse if the religious attitude is to emerge. His reader must agree that although science in our time is finished with everything, it has "forgotten the point of it all".
- The point is not to deny scientific progress. Even a finished physics leaves a question it cannot answer: what is the point of it all? That is the religious question. If you think it a silly or nonsensical question, you cannot enter the religious.
- Kraal quoted Wittgenstein here: "To believe in God is to believe that life has meaning."
- Kraal's board line: "Only by doubting the idea that current culture is 'the endpoint' can you follow him into the religious." The religious is out of fashion, not the zeitgeist, and few philosophers made as much of that as Kierkegaard.

## The Crumbs and the Postscript: truth is subjectivity

- Parallel with *Prefaces* came the major project of the end of the authorship: the *Philosophical Crumbs* (also translated *Fragments*), about 100 pages, and its appendix, the *Concluding Unscientific Postscript*, about 800 difficult pages.
- The *Postscript* is aptly named: it is the text that concludes the authorship.
- It takes the uncertainty of the 70,000 fathoms, which had characterized the religious domain, and universalizes it. This is the human condition. There is no objectivity; there is subjectivity everywhere. Truth is subjectivity.
- The defence of that claim is why many call Kierkegaard the first postmodernist. Kraal named Lyotard, Derrida and Foucault, said postmodernism held Kierkegaard in high esteem, and said one of Derrida's last books was a play on *Fear and Trembling*.
- As in postmodernism, the defence comes from the nature of language. Kraal gave it in a nutshell and pointed to PHIL 316 next term, which is on this book.
  1. Reality is concrete. Everything in it is particular.
  2. Language and thought are abstract and general.
  3. Truth, in the traditional sense Kierkegaard accepts, is agreement or correspondence between thought and reality.
  4. Something general cannot fully express something particular. A statement expresses reality only approximately, through general categories, which are human categories and so categories of human subjectivity.
  5. Approximation means probability, and probability means choice: you conceptualize the thing one way and could as well have chosen another. So truth is subjectivity.
- His first example: "It's raining outside" applies equally truly to a trillion different rains, in Vancouver or Saudi Arabia, so it does not adequately describe the rain outside now.
- His second: "He's a good guy" would describe a thousand other people, while you are a particular thing with nothing else like you. The phrase can only gesture towards you.
- The claim holds across the board, in natural science as well as ethics, because the universe consists of particular facts, not general facts.
- Many consider the *Postscript* the most influential of the eight books in philosophy.

## The Crumbs: two possibilities

- The *Crumbs* sets two situations side by side.
- First: you have in yourself the ability to find the truth about life and ethics, because you have reason. We have "the condition of truth" in us. Much of Western philosophy assumes this; Plato and Aristotle do.
- Second, the one associated with the religious: I do not have the condition of truth. I cannot find the truth with my own intellectual resources.
- Even Plato, the arch-rationalist, occasionally says he doubts whether the divine spark in him is not a monster instead, a many-headed monster. The *Crumbs* makes much of that doubt, because it shows the second possibility cannot be refuted.
- If you lack the condition of truth, it has to come to you from without. So to find the truth you need a saviour. On Plato's hypothesis you do not.
- Philosophy cannot tell you which situation is yours. The recording cuts off here, at "Philosophy is predicated".

## Clarifications

### Names the recording garbled
- The transcript's "Nicolas Notetaker" is Nicolaus Notabene, the pseudonym of *Prefaces*. Nota bene is Latin for "note well".
- The transcript's "Westfall" is most likely Merold Westphal, an American Kierkegaard scholar who writes on Kierkegaard and postmodernism. He is not one of the French postmodernists.
- The Derrida book is *The Gift of Death* (1992), which reads *Fear and Trembling*. Derrida died in 2004, so it is a late book rather than one of his very last.

### Who speaks in each book, and the order
- The 70,000 fathoms passage is by Frater Taciturnus, in the last part of *Stages on Life's Way* (1845). The book as a whole is edited by Hilarius Bookbinder.
- *The Concept of Anxiety* (1844) is by Vigilius Haufniensis. The *Crumbs* (1844) and the *Postscript* (1846) are both by Johannes Climacus.
- Kraal's order today was not the order of publication. The *Crumbs* came out on 13 June 1844, four days before *Anxiety* and *Prefaces*, which appeared on the same day. *Stages* is 1845 and the *Postscript* 1846.
- *Prefaces* has eight numbered prefaces, with a preface of its own in front of them, which is why the count wavers between eight and nine.

### The numbers
- A fathom is six feet, about 1.83 metres. So 70,000 fathoms is about 128 kilometres. Kraal's "about 2 metres" and "120,000 metres" are rough.
- The recording has "140,000 fathoms" in the sentence about the one who loves. The image everywhere else is 70,000, so this may be a slip in the reading or the transcription. Check it against the handout if he posts the passage.

### The Either/Or page
- Kraal first guessed the "young men" passage was in "The Aesthetic Validity of Marriage". Hannay p. 487 is in "Equilibrium", in B's pages on what philosophy and mediation cannot do, a few pages after "Is it good and evil? No" (p. 480).

### Sources for two lines he quoted
- The Wittgenstein line is from his wartime notebooks, 8 July 1916: "To believe in God means to see that life has a meaning."
- Plato's doubt is *Phaedrus* 230a. Socrates says he does not yet know himself, whether he is a beast more complicated and furious than Typhon or a gentler, simpler creature with a divine share. Typhon is the hundred-headed monster, which is Kraal's "many-headed monster". Climacus opens chapter 3 of the *Crumbs* with this passage.

### The Crumbs' two possibilities in Climacus's terms
- The first is the Socratic position: the truth is already in the learner, learning is recollection, and the teacher is only an occasion.
- The second is the alternative Climacus builds as a thought experiment: the learner is in untruth and lacks the condition, so the teacher must give it. Only the god can do that, and Climacus calls such a teacher a saviour. He never names Christianity, but that is what he is describing.
