## Clarifications

### The tree, and whether history is "really a branch"
- Kraal's three branches, nature, logic and history, are B's list, not Hegel's. B writes that the spheres that are properly for thought are "logic, nature and history", and that in them necessity rules, so mediation is valid there (PDF p. 426).
- In Hegel's own system (the *Encyclopaedia*, 1817) the three parts are Logic, Nature and Spirit. World history is the last stretch of objective spirit, after the state. So your question is fair: history is a branch for B's purposes, not a top-level branch for Hegel.
- The word you wrote as "heriditary?" was most likely "necessity": history as the necessary unfolding of the Idea. That is B's phrase too, "think the whole of history and you think the necessary movement of the Idea" (p. 524).
- The fork under history is the question whether history is a necessary unfolding or the sum of free volitions and their contingencies. B's answer is both at once. The individual acts freely, but the action "enters into the order of things", and the higher order that "digests the free actions" into its eternal laws is necessity. So the philosopher is right to use mediation about history, but only "relative mediation" (PDF p. 426).
- The blank in "Ethics is the [blank] unfolding of history / reality" cannot be filled from your page. The Hegel claim behind it is that ethical life is freedom made actual, "the Idea of freedom" become "the existing world" (*Philosophy of Right*, §142). The shared Notion page may have the missing word.

### Ethics on the tree: marriage, civil society, state
- Hegel's name for this part is ethical life (German *Sittlichkeit*). It has three parts, in this order: the family, whose first form is marriage; civil society; the state (*Philosophy of Right*, 1820).
- The sentence Kraal quoted, "marriage is in essence an ethical relationship", is Hegel's. Knox's translation of the Addition to §161 reads "Marriage is in essence an ethical tie". Hegel's point there is that earlier natural-law writers looked only at the physical side of marriage, or at the contract. For Hegel the ethical side is the couple's consciousness of their unity as their substantial aim, and marriage begins in their free consent.
- This is the background to B's first letter: B treats marriage as the ethical and gives it a vow, an intention and duty. It is also why some readers call B a Hegelian (lecture 19).

### The Hegel steps, and the two you are missing
- Steps 1 to 3 on your page are Kraal's reconstruction of Hegel's starting point: the will is free, and freedom is its substance (*Philosophy of Right*, §4). Step 1, consciousness of being free, echoes Hegel's line that world history is the progress of the consciousness of freedom (*Lectures on the Philosophy of History*).
- Steps 4 and 5 are missing on your page (you wrote "5. ?"). In Hegel the path from the free will to marriage runs like this: a free will must give itself existence in the world (§29, right is "the existence of the free will"), and that existence is real only when other free wills acknowledge it. That acknowledgement is recognition (German *Anerkennung*). This is a reconstruction, not your notes; check it against the shared Notion page.
- Step 6 then follows: the self-actualization of freedom must be recognized, which happens in society, and the first institution where it happens is marriage.
- Your "'ought' is to be recognized / demanded" fits here. For Hegel the ethical is not a bare "ought" standing over against reality, the way he read Kant's duty. What ought to be is already realized and recognized in institutions. That is what "the rational is actual" means in the Preface to the *Philosophy of Right*.

### Your question about love and existence
- In the first letter love carried the argument because marriage is love with the stamp of the eternal. In the second letter the stress moves from love to choice. Choice is what gives a person a self, "the I chooses itself, or, more correctly, it accepts itself" (PDF p. 427).
- Love comes back in one place: the love with which one loves God has "only one expression", repentance, and repentance is how one chooses oneself absolutely (PDF p. 451). So love and existence meet in the choice of oneself before God, not in romance.

### B's view against Hegel: ethics is not one of the necessities
- B agrees with Hegel that philosophy's business is necessity, so in logic, nature and history mediation holds (PDF p. 426). Mediation is the Hegelian move of reconciling two opposites in a higher unity.
- He refuses to let ethics in. "For thought the opposition has no substance, it goes over into the other"; "for freedom the opposition does have substance, because the one opposite now excludes the other" (PDF p. 425). Exclusion is "precisely the opposite of mediation".
- The inward work, the "true life of freedom", is what philosophy "has nothing whatever to do" with. "In this world there rules an absolute either/or, but it is a world philosophy has nothing to do with" (PDF p. 426).
- B does not say there is no good and evil. He says good and evil are not a category of thought. That is what your "No such thing as good / evil" means.

### Page 524, the passage you marked very important
- The question B asks is whether the difference between good and evil can be thought. His answer is no.
- Reason: "Whenever I think, my relation to what it is I am thinking is one of necessity, but that is exactly why the difference between good and evil does not arise." Thought gives "relative differences, never the absolute difference".
- Philosophy "may well be justified in claiming it cannot think an absolute contradiction, but it by no means follows that the latter does not exist". Philosophy seems to have abolished the principle of contradiction only because "it has not yet reached it".
- The absolute difference is posited by the choice: "only when I have absolutely chosen myself have I posited an absolute difference, namely that between good and evil". Before the choice the difference "remains latent".
- The contrast with the absolute. Philosophy says "the absolute is by virtue of my thinking it", and then corrects itself to "the absolute's self-thinking in me", because my thought of the absolute is necessary. "Things are different with the good. The good is by virtue of my willing it, and otherwise it has no existence. This is the expression of freedom"; the same for evil.
- Your page has this right: "a matter of decision, a matter of will, but this does not imply that the good isn't real." B's words: this is "in no way to belittle the categories of good and evil or to reduce them to merely subjective determinations. On the contrary, it is to assert the absolute validity of these categories."
- B's last line there: "The good is the in-and-for-itself posited by the in-and-for-itself, and that is freedom."
- The "faith in the categories" line on your page 3 is close to B's point that good and evil are posited, not demonstrated. "Nothing empirical" is right: nothing you can think or observe produces the difference.

### Objective, relative, absolutism
- Objective morality: good and evil hold whether or not anyone wills them. Relative morality: good and evil depend on a person or a culture. Absolutism: some moral rules hold without exception.
- B fits none of these cleanly. Good and evil have "absolute validity", so he is no relativist. But they are not facts that thought can reach or prove, so they are not objective in the sense of a demonstrable fact. They are posited by freedom, by the choice.
- B also rules out two readings of that. It is not *liberum arbitrium*, the bare freedom to pick either way, because true freedom "has for all eternity evil outside itself" and perfects itself by excluding evil (PDF p. 425). And it does not assume "a notion of radical evil" (PDF pp. 426–427).

### Your page 480 line, in full
- PDF p. 423: "What is it, then, that I separate in my either/or? Is it good and evil? No, I simply want to bring you to the point where that choice truly acquires meaning for you."
- The sentence after it: "My either/or does not denote in the first instance the choice between good and evil, it denotes the choice whereby one chooses good and evil or excludes them" (PDF p. 424). The alternative is living aesthetically or living ethically, and "the aesthetic is not evil but indifference".
- Two lines near it that exams like. "Your choice is an aesthetic choice, but an aesthetic choice is no choice." "The only absolute either/or there is is the choice between good and evil, but it is also absolutely ethical" (PDF p. 423).
- The definitions, PDF p. 428: the aesthetic factor in a person is "that by which he is immediately what he is"; the ethical factor is "that by which he becomes what he becomes".
- The letter opens with A's own line from the first letter, "do it or don't do it, you will regret both", which B calls "a deep mockery of yourself" (PDF p. 416).

### Why good and evil cannot be proven, and God
- Your question "is it important that good vs evil can't be proven? → whether god exists?" has a B answer. B does not prove God either. In the choice "there appears before it not a distinguished person, but the eternal power itself", and the I accepts itself before it (PDF p. 427). Repentance is the one love of God, and its ground is "that he loved me first" (PDF p. 451).
- So for B the unprovability is not a loss. Proof belongs to thought and necessity; the choice, God and good and evil all belong to freedom.
