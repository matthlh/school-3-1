### The losses, in order
- He was born 5 May 1813, the youngest of the seven children of Michael Pedersen Kierkegaard and
  Ane Sørensdatter Lund.
- Your "age 6 to 21" is exactly right. Five of his six siblings and his mother died inside those
  fifteen years.

| Died | Who | Age at death | Søren's age |
|---|---|---|---|
| 14 Sep 1819 | Søren Michael, brother | 12 | 6 |
| 15 Mar 1822 | Maren Kirstine, sister | 24 | 8 |
| 10 Sep 1832 | Nicoline Christine, sister | 32 | 19 |
| 21 Sep 1833 | Niels Andreas, brother | 24 | 20 |
| 31 Jul 1834 | Ane, his mother | 66 | 21 |
| 29 Dec 1834 | Petrea Severine, sister | 33 | 21 |
| 9 Aug 1838 | Michael Pedersen, his father | 81 | 25 |

- Both married sisters died after childbirth, Nicoline in 1832 and Petrea two years later.
- The one who was left is **Peter Christian Kierkegaard**, 1805–1888, the second child. He became
  Bishop of Aalborg from 1857 to 1875 and Danish minister of church affairs in 1867–68, and he gave
  the address at Søren's funeral in 1855.
- Søren himself died on 11 November 1855, aged 42.
- Two dates are printed differently in different places. The father's death often appears as
  8 August 1838, because Søren's journal calls it the night of Wednesday the 8th; the record says
  9 August. The mother's occasionally appears as 29 July 1834; the date to use is 31 July.

### The father, and how much of the story is actually documented
- Michael Pedersen Kierkegaard was born 12 December 1756 at Sædding in West Jutland, went to
  Copenhagen as a boy, took out his own burgess rights as a hosier in December 1780, and died rich.
  He retired from trade in his forties and lived on his capital for the rest of his life.
- He belonged to the Moravian Brødremenighed in Copenhagen and was prominent in it, while the
  family also attended the state church under Mynster, who confirmed Søren in April 1828. Sunday
  morning at Vor Frue with Mynster, Sunday evening at the Moravian meeting.
- **The heath curse.** The one primary trace is a journal entry Søren wrote in 1846, Journal JJ 416.
  It describes the dreadful thing about the man who once as a little boy, herding sheep on the
  Jutland heath, starving and worn out, stood up on a hill and cursed God, and who could not forget
  it when he was 82. The entry names nobody. It says "the man", not "my father", it says the Jutland
  heath rather than Sædding, and it gives the boy no age. The identification with his father comes
  from Peter Christian, who wept when the passage was shown to him after Søren's death and said it
  was their father's story. So: family tradition recorded by a son eight years after the father
  died, not a documented event. The age does not even fit — the father was 81, four months short of
  82.
- **The "great earthquake".** Kierkegaard's own phrase is *den store Jordrystelse*, the great
  upheaval; "earthquake" is the conventional English. It is Papir 305:3 in the modern edition. In it
  he says the upheaval forced on him a new infallible law for reading everything: that his father's
  great age was a curse rather than a blessing, that the family's gifts existed only to tear each
  other apart, that his father would outlive them all and stand as a grave-cross over the grave of
  all his own hopes, and that a guilt rested on the whole family and God's punishment with it, the
  family to be wiped out as a failed experiment.
- Two things that entry does **not** contain, despite being cited for both: the heath curse, and any
  sexual sin. It never says what the guilt is. Its date is disputed as well — traditionally 1838,
  because the manuscript is headed "25 years old", but the modern edition puts it at 1843–45, and
  Brandt showed in 1949 that it cannot be earlier than 1839 because the Shakespeare motto is quoted
  from a German translation published in May of that year.
- **The age of Christ.** The belief that none of the children would outlive Jesus is not in the
  earthquake papers either. It comes from Brøchner, who records Søren saying he had been convinced
  for years that he would die at 33, and from Søren's own journal shortly after his birthday in
  1847: strange that he has turned 34, wholly inconceivable to him, he had been so certain he would
  die on or before that birthday that he could be tempted to assume his birth date was recorded
  wrongly so that he would still die at 34. Both numbers circulate, which is why it is worth knowing
  that the journal entry is about turning 34.
- **The dates behind the guilt, if he asks.** The father's first wife, Kirstine Nielsdatter Røyen,
  died childless on 23 March 1796. He married Ane, who was his household servant, on 26 April 1797,
  about thirteen months later. Their first child was born on 7 September 1797, four and a half
  months after the wedding.
- One caution on all of it. The standard story that Søren's melancholy came from his father's
  gloomy piety rests almost entirely on Søren's own retrospective accounts of himself. It is the
  received reading, and Kraal will use it, but it is testimony rather than independent evidence.

### "How numb did he feel?"
- The journals show the opposite of numbness. What he did with the deaths was read them as a system:
  a law of interpretation, a guilt on the household, a sentence being carried out in order. That is
  a mind working at full strength on its own catastrophe, not one shutting down.
- It is also why the biography gets a lecture before the essay. The essay's whole subject is a man
  whose ideal lies outside himself in a past or a future, and Kierkegaard had spent his twenties
  with his own life arranged that way.

### Why he opened with the biography, and the trap in it
- The essay you are reading is an address by A, an aesthete, to a fictional club of people who count
  themselves already dead. It is not Kierkegaard speaking. The biography explains what he had to
  work with, not what the essay claims.
- This is exactly the distinction Kraal has been hammering since lecture 1, and it is the easiest
  factual mark to lose. An exam option that begins "Kierkegaard holds that the unhappiest one…" is
  reporting A's position, not his.

### "Essentially not being present to oneself" — the definition to memorise
- A's sentence: the unhappy one is the one who has his ideal, the content of his life, the fullness
  of his consciousness, his own real nature, in one way or another outside himself. He is always
  absent from himself, never present to himself.
- From that alone the whole territory follows. You can only be absent in the past or in the future.
  So there are exactly two directions of unhappiness, and your 1 and 2 are right.

### Where "unhappy consciousness" comes from
- A opens the section by saying that in all Hegel's systematic writings there is a section on the
  unhappy consciousness, and thanks him for the firm delimitation.
- That is not literally true, and the exaggeration is the joke. It appears in one book, the
  *Phenomenology of Spirit* of 1807, chapter IV part B, "Freedom of Self-Consciousness: Stoicism,
  Scepticism and the Unhappy Consciousness". The mature system drops it entirely.
- A's next line gives the game away: happy is the man who has no more to do with the matter than to
  write a paragraph about it, happier still the one who can write the paragraph after. Then he says
  he and the fellowship will look at the region as natives, not as philosophers seeing the kingdom
  from a distance.
- What A borrows is only the boundary. What he does not borrow is Hegel's content: a consciousness
  split between the Unchangeable and its own changeable self, working through devotion, labour and
  a priestly mediator. A fills the same territory with hope and memory instead.
- The contrast worth one line in your memory: for Hegel the unhappy consciousness is a **stage**,
  and it is sublated into Reason at the end of the chapter. For A it is a **terminus**. The address
  reaches no winner and the grave stays empty.

### The grammar analogy
- A credits philology, not Hegel, for this one. Some tenses are present in a past time or present in
  a future time. But the pluperfect has nothing present in it, and the future perfect is the same.
- Those two are the hoping and the remembering individuals. Read the pairing carefully, because A
  lists the tenses pluperfect-first and then names the people hope-first: pluperfect goes with the
  rememberer, future perfect with the hoper.

### "Can we manufacture the most unhappy?" — no, and A says why
- A states it directly: one blow, however heavy, can never make a person the unhappiest.
- The reason is structural. A single blow can only take away hope, which leaves the man present in
  his memory, or take away memory, which leaves him present in his hope. Either way he is still
  present to himself somewhere.
- So unhappiness in A's sense is not a quantity of suffering you could pile up. It is a shape. Job
  is A's own illustration: he lost everything, but not at one blow, because the Lord took, and the
  Lord took, and the Lord took.
- A also says, before the competition even starts, that no single individual is the unhappiest —
  it is a **class**, and he awards the class's representative the name and the grave. That is why
  the essay ends with a gallery of types rather than a person.

### "Isn't shattered hope the most tragic thing?" — A says the opposite
- Hope's unhappy individuals never have the painfulness that memory's have. Their disappointment is
  always the more cheerful kind. So the unhappiest is always to be sought among the unhappy
  rememberers.
- His reason: memory is pre-eminently the proper element of the unhappy, because the past has the
  peculiar property of being over, whereas the future is still to come. In that sense the future
  lies nearer the present than the past does.
- The award at the end confirms it. The winner is hailed as recollection's unhappy lover.
- Your instinct is the ordinary one and it is worth holding onto as the objection — but on the exam,
  A's answer is memory, not hope.

### The two strict formations, and then the crossing
- A **hoper** is not unhappy in the strict sense as long as he is present *in* his hope. He becomes
  strictly unhappy when he cannot: he loses hope, hopes again, loses it again. The genuine case is
  sharper, and A corrects his own wording to get at it: it is not that he hopes for something with
  no reality for him, it is that he hopes for something he himself knows cannot be realised, because
  when hope fails he wills to go on being a hoper instead of becoming a rememberer.
- A **rememberer** is likewise fine as long as he is present in his memory. He becomes strictly
  unhappy when he remembers a time that never had reality for him. A's two examples: the man who
  never really had a childhood, becomes a teacher of children, discovers there what is beautiful in
  childhood, and spends the rest of his life staring back at a childhood he never had; and the man
  who lived without ever grasping life's joy, catches sight of it in the moment of death, and then
  does not die but lives again without living it over.
- Your 3 is the combination and you have it right, including the direction of each half. Memory is
  what stops him being present in his hope; hope is what stops him being present in his memory. He
  hopes for what ought to be remembered and remembers what he ought to hope for. So what he hopes
  for lies behind him and what he remembers lies ahead.
- The mechanism underneath it, which is the kind of thing Kraal can ask: an ordinary hope is
  disappointed because the goal moved further off. His is disappointed because he is *past* the
  goal. It has already been lived through, or should have been, and has passed over into memory.
  A's summary: his misfortune is that he came into the world too soon and therefore constantly
  arrives too late.

### "Is the biggest indicator regret?" — a near miss, and it matters
- Regret is the everyday face of the remembering individual, so you are in the right part of the
  essay. But as a definition it fails A's test twice.
- First, a man who regrets something he actually did is remembering a *real* past. A has just said
  that whoever is present in his memory is not unhappy in the strict sense. The strictly unhappy
  rememberer mourns a time that never had reality for him at all.
- Second, the unhappiest one is not fixed by *what* he remembers but by the crossing. Regret points
  in one direction. His life points the wrong way in both.
- What Kraal was getting at with "I should have done that, or done it better" is the everyday
  version: most people's unhappiness is a past they keep re-entering. A's analysis starts there and
  then argues that it is not yet the worst case.

### "I don't really understand this perspective" — the two lines that make it click
- On being alone: he has no contemporary he can attach himself to, no past he can long for, because
  his past has not yet come, and no posterity he can hope for, because his posterity is already
  over. The whole rest of the world is for him a single person, and that inseparable, importunate
  friend is **misunderstanding**.
- On why it is worse than madness: in daily life he is granted the honour of being taken for a man
  in his right mind, and he knows that if he explained himself to one person he would be declared
  insane. It is enough to drive a man mad, and he does not go mad, and that is precisely his
  unhappiness. Madness would at least be a resolution.

### On "death is a comfort rather than a tragedy"
- That is A's premise, not his conclusion, and he states it as the entrance requirement. Anyone who
  thinks death the greatest misfortune, or who became unhappy because he fears death, is shut out
  as an uninvited guest. The fellowship does not fear death; it knows a worse misfortune, which is
  living.
- A then ranks it, borrowing the old Silenus line: happy the one who could die, happy the one who
  died in old age, happier the one who died young, happiest the one who died at birth, happiest of
  all the one who was never born.
- **One thing from your reading notes to unlearn, and I had it wrong too.** "The unhappiest one is
  the one who cannot die" is raised by A and then thrown away. He puts it as the legend of the
  Wandering Jew: if that were true, the empty grave would be explained and the contest settled.
  Then he cancels it — but it is not so, death is the common good fortune of all human beings, so
  the unhappiest must be looked for inside that limit. "Cannot die" comes back later only in a
  hedged sense: in a way he cannot die, for he has not lived; in a way he cannot live, for he is
  already dead. That is about absence, not immortality. I have corrected the reading page and two
  questions in the bank.

### Which words to use
- You are reading Hannay, and Hannay says **memory**, **remembering individuals** and "the unhappy
  rememberers". Hong says **recollection** and "recollecting individualities". Most secondary
  writing on the essay uses Hong's vocabulary, so expect to see "recollection" everywhere and know
  it is the same thing.
- Two other splits: Hannay has "his life is not backwards but back-to-front in two directions",
  Hong "turned the wrong way in two directions". Hannay's title block is "The Unhappiest One / An
  Enthusiastic Address to Symparanekromenoi / Peroration in the Friday Meetings"; Hong has
  "inspired" for "enthusiastic".
