## Clarifications

### Kierkegaard's family losses

- Søren Kierkegaard was born on 5 May 1813 to Michael Pedersen Kierkegaard and Ane Sørensdatter Lund. He was the youngest of their seven children.
- The notes' "age 6 to 21" is exactly right. Five of his six siblings and his mother died inside those fifteen years.

| Died | Who | Age at death | Søren's age |
|---|---|---|---|
| 14 Sep 1819 | Søren Michael, brother | 12 | 6 |
| 15 Mar 1822 | Maren Kirstine, sister | 24 | 8 |
| 10 Sep 1832 | Nicoline Christine, sister | 32 | 19 |
| 21 Sep 1833 | Niels Andreas, brother | 24 | 20 |
| 31 Jul 1834 | Ane, his mother | 66 | 21 |
| 29 Dec 1834 | Petrea Severine, sister | 33 | 21 |
| 9 Aug 1838 | Michael Pedersen, his father | 81 | 25 |

- Both married sisters died after childbirth, Nicoline in 1832 and Petrea two years later.
- The one sibling who was left is **Peter Christian Kierkegaard** (1805–1888), the second child. Peter was Bishop of Aalborg from 1857 to 1875 and Danish minister of church affairs in 1867–68.
- Søren died on 11 November 1855 at age 42. Peter gave the address at his funeral in 1855.
- Two dates are printed differently in different places.
  - The father's death often appears as 8 August 1838, because Søren's journal calls it the night of Wednesday the 8th. The record says 9 August.
  - The mother's death occasionally appears as 29 July 1834. The date to use is 31 July.

### His father's life

- Michael Pedersen Kierkegaard was born in Sædding in West Jutland on 12 December 1756. He moved to Copenhagen as a boy and took out his own burgess rights as a hosier in December 1780.
- He retired from trade in his forties, lived on his capital for the rest of his life and died rich.
- He belonged to Copenhagen's Moravian Brødremenighed and was prominent in it. The family also attended the state church under Mynster, who confirmed Søren in April 1828. They went to Vor Frue with Mynster on Sunday morning and to the Moravian meeting on Sunday evening.
- His first wife, Kirstine Nielsdatter Røyen, died childless on 23 March 1796. He married Ane, his household servant, on 26 April 1797, about thirteen months later.
- Their first child was born on 7 September 1797, four and a half months after the wedding.
- These are the dates behind the family guilt.
- The standard story traces Søren's melancholy to his father's gloomy piety. It rests almost entirely on Søren's own retrospective accounts of himself.
- It is the received reading, and Kraal will use it, but it is testimony rather than independent evidence.

### The heath curse

- The only primary trace of the **heath curse** is a journal entry Søren wrote in 1846, Journal JJ 416.
- It describes the dreadful thing about "the man" who once as a little boy, herding sheep on the Jutland heath, starving and worn out, stood up on a hill and cursed God. The man could not forget it when he was 82.
- The entry names nobody. It says "the man", not "my father", it names the Jutland heath rather than Sædding, and it gives the boy no age.
- The identification with his father comes from Peter Christian. He wept when the passage was shown to him after Søren's death and said it was their father's story.
- So the curse is family tradition, recorded by a son eight years after the father died. It is not a documented event.
- The age does not even fit. The man in the entry was 82, and Michael Pedersen died at 81, four months short of 82.

### The great upheaval

- Kierkegaard's own phrase is *den store Jordrystelse*, the **great upheaval**. "Great earthquake" is the conventional English. The passage is Papir 305:3 in the modern edition.
- He says the upheaval forced on him a new infallible law for interpreting everything. Under that law:
  - His father's great age was a curse rather than a blessing.
  - The family's gifts existed only to tear each other apart.
  - His father would outlive them all and stand as a grave-cross over the grave of all his own hopes.
  - A guilt rested on the whole family, and God's punishment with it. The family was to be wiped out as a failed experiment.
- The entry is cited for the heath curse and for a sexual sin, but it contains neither. It never says what the guilt is.
- Its date is disputed. The traditional date is 1838, because the manuscript is headed "25 years old". The modern edition dates it to 1843–45.
- In 1949, Brandt showed it could be no earlier than 1839, because the Shakespeare motto is quoted from a German translation published in May of that year.

### The age of Christ

- The belief that none of the children would outlive Jesus is not in the upheaval papers either.
- It comes from Brøchner, who records Søren saying he had been convinced for years that he would die at 33.
- It also comes from Søren's own journal shortly after his birthday in 1847. He found it strange, wholly inconceivable, that he had turned 34.
- He had been so certain he would die on or before that birthday that he could be tempted to assume his birth date was recorded wrongly, so that he would still die at 34.
- Both numbers, 33 and 34, circulate. The journal entry is about turning 34.

### How he responded to the deaths

- The journals show the opposite of numbness. He read the deaths as a system: a law of interpretation, a guilt on the household, and a sentence being carried out in order.
- His mind was working at full strength on its own catastrophe, not shutting down.
- This is also why the biography gets a lecture before the essay. The essay's subject is a man whose ideal lies outside himself in a past or a future, and Kierkegaard had spent his twenties with his own life arranged that way.

### What A means by unhappiness

- The essay is an address by A, an aesthete, to a fictional club of people who count themselves already dead. It is not Kierkegaard speaking.
- The biography explains what Kierkegaard had to work with, not what the essay claims.

> **Watch out.** Kraal has stressed this distinction since lecture 1, and it is the easiest factual mark to lose. An exam option that begins "Kierkegaard holds that the unhappiest one…" is reporting A's position, not his.

> **Definition.** A's definition: the unhappy one is the one who has his ideal, the content of his life, the fullness of his consciousness, his own real nature, in one way or another outside himself.
> He is always absent from himself, never present to himself. This is the definition to memorise, and it is what the notes' "Essentially not being present to oneself" captures.

- The whole territory follows from that definition. A person can only be absent in the past or in the future, so there are exactly two directions of unhappiness. The notes' 1 and 2 are right.

### What A takes from Hegel

- A opens the section by saying that in all Hegel's systematic writings there is a section on the unhappy consciousness. He thanks Hegel for the firm delimitation.
- That is not literally true, and the exaggeration is the joke. The section appears in only one book, the *Phenomenology of Spirit* (1807), chapter IV part B, "Freedom of Self-Consciousness: Stoicism, Scepticism and the Unhappy Consciousness". The mature system drops it entirely.
- A's next line gives the joke away. Happy is the man who has no more to do with the matter than to write a paragraph about it, and happier still the one who can write the paragraph after.
- A then says he and the fellowship will look at the region as natives, not as philosophers seeing the kingdom from a distance.
- What A borrows is only the boundary. He does not borrow Hegel's content: a consciousness split between the Unchangeable and its own changeable self, working through devotion, labour and a priestly mediator.
- A fills the same territory with hope and memory instead.

> **Watch out.** For Hegel the **unhappy consciousness** is a **stage**. It is sublated into Reason at the end of the chapter, which means Reason takes it up and supersedes it.
> For A it is a **terminus**, or endpoint. The address reaches no winner, and the grave stays empty.

### How the tenses map to hope and memory

- A credits philology, the study of language, not Hegel, for this analogy. Some tenses are present in a past time or present in a future time.
- But the pluperfect has nothing present in it, and the future perfect is the same.
- Those two are the hoping and the remembering individuals. The pluperfect goes with the rememberer and the future perfect with the hoper.
- Read the pairing carefully. A lists the tenses pluperfect first, then names the people hoper first.

### Why one blow cannot make the unhappiest person

- A states it directly: one blow, however heavy, can never make a person the unhappiest. So the most unhappy cannot be manufactured.
- The reason is structural. A single blow can only take away hope, which leaves the man present in his memory, or take away memory, which leaves him present in his hope. Either way he is still present to himself somewhere.
- So unhappiness in A's sense is not a quantity of suffering that could be piled up. It is a shape.
- Job is A's own illustration. He lost everything, but not at one blow, because the Lord took, and the Lord took, and the Lord took.
- Before the contest even starts, A says that no single individual is the unhappiest. It is a **class**, a kind of unhappy person, and A awards the class's representative the name and the grave.
- That is why the essay ends with a gallery of types rather than a person.

### Why memory, not hope, is the worse unhappiness

- Hope's unhappy individuals never have the painfulness that memory's have. Their disappointment is always the more cheerful kind.
- So the unhappiest is always to be sought among the unhappy rememberers.
- A's reason is that memory is pre-eminently the proper element of the unhappy. The past has the peculiar property of being over, whereas the future is still to come. In that sense the future lies nearer the present than the past does.
- The award at the end confirms it. The winner is hailed as recollection's unhappy lover.

> **Watch out.** The notes suggest that shattered hope is the most tragic thing. That is the ordinary view and a fair objection, but on the exam A's answer is memory, not hope.

### The unhappy hoper

- A hoper is not unhappy in the strict sense as long as he is present in his hope. Hope that carries a person forward is fine.
- A's example of a hoper who is not strictly unhappy is the person who hopes for eternal life. He gives up the present, so he is unhappy in a loose sense. But he is present in that hope and never collides with the particular moments of finite life.
- The hoper becomes strictly unhappy when he cannot be present in his hope. He loses hope, hopes again and loses it again.
- The genuine case is sharper, and A corrects his own wording to reach it. It is not that he hopes for something with no reality for him. It is that he hopes for something he himself knows cannot be realised.
- He does this because when hope fails, he wills to go on being a hoper instead of becoming a rememberer.

### The unhappy rememberer

- A rememberer is likewise not strictly unhappy as long as he is present in his memory.

> **Definition.** The **strictly unhappy rememberer** remembers a time that never had reality for him.

- A's first example is a man who never really had a childhood. He becomes a teacher of children, discovers there what is beautiful in childhood, and spends the rest of his life staring back at a childhood he never had.
- A's second example is a man who lived without ever grasping life's joy. He catches sight of it in the moment of death, then does not die but lives again without living it over.

### Why regret is not the definition

- Regret is the everyday face of the remembering individual, so the notes are in the right part of the essay. As a definition, though, it fails A's test twice.
- First, a man who regrets something he actually did is remembering a real past. A says that whoever is present in his memory is not unhappy in the strict sense. The strictly unhappy rememberer mourns a time that never had reality for him at all.
- Second, the unhappiest one is fixed by the crossing, not by what he remembers. Regret points in one direction. His life points the wrong way in both.
- Kraal's "I should have done that, or done it better" is the everyday version: most people's unhappiness is a past they keep re-entering. A's analysis starts there and then argues that it is not yet the worst case.
- These three ways to relate to a past a person missed come from our discussion.
  - "I wish I had done that" is ordinary regret about a real absence. The person is present in his memory.
  - Living inside a version of one's youth that never happened, trying to remember it as if it had, is A's stuck-in-the-past case, the unhappy rememberer.
  - Trying to have that youth now, at 25 or 40, and finding it does not land because the moment it was built for is gone, is hope pointed backwards. That is the combination.
- Noticing the gap is fine. Building a life around it in either direction is the trap.

### How hope and memory cross

- The **combination** is the third formation, and the notes have the direction of each half right. Memory is what stops him being present in his hope, and hope is what stops him being present in his memory.
- He hopes for what ought to be remembered and remembers what he ought to hope for. So what he hopes for lies behind him, and what he remembers lies ahead.
- An ordinary hope is disappointed because the goal moved further off. His is disappointed because he is past the goal. It has already been lived through, or should have been, and has passed over into memory.
- A's summary is that his misfortune is that he came into the world too soon and therefore constantly arrives too late.
- Planning is not the second half. A planner thinks about the future while staying in the present: the logistics, the times, the responses. A planner still expects to be surprised.
- A's man has *consumed* the future. He has lived it in imagination so thoroughly that when it arrives it is already used up, so nothing happens for the first time.
- The line to draw is between working out the logistics and pre-living how it will feel. A does not draw that line, and he gives no advice at all.

### Kraal's Gatsby example

- Gatsby thinks he is hoping for a future with Daisy. What he wants is Daisy in 1917, before Tom, and he says that of course you can repeat the past.
- So what he hopes for lies behind him. That is the first half, hoping for what should be remembered.
- He has lived the reunion in his head every night for five years, staring at the green light. When the real Daisy is in front of him, she cannot match it. Fitzgerald says the illusion had gone beyond her.
- He had already experienced the future, so the real one arrives as a memory. That is the second half, remembering what should be hoped for.
- When he finally has her, the green light loses its meaning for him. The hope is disappointed not because the goal moved away but because he is past it.
- He got rich too late. The money that would have won her at 22 is exactly what cannot buy her back now. That is A's line about coming into the world too soon and always arriving too late.
- The novel's last sentence states the formula: boats beating toward the future, carried back into the past.
- Gatsby, from 1925, is Kraal's illustration. A's own examples are the teacher who never had a childhood and the man who sees life's joy only on his deathbed.

### Why the unhappiest person is alone

- He has no contemporary he can attach himself to.
- He has no past he can long for, because his past has not yet come. He has no posterity he can hope for, because his posterity is already over.
- The whole rest of the world is for him a single person, and that inseparable, importunate friend is **misunderstanding**.

### Why it is worse than madness

- In daily life he is granted the honour of being taken for a man in his right mind. He knows that if he explained himself to one person, he would be declared insane.
- It is enough to drive a man mad, and he does not go mad. That is precisely his unhappiness, because madness would at least be a resolution.

### Death as the entrance requirement, and the one who cannot die

- The notes' "death is comfort rather than tragic" is A's premise, not his conclusion. A states it as the entrance requirement for the fellowship.
- Anyone who thinks death the greatest misfortune, or who became unhappy because he fears death, is shut out as an uninvited guest.
- The fellowship does not fear death. It knows a worse misfortune, which is living.
- A then ranks it, borrowing the old Silenus line. Happy is the one who could die, happy the one who died in old age, happier the one who died young, happiest the one who died at birth, and happiest of all the one who was never born.
- A raises "The unhappiest one is the one who cannot die" and then throws it away. He puts it as the legend of the Wandering Jew. If that were true, the empty grave would be explained and the contest settled.
- He cancels it: it is not so, because death is the common good fortune of all human beings. So the unhappiest must be looked for inside that limit.
- "Cannot die" comes back later only in a hedged sense. In a way he cannot die, for he has not lived; in a way he cannot live, for he is already dead.
- That hedged sense is about absence from himself, not immortality. "The one who cannot die" is not A's answer.
