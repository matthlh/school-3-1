## Clarifications

### Kierkegaard's family losses

- Søren Kierkegaard was born on 5 May 1813 to Michael Pedersen Kierkegaard and Ane Sørensdatter Lund. He was the youngest of their seven children.
- Five of his six siblings and his mother died in the fifteen years from age 6 to 21.

| Died | Who | Age at death | Søren's age |
|---|---|---|---|
| 14 Sep 1819 | Søren Michael, brother | 12 | 6 |
| 15 Mar 1822 | Maren Kirstine, sister | 24 | 8 |
| 10 Sep 1832 | Nicoline Christine, sister | 32 | 19 |
| 21 Sep 1833 | Niels Andreas, brother | 24 | 20 |
| 31 Jul 1834 | Ane, his mother | 66 | 21 |
| 29 Dec 1834 | Petrea Severine, sister | 33 | 21 |
| 9 Aug 1838 | Michael Pedersen, his father | 81 | 25 |

- Both married sisters died after childbirth, Nicoline in 1832 and Petrea two years later.
- His surviving sibling was **Peter Christian Kierkegaard** (1805–1888), the second child. Peter was Bishop of Aalborg from 1857 to 1875 and Danish minister of church affairs in 1867–68.
- Søren died on 11 November 1855 at age 42. Peter gave the address at his funeral in 1855.
- Sources disagree on both parents' death dates. The father's record says 9 August 1838, while Søren's journal refers to the night of Wednesday the 8th, the basis for the date 8 August 1838. The mother's date is 31 July 1834, though 29 July 1834 also appears.

### His father's life

- Michael Pedersen Kierkegaard was born in Sædding in West Jutland on 12 December 1756. He moved to Copenhagen as a boy and took out burgess rights as a hosier in December 1780.
- He retired from trade in his forties, lived on his capital and died rich.
- He was prominent in Copenhagen's Moravian Brødremenighed. The family also attended the state church under Mynster, who confirmed Søren in April 1828. They went to Vor Frue on Sunday morning and to the Moravian meeting on Sunday evening.
- His first wife, Kirstine Nielsdatter Røyen, died childless on 23 March 1796. He married his household servant Ane on 26 April 1797, about thirteen months later.
- Their first child was born on 7 September 1797, four and a half months after the wedding.
- The usual account traces Søren's melancholy to his father's gloomy piety. Kraal uses that reading, which rests almost entirely on Søren's later recollections of himself.

### The heath curse and the great upheaval

- The only primary trace of the **heath curse** is Søren's Journal JJ 416 from 1846. It describes "the man" who cursed God as a boy. He was starving and worn out while herding sheep on the Jutland heath. He stood on a hill to curse God and still remembered it at 82.
- The entry gives neither the man's identity nor the boy's age. It names the Jutland heath without naming Sædding and never says "my father".
- Peter Christian wept when he saw the entry after Søren's death and identified it as their father's story. The journal entry dates to eight years after his father's death, and Peter's identification is family testimony.
- The ages disagree. The man in the journal was 82. Michael Pedersen died at 81, four months short of 82.
- Søren calls his **great upheaval** *den store Jordrystelse*. The usual English name is the "great earthquake". The passage is Papir 305:3 in the modern edition.
- He says the upheaval gave him an infallible rule for interpreting everything. He understood his father's long life as a curse and his family's gifts as a means for them to tear each other apart.
- He expected his father to outlive them all. He pictures him standing as a grave-cross over the grave of his own hopes.
- He thought the whole family shared a guilt and would be wiped out as a failed experiment in God's punishment. The entry leaves the guilt unspecified, with no mention of a heath curse or sexual sin.
- The dating disagrees across accounts. The traditional date is 1838, based on the heading "25 years old". The modern edition dates it to 1843–45. In 1949, Brandt showed it could be no earlier than 1839 because the Shakespeare motto came from a German translation published in May of that year.

### The age of Christ

- The belief that none of the children would outlive Jesus comes from sources outside the upheaval papers. Brøchner records Søren saying he had long expected to die at 33.
- After turning 34 in 1847, Søren wrote that he had been certain he would die by that birthday. He was tempted to think his birth date had been recorded incorrectly and that he could still die at 34.
- These accounts give different ages, 33 in Brøchner and 34 in the journal entry.
- The journals show Søren actively interpreting the deaths as punishment for a guilt the household shared.

### What A means by unhappiness

- A, the aesthete, addresses a fictional fellowship whose members count themselves already dead. Kierkegaard's biography helps explain the subject of the essay. Its claims belong to A.
- The unhappy person has the ideal and content of his life, the fullness of his consciousness and his real nature outside himself. A calls this "Essentially not being present to oneself".
- In A's account, a person can be absent from himself in two directions, toward the past or toward the future. Kierkegaard spent his twenties interpreting his own life through a past or a future outside himself.

### What A takes from Hegel

- A thanks Hegel for marking out the unhappy consciousness and jokes that all of Hegel's systematic writings have a section on it.
- That claim and Hegel's texts differ. The section appears in the *Phenomenology of Spirit* (1807), chapter IV part B, "Freedom of Self-Consciousness: Stoicism, Scepticism and the Unhappy Consciousness". The mature system omits it.
- A jokes that the man who only has to write a paragraph on the subject is happy, and the man who writes the next paragraph is happier. The fellowship discusses unhappiness as people who know it from experience.
- Hegel's **unhappy consciousness** is split between the Unchangeable and its own changeable self. It passes through devotion, labour and a priestly mediator, then gives way to Reason, which incorporates and supersedes it.
- A takes the scope of the subject from Hegel and makes hope and memory its content. For A, unhappy consciousness is a **terminus**, or endpoint. The contest names a representative of a class and the grave stays empty.

### How the tenses map to hope and memory

- A takes the tense analogy from philology, the study of language. Some tenses express a present within the past or the future.
- The pluperfect and future perfect contain no present in A's analogy. The pluperfect corresponds to the rememberer and the future perfect to the hoper. A lists the tenses pluperfect first, then names the people hoper first.

### Why one loss cannot make the unhappiest person

- One blow can destroy either hope or memory. The person can still be present to himself in whichever remains.
- Job loses everything in a series of losses. A uses that sequence to show why the extent of a person's suffering alone cannot explain the strictest unhappiness.
- The title and grave are awarded to a representative of a **class**, a kind of unhappy person. The essay ends with a set of types without naming one individual.
- A ranks unhappy memory as more painful than disappointed hope. The past is over, while the future is still to come and so lies nearer the present.
- The notes suggest shattered hope is most tragic. A looks among the unhappy rememberers and calls the winner recollection's unhappy lover.

### The unhappy hoper

- A person who is present to himself in a hope can give up the present in a loose sense while avoiding strict unhappiness. A's example is someone who hopes for eternal life and whose hope never collides with the moments of finite life.
- The **strictly unhappy hoper** loses hope, hopes again and loses it again. The decisive case is someone who hopes for something he knows cannot happen. He chooses to keep hoping when the hope fails, so he can remain a hoper.

### The unhappy rememberer

- A person can be present in a memory of an actual past. Kraal's example of ordinary regret is "I should have done that, or done it better". The person keeps revisiting a past that was real.
- The **strictly unhappy rememberer** remembers a time that never had reality for him.
- A gives the example of a man who becomes a teacher, discovers what is beautiful in childhood and spends his life recalling a childhood he never had.
- In A's other example, a man sees life's joy at the moment of death after failing to grasp it all his life. He survives and resumes living without living that life over.

### How hope and memory cross

- The **combination** is the third formation. Memory keeps the person absent from hope, and hope keeps him absent from memory. He hopes for what he should remember and remembers what he should hope for.
- Ordinary hope is disappointed when the goal moves further away. For the combination, hope disappoints because the person is already past the goal in his mind. He feels he already lived it, or should have lived it, so it has passed into memory.
- A describes his misfortune as coming into the world too soon and therefore always arriving too late.
- A planner works out practical details and can still be surprised. The person in A's example imagines a future experience so thoroughly that when it arrives, it feels as though it already happened. The contrast helps explain the idea. A gives no advice about how to plan.
- "I wish I had done that" expresses ordinary regret about a real past. Someone who recalls a youth he never lived is the unhappy rememberer. Someone who tries to recover it at 25 or 40 hopes for something that belongs to the past. Regret alone explains only one direction of absence.

### Kraal's Gatsby example

- In the novel from 1925, Gatsby appears to want a future with Daisy. He wants Daisy as she was in 1917 before Tom and says the past can be repeated. He hopes for what he should remember.
- Gatsby imagines the reunion every night for five years as he watches the green light. Fitzgerald says his illusion went beyond Daisy. Gatsby has already lived the reunion in his mind, so he experiences the real reunion as a memory of what he should still hope for.
- The green light loses its meaning when he finally has Daisy. He is past the goal he had imagined.
- Gatsby became rich too late. Money that could have won Daisy at 22 cannot bring her back. The novel ends with boats moving toward the future and carried back into the past.
- Gatsby is Kraal's illustration. A's own examples are the teacher and the man who sees life's joy on his deathbed.

### Why the unhappiest person is alone

- He has no contemporary to attach himself to. His past has yet to arrive, and the future generations he could hope for are already over for him.
- To him the whole world is a single, inseparable companion named **misunderstanding**.
- People take him to be in his right mind. He expects that anyone who heard him explain himself would declare him insane. Remaining sane prolongs the unhappiness because madness would at least resolve the conflict.

### Why death cannot settle the contest

- The fellowship regards living as a worse misfortune than dying. Someone who fears death or thinks it the greatest misfortune is excluded from the meeting.
- Drawing on Silenus, A calls the one who could die happy. He calls death in old age happy, death young happier, death at birth happiest, and never being born happiest of all.
- A considers the claim "The unhappiest one is the one who cannot die" through the legend of the Wandering Jew. Literal inability to die would explain the empty grave and settle the contest.
- He rejects the explanation because death is a good fortune shared by all humans. He later says the unhappiest person in a way cannot die because he has never lived, and in a way cannot live because he is already dead. Those phrases describe absence from himself.
