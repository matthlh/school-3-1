## From the reading

### Where these pages sit
- The two sections come after the music-and-language argument (pp. 77–83) and before the Third Stage. Page 90 is in the Second Stage.
- For each of the first two stages A takes one character as its picture, not the whole opera: Cherubino from *The Marriage of Figaro*, Papageno from *The Magic Flute*.

### The First Stage: desire dreams (Cherubino, "the Page")
- Desire has not woken up yet. Desire and its object are not yet separate: the object is there only as something dreamt inside the desire itself.
- So the desire does not know what it wants. It is a longing with no aim, in love with love itself.
- Its mood is quiet and melancholy, not joyful.
- In the aria Kraal played on Monday, Cherubino searches for "a good outside myself" without knowing who holds it or what it is (see the [lecture 9 page](09-the-musical-erotic.md)).

### The Second Stage: desire seeks (Papageno)
- Desire wakes up. Its object separates from it and appears outside it, and it appears as many: all the women in the world.
- So desire turns outward and goes looking among them.
- Its mood is cheerful and lively, the opposite of the first stage's melancholy.
- It looks among the many but does not yet desire any one of them. It seeks one that it can desire (p. 90).
- Papageno, the bird-catcher with his panpipe, fits this. His aria wants "a net for girls" to catch them by the dozen (lecture 9 page).
- A takes Papageno, not the opera's hero Tamino, as the picture of this stage.

### How the two stages lead to the third
- Dreaming has no object. Seeking has many objects but wants none in particular. Desiring, Don Giovanni, wants each particular woman, as woman in general.
- The third stage keeps something of each: the pull toward an ideal from the first, and the turn toward the many from the second.
- The order follows the growth of desire, not the dates of the operas: *Figaro* 1786, *Don Giovanni* 1787, *The Magic Flute* 1791.
