## Clarifications

### What this lecture adds

- Most of the friend's page is Kraal's synthesis of the essay already covered in [lecture 3](03-losses-and-the-unhappy-consciousness.md) and the [reading page](../readings/unhappiest-one.md). That part covers:
  - the class rule;
  - fear of life rather than death;
  - the past, future and combination forms;
  - the teacher-of-children example;
  - Gatsby;
  - the young-or-old and live-or-die symmetry;
  - the four closing clauses.
- The new material is the engagement, what the childhood injury actually was, the Tolstoy comparison, and Kraal's three words for the ending: doxology, paradox and comfort.

### A's premise: happy only when present to oneself

- A does say that a person can only be happy in the present, but as a conditional he slips in while defining the hoping and remembering individuals. They are unhappy in a sense "if otherwise it is only the person who is present to himself that is happy."
- A never argues for the premise. The whole contest runs on it, which is why the objection from our discussion, that A simply stipulates it, bites here.
- The safe exam form is this: on A's view a person is happy only insofar as he is present to himself, so absence in past or future is what unhappiness *is*.

### Unable to die, live, hate or be strong

- Die and live are right. In a way he cannot die, for he has never lived; in a way he cannot live, for he is already dead.
- "Hate" and "be strong" are half-remembered in the notes. A does not say he cannot hate. He says he hates the world only because he loves it.
- He has no passion, not because he lacks it, but because in the same instant he has the opposite one.
- He is powerless, not because he lacks strength, but because his own strength makes him impotent.
- The pattern in all of these is the same: each capacity is cancelled by its own opposite, so he is never on one side of anything. This is what Kraal's aside in the notes points at.

### The four closing clauses

- The Danish asks what life is but *Afsindighed*, faith but *Daarskab*, hope but *Galgenfrist*, and love but *Eddike i Saaret*.

| A asks about | Danish | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Life | *Afsindighed* | Madness |
| Faith | *Daarskab* | Folly |
| Hope | *Galgenfrist* | A gallows reprieve |
| Love | *Eddike i Saaret* | Vinegar in the wound |

- **Galgenfrist** is the sharp one. Literally it is the stay of execution granted to a condemned man. Hope is not a way out, only a postponement.
- The notes' "reprieve / postponing" has *Galgenfrist* exactly.
- The translations differ on the last image. Hannay has "salt in the wound" and Hong has "vinegar in the wound". The notes gloss it as "pain", so on an MC option expect salt or vinegar.

### The childhood injury and Kierkegaard's death

- The notes mention a childhood brain hemorrhage. There is no record of one for Kierkegaard, and none of the standard sources has one.
- The line is almost certainly two other facts run together.
  - His brother **Søren Michael** died at 12 in September 1819, when Søren was 6, after a collision in the schoolyard. The injury is usually described as to the head.
  - "Brain hemorrhage" was one of the causes later proposed for Kierkegaard's own fatal illness in 1855, and rejected.
- The childhood story that does attach to him is a fall from a tree, which he himself blamed for his crooked back. The Danish medical writers who record it doubt it as a cause and propose spinal tuberculosis instead.
- Treat the fall as his own explanation, not a documented event.
- What is documented: he collapsed in the street in late September 1855 with his legs paralysed. He entered Frederiks Hospital on 2 October and died there on 11 November 1855, aged 42.
- There was no autopsy. The cause of death is still disputed.

### Kierkegaard and Regine Olsen

- The notes' "marriage" was an engagement. He was engaged to **Regine Olsen** and never married her.
- Regine was born in 1822. They met in May 1837, when she was 15 and he was 24.
- He proposed on 8 September 1840. She accepted on 10 September.
- He sent back the ring with a farewell letter on 11 August 1841. She refused to accept the break. The final break in person came in October 1841.
- He left for Berlin on 25 October, two weeks later, to hear Schelling. That is the trip from lecture 1.
- The notes' reason is one of his own stated reasons, and the one Kraal gave. His journals say there was "something spectral" about him that no one could endure who had to see him every day. The ring letter says he could not make a girl happy.
- He gave two more reasons.
  - A secret he felt he would have to confide to a wife and could not. He called it his "thorn in the flesh" and repeated it on his deathbed.
  - His religious task as an author, which Regine herself later named as the reason.
- On an exam, "his melancholy" is safe. "Only his melancholy" is not.
- Regine became engaged to her former tutor Johan Frederik Schlegel in 1843. They married on 3 November 1847. Schlegel became governor of the Danish West Indies in 1855.
- They never spoke again after 1841 except once. On 17 March 1855, the day she sailed, she stopped him in the street and said God bless you, may all go well with you. He tipped his hat. He died eight months later.
- His will was a sealed letter to Peter Christian. It left everything to Regine unconditionally, because for him an engagement was and is as binding as a marriage.
- She declined it and asked only for her letters back.

### The engagement in the books

- Kraal will come back to where the engagement shows up in the books.
- *The Seducer's Diary* was read in Copenhagen as his own treatment of Regine. It was meant to make him look like a scoundrel.
- Judge William's marriage essays defend the life Kierkegaard could not enter.
- In *Repetition*, a young man finds he is already recollecting the girl and breaks with her. Kierkegaard rewrote its ending after learning in July 1843 that Regine was engaged.
- *Fear and Trembling* reads Abraham's sacrifice of Isaac as Kierkegaard's sacrifice of Regine.
- "Guilty?/Not Guilty?" in *Stages* quotes his farewell letter almost word for word.

### Tolstoy's *On Life*

- The comparison is Kraal's, not something in the scholarship.
- Tolstoy wrote *On Life* in 1886–87, thirty years after Kierkegaard died. There is no evidence he ever read "The Unhappiest One", so the parallel is a convergence, not an influence.
- What they share: Tolstoy argues that true life exists only in the present, outside time, and that the fear of death is an illusion produced by mistaking one's temporal, animal existence for life.
- Tolstoy's sharpest line is that the thought of a future death is only the transfer into the future of a death already happening in the present. It is a sign of the life a man ought to have and does not.
- That is the Tolstoyan twin of A's man who cannot die because he has not lived.
- The differences are the exam-safe half. Tolstoy has a positive doctrine: subject the animal self to reason, and a person lives now and loses the fear of death.
- A has no cure at all. He crowns the unhappiest one and walks off.
- Tolstoy's target is other-worldly religion, people who put the good of life before birth or after death, not memory and hope.

### Doxology, paradox and comfort

- These are Kraal's three words for the ending.
- A **doxology** is a short hymn of praise, such as "Glory be to the Father". It is Kraal's label for the ending, not a term found in the literature.
- The label fits. A hails the winner with a chain of titles, salutes him as "great unknown", pronounces wishes, and reverses the title to "the happiest". Know the word as Kraal's, and know what it names.
- The **paradox**: A says the unhappiest one is in "conflict" with the world as a single Thou. The whole world is for him one person, Misunderstanding, and his lips smile at the world that misunderstands him.
- The notes say "victimized". A's word is conflict.
- The satisfaction is that the title is a distinction. It is a gift of fortune no one can give himself, a prize the world cannot take away, so the one who holds it need never fear good fortune.
- That is why A corrects himself to "the happiest" and says language fails.
- **Comfort** is not A's word. A's word throughout is *Lykke*, fortune or happiness: a gift of fortune, the fellowship gathers to envy his fortune, and he need not fear fortune.
- A never says the unhappiest one is consoled.
- Kraal's "not joy but comfort" is a fair reading of what the four clauses do. Faith, hope and love are each downgraded to something an aesthete can bear.
- On an MC option, the words are "the happiest", "gift of fortune" and "envy".
