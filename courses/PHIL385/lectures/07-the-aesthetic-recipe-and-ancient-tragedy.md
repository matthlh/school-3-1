## Clarifications

### Kraal's three-part recipe

- The recipe is Kraal's synthesis of A's three essays so far, not A's own. It is the exam summary of A so far.
- On the aesthetic view, satisfaction is best served in three ways, one from each essay.

| Essay | Satisfaction is best served by |
|---|---|
| "The Unhappiest One" | A pessimistic baseline. |
| "Crop Rotation" | Having no deeper lifelong commitments. |
| "Ancient Tragedy" | Seeing your fortune or misfortune as beyond your control. |

- Learn it as a set of three with the essay attached to each. A question asking which essay supplies which claim is exactly what the seven-question exams are made of.

### Two meanings of aesthetic

- The word has two uses in this course, and A slides between them.
- In the narrow sense, **aesthetic** is the artistic category: what counts as beautiful, tragic, comic or interesting. This is the sense in "aesthetic validity", "aesthetic interest" and "aesthetic guilt", and the sense in most of this essay.
- When A says sin is not an aesthetic element, he means it is not material for art.
- In the wide sense, the **aesthetic life-view** is the first of the three spheres. It means living for immediacy, moods, enjoyment and the interesting.
- The aesthetic life-view judges a life by what it is like to experience, rather than by duty or by God. A lives this way, and the recipe above is what it amounts to.
- This essay gives the three spheres in one sentence. The aesthetic gives rest before the deep opposition of sin has been asserted. The ethical is strict and hard. The religious gives rest only after that opposition has been seen in its full terror.
- A calls the tragic a motherly love that soothes, and the religious a fatherly love.

### Freedom, order and the exit door

- A's freedom is the absence of ties. It is the exit door in every relationship.
- A condemns friendship, marriage and office, each because it makes you "several" and costs you your freedom.
- The notes say "Free vs order". "Order" is the ethical, Judge William's side.
- The notes object that there is meaning in the struggle and that an exit at the first difficulty is a slippery slope. That objection is the Judge's position almost word for word.
- The Judge's marriage essay in volume 2 argues that commitment is what lets first love last and that the ethical is the more beautiful life. The objection is where the book is going.
- "Happiness is not satisfaction" is Kraal's distinction. A does not promise happiness. He promises never being bored and never being trapped, and satisfaction in that sense is what the recipe delivers.

### Camus and French existentialism

- Camus is Kraal's forward reference.
- Meursault, the narrator of Camus's *The Stranger* (1942), is indifferent to his mother's death, his lover and his own trial. This is the "apathetic, pessimistic" attitude Kraal meant, and it rhymes with satisfaction found inside unhappiness.
- Camus refused both labels, nihilist and existentialist. His word was the **absurd**, and *The Myth of Sisyphus* argues against nihilism.
- Meursault does not cultivate his indifference the way A cultivates arbitrariness. He simply has it. So the parallel is a mood, not a method.
- French existentialism means Sartre and de Beauvoir, with Camus usually filed beside them. The course reaches it in its last week, December 4 and 7.
- French existentialism takes the single individual, choice, anxiety and the refusal of a system from Kierkegaard. It drops God. Lecture 1's clarification on the label covers the rest.

### Ancient and modern tragedy

- A distinguishes ancient and modern tragedy by where the hero's downfall comes from.
- In ancient tragedy, the individual, however freely he moves, rests in **substantial ties**: the state, the family and fate.
- The ancient hero's action is only partly his own. It has a share of suffering in it, "as much event as action". This is why ancient tragedy has a chorus, a monologue and an epic element.
- So the ancient hero's downfall is a suffering as well as a deed.
- The modern hero is reflected out of every immediate relation to state, kin and fate, often even out of his own past. Situation and character dominate. Everything is exhausted in dialogue, with no epic foreground and no epic remainder.
- On page 143, the modern hero "stands and falls entirely on his own deeds".
- The notes' "the protagonist causes the incident" is right as a description of the modern tendency. A's point is that this tendency, pushed all the way, destroys the tragic.

### Tragic guilt

- Aristotle requires the hero to have **hamartia**.
- In Greek tragedy the action lies between doing and suffering, so the guilt does too. That ambiguity is the tragic collision.
- The tragic lies between two extremes. With no guilt, the collision goes slack.
- With absolute guilt, the hero no longer interests us tragically. His aesthetic guilt has become ethical guilt, he is simply bad, and evil has no aesthetic interest. Sin is not an aesthetic element.
- The modern age errs by loading the hero's whole life onto his shoulders as his own deed. It will not hear of an unhappy childhood as an excuse.
- The result, A says, is that "as the age loses the tragic, it gains despair." The courage to be one's own creator is an illusion.
- The bitterest pain is repentance. It has ethical, not aesthetic, reality, and it does not want to be watched. That is why putting repentance on stage is a mistake.

### Antigone, Hamlet and Faust

- Antigone is the principal example of ancient tragedy because Sophocles' trilogy, *Oedipus at Colonus*, *Oedipus the King* and *Antigone*, turns on inherited guilt.
- Inherited guilt is guilt that is guilt and yet not guilt. It passes down the house of Labdacus through piety.
- In *Antigone* the guilt concentrates on one act, burying her brother against the king's ban. Taken alone, that act would be a modern subject.
- What makes it Greek is that Oedipus's fate echoes through it, so that "not an individual but a little world" goes under.
- Hamlet is in A's text, for a precise reason: "Hamlet is so tragic because he suspects his mother's crime." Suspicion is anxiety, and anxiety, A says, belongs to the modern tragic.
- Faust is Kraal's example, not A's. Goethe's Faust, with the scholar's pact and Gretchen, is discussed elsewhere in the book.
- The Faust in this essay is Grabbe's play *Faust and Don Juan*, which A names as a work "built on evil". It is A's example of what goes wrong when guilt becomes absolute.

### Absolute and relative

- The notes' paragraph on absolute and relative has the right passage but the wrong lesson.
- A says every individual, however original, is the child of God, of his age, his people, his family and his friends. Only there does it have its truth.
- If, in all that relativity, the individual wants to be **the absolute**, it becomes ridiculous.
- If it gives up that claim and is content to be **relative**, it has the tragic, "even if it were the happiest individual — indeed I would say the individual is only happy when it has the tragic."
- The passage is about guilt and belonging, not about knowledge. A is not saying that some views are objective and some subjective and that one should balance them.
- A is saying that a person who denies he is shaped, and partly answerable, through his family and his time is comic. A person who accepts it gets the tragic, which A calls a mildness and a healing. That is item 3 of the recipe.
- So "balance" is the wrong word. A's position is not a middle. It is a preference, on aesthetic grounds, for the ancient, relative self over the modern self-creator.
- The notes' "there are some objective views" is a different question, about truth, which this essay never raises.
- The comic image A uses is a word stuck as an adverb in one case that suddenly demands to be declined through all the cases.

### The spirit of the age

- **Zeitgeist** means the spirit of the age, its characteristic mood. It is Kraal's word for A's diagnosis of the age.
- A says "the whole age tends toward the comic" and speaks of the age's consciousness.
- Kraal can ask A's diagnosis. Existence is undermined by doubt, and isolation is growing, which the mania for associations proves.
- Everyone wants to rule, and nobody wants the responsibility.
- The age is more melancholy than Aristophanes' Greece, and therefore more deeply in despair.

### What the reading adds for Monday's lecture: sorrow, pain and A's modern Antigone

- Ancient tragedy has deeper sorrow and less pain. Modern tragedy has more pain and less sorrow.
- The reason is that pain is reflection on suffering, and sorrow has something substantial in it.
- A's image is a child watching an adult suffer.
- A's own modern Antigone keeps everything as in Sophocles, except that the secret of Oedipus is known to no one but her.
- Anxiety, not sorrow, is her organ. A calls her "sorrow's bride".
- She falls in love, and the beloved's pressing for the secret is the collision. She can only confess her love in the moment she dies.
- This is the figure the Ask Kraal question is about. She now looks like the Antigone of "The Unhappiest One" too.
