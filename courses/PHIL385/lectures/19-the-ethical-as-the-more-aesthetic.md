## Clarifications

### Why marriage is more beautiful in B’s account

- For Kraal, the ethical gives immediate romantic passion a more beautiful form. This is how he reads B’s argument that the ethical is more aesthetic.
- B calls marriage the "transfiguration" of first love. Marriage keeps that love and makes it "even more beautiful than it". As B puts it, "really it is marriage that is poetic".
- Kraal draws the connection to Plato. In Book VI of the *Republic*, the Form of the Good is the most important thing to know. Just and beautiful things benefit us through their relation to it.
- In Kraal’s reading of B, the good is the ethical. The beautiful draws its value from the good, so marriage offers more beauty even by A’s standard. B does not name Plato in the assigned text.

### Kraal’s two comparisons

| Example | Aesthetic life | Ethical life |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Children | A person dies childless and alone. | A person dies surrounded by people they love. |
| Confinement | A person conceals their secrets and relies on deception. | A person shares their most private thoughts openly with their lover. |

- In each case, Kraal argues that the ethical life is the good one. “Confinement” is the lecture’s term and most likely names the self shut in with its secrets. B does not use that word in the essay.

### What B says about children

- B calls children "a blessing". He says that if he were a painter, he would paint only the poor woman trading in the square with her baby in her arms.
- B says children humble proud parents. They connect a person to a future and a past.
- Married people console themselves that their children and grandchildren will "close our eyes" and mourn them. B says they speak "with a rare Sunday flourish".
- B rejects marrying for children or for a home as one-sided reasons. For him, marriage must have no "why". Kraal’s deathbed example compares the two ways of living.
- The closest B comes to picturing A dying alone is when he says A’s life will end with conscience recalling its record "of wasted possibilities". Elsewhere B pictures A as "alone in one's kayak", then alone with his care and "alone with one's despair".

### Why openness matters in love

- B calls the first attitude "secretiveness". He says openheartedness, uprightness and "publicity" between the lovers sustain love. He calls publicity "the life-principle of love".
- Love requires giving oneself, which means going "out of" oneself. Secretiveness keeps a person closed off and kills love.
- B calls it "not love but the coquetry of the seducer" when a man impresses a girl from a distance and she falls in love with the image she has made of him. This fits Johannes’s method in "The Seducer's Diary", although B does not name Johannes.
- B gives the example of A’s imaginary castle, where A’s wife lives in a separate wing. Their relationship depends on "secretiveness, mystification, refined coquetry".
- B says it takes courage to reveal oneself as one truly is. He calls privacy from outsiders "the absolute condition" for the aesthetic in marriage. He asks for openness between the spouses.

### Marriage as promise keeping

- Kraal says marriage stands for the ethical because it involves "Promise keeping". B’s own terms are vow, intention and duty.
- At the wedding the Church "exacts a vow". Kraal dates the Christian vow to around 1100.
- The vow expresses the intention. It turns the will inward and gives married love a "law of motion", or a direction in which to develop.
- B calls this development from within an inner history. First love lacks that history.
- B says the language of duty uses the future tense because a promise binds who the couple will be.
- A’s "no mishmash" objection questions what it means "to pledge oneself to love", where the obligation ends and who can enforce it.

### Why Kraal says A does not love Cordelia

- Kraal reads "The Seducer's Diary" as A’s own work. Cordelia appears in the Diary, outside B’s letter. The [lectures 13–15 page](13-17-seducers-diary-and-diapsalmata.md) covers the Diary.
- B distinguishes love from lust by "the stamp of the eternal". Kraal applies this test to A’s interest in Cordelia. He says A’s interest is limited in time, so it fails B’s test for love.
- B says A thinks any girl should count herself lucky to be his sweetheart "even for a week". A keeps finding the illusion of first love in new girls, which "betrays as much desperation as depravity".
- In the Diary, Johannes loses interest when Cordelia desires him completely.

### Kraal’s five steps from love to the will

1. **Love defines marriage.** B calls the "substance" of marriage love, or "the being in love" (Owen p. 399, PDF p. 348).
2. **Love differs from lust.** Love carries "the stamp of the eternal" (Owen p. 393, PDF p. 342).
3. **Love includes the sensual.** Romantic love is "based essentially on the sensual" (Owen p. 392, PDF p. 342).
   - First love is "not simply sensual" (Owen p. 406, PDF p. 352).
   - The sensual becomes lust when it is cut off from the eternal. B calls the sensual on its own "the instantaneous" because it seeks instant satisfaction.
4. **Love claims eternity.** First love, including its sensual side, presupposes itself back "into all eternity" and forward into all eternity (PDF p. 352).
   - This is why the diagram says romantic love seeks to become conjugal love, meaning married love.
5. **The will gives romantic love a lasting form.** The intention and decision in marriage are "a determination of the will" (PDF p. 343).
   - The lovers’ thanks, intention and obligation give their love "an absolute stamp of eternity" (Owen p. 411, PDF p. 358).
   - The Church requires a vow and the intention "makes the individual free" (Owen p. 432, PDF p. 376).
   - The passage on duty gives another reference for this step (Owen p. 468, PDF pp. 408–409).

### The note about civil marriage

- The note attached to step 1 says a civil marriage, one without religious officials, does not "require love". Kraal’s diagram and B’s account use marriage in different senses. The note likely refers to the legal procedure, while B is explaining what makes a union a marriage.
- B says a shared life without love satisfies sensual desire or amounts to "an association, a partnership".
- In B’s account, the reflective age turns marriage into "a civil arrangement" for a period. A person can end it by notifying the authorities as if changing address. Such unions "are not marriages" in B’s sense because they lack the consciousness of the eternal.
- B does not discuss civil weddings as such. He says the Church does not ask if the couple love each other because it "takes it for granted".

### How first love lasts

- Romantic love already means to last forever. B calls its eternity "illusory" on its own because romantic love has no inner history.
- B says romantic love is non-historical and "goes astray or comes to a standstill" (PDF pp. 412–413). The intention and vow in marriage give it the eternity it claims.
- B says first love enters marriage "without being changed". He calls the resulting marriage "a higher concentricity", a larger circle around the same centre, and names the change "transfiguration".
- B normally calls the love that enters marriage “first love”. He calls the immediate love of old romances “romantic love”. Kraal’s diagram treats both as immediate love.

### Why some readers call B a Hegelian

- B uses Hegelian terms. He calls first love "an unreal An-sich", meaning the in-itself. He calls marriage "the immediacy which has mediacy in itself" and love a unity of "the universal and the singular".
- B imagines A asking him to avoid "any attempt at mediation". In Hegelian usage, mediation is the reconciliation of opposites.
- Hegel’s *Philosophy of Right* (1820) treats marriage as the first ethical relationship, in which love takes a lasting and lawful form. This resembles B’s argument.
- B does not name Hegel in the letter. He is alarmed by young men who repeat "de omnibus dubitandum", meaning doubt everything, a Hegelian slogan then in fashion in Copenhagen.
