## Clarifications

### Why marriage is more beautiful in B’s account

- Kraal’s statement of B’s major argument is that the ethical ends up more aesthetic, more beautiful, than the immediate, purely aesthetic passion of romantic love.
- B calls marriage the "transfiguration" of first love. It is the same love, kept and made more glorious, and "even more beautiful than it".
- B also says "really it is marriage that is poetic".
- The link to Plato is Kraal’s. B does not name Plato anywhere in the assigned text.
- In Book VI of Plato’s *Republic*, the Form of the Good is the most important thing to know, because just things, beautiful things and the rest are beneficial only through their relation to it.
- Kraal’s short form of this is that the beautiful derives its value from the good.
- Applied to the essay, the good is the ethical (from the lecture 18 notes), so the aesthetic gets its value from the ethical.
- A life that wants the most beauty therefore ends up in the ethical. That is why marriage wins even on A’s own terms.
- The "Author B" line under the Plato bullet in the notes marks B, not A, as the one who takes this Platonic line.

### Kraal’s two comparisons

- Kraal set an aesthetic life against an ethical life in a series of comparisons. Each time he argued that the ethical life is the good one.

| Example | Aesthetic life | Ethical life |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Children | A person dies childless and alone. | A person dies with the people they love around them. |
| Confinement | A person keeps their inner secrets shut in and relies on deception. | A person shares their biggest secrets openly with their lover. |

- “Confinement” is the lecture’s heading for this comparison. It most likely names the aesthetic side, a self shut in with its secrets. The word is not in the essay.

### What B says about children

- B calls children "a blessing". His example is a poor woman trading in the square with her baby in her arms. If he were a painter, he would paint her and nothing else.
- B adds that children humble proud parents. They connect a person to a future and a past.
- B does not argue that one should marry so as not to die alone.
- He quotes married people saying that their children and grandchildren will "close our eyes" and mourn them. He treats this as their consolation, said "with a rare Sunday flourish".
- He counts marrying for children or for a home among the one-sided "whys" he rejects, because marriage must have no "why".
- So the deathbed picture is Kraal’s comparison of two lives, not B’s reason for marrying.
- The closest B comes to A dying alone is his warning that A’s life will draw to its close with only the recollection of conscience and its record "of wasted possibilities".
- B also pictures A as "alone in one's kayak". He corrects it to alone with one’s care and "alone with one's despair".

### Why openness matters in love

- B’s word for the first picture in the confinement comparison is "secretiveness".
- His words for the second picture are openheartedness, uprightness and "publicity", which he calls "the life-principle of love".
- Secretiveness between the lovers is the death of love.
- His reason is that love is giving oneself, and one can give oneself only by going "out of" oneself. Secretiveness wants to remain in itself.
- B calls it "not love but the coquetry of the seducer" when a man makes an impression from a distance, so that the girl loves an image she made herself.
- This fits Johannes’s method in "The Seducer's Diary", although B does not name Johannes.
- A’s castle in the air, with his wife living in a separate wing, runs on "secretiveness, mystification, refined coquetry". It is B’s example of the first picture.
- B says it takes courage to reveal oneself as one truly is.
- B keeps one kind of secrecy. The couple’s secretiveness toward the outside world is "the absolute condition" for the aesthetic in marriage. So the openness he asks for is between husband and wife.

### Marriage as promise keeping

- "Promise keeping" is Kraal’s phrase for why marriage stands for the ethical. B’s own words for it are vow, intention and duty.
- At the wedding the Church "exacts a vow".
- The vow carries the intention, in which the will turns inward.
- B says this gives married love a "law of motion" (a direction it develops in over time) and an inner history (growth from within, not outward events), which first love lacks.
- B says the language of duty is in the future tense, to suggest the historical. A promise binds who the couple will be.
- A’s "no mishmash" objection attacks the same promise. He asks what it means "to pledge oneself to love", where its boundary is, and what power can compel it.
- Kraal’s Monday point, that the vow comes from Christianity around 1100, belongs with this.

### Why Kraal says A does not love Cordelia

- This is Kraal’s claim. A’s love for Cordelia is bound to a stretch of time and is not "eternal", so it is not really love.
- Cordelia is not in B’s letter. She is the girl of "The Seducer's Diary", which Kraal reads as A’s own work (see the [lectures 13–15 page](13-17-seducers-diary-and-diapsalmata.md)).
- The test Kraal applies is B’s. Love differs from lust by "the stamp of the eternal".
- B’s lines about A fit the claim. A thinks any girl should count herself lucky to be his sweetheart "even for a week".
- B also says A renews first love by finding its illusion in new girls, which "betrays as much desperation as depravity".
- In the Diary itself, Johannes loses interest once Cordelia desires him completely, so the affair has an end built in.

### Kraal’s five steps from love to the will

- The diagram is a box marked "Marriage" with five numbered steps under it.
- Owen’s page numbers are from his copy. The PDF pages are where each claim sits in your file.

1. **Love defines marriage.** B says marriage’s "substance" is love, "the being in love" (Owen p. 399, PDF p. 348).
2. **Love is not lust.** B says what separates them is "the stamp of the eternal" (Owen p. 393, PDF p. 342).
3. **Love is not empty of the sensuous, and the sensuous is not lust.** B grants that romantic love is "based essentially on the sensual" (Owen p. 392, PDF p. 342).
   - First love has the sensual in it but is "not simply sensual" (Owen p. 406, PDF p. 352).
   - What makes the sensual into lust is being cut off from the eternal. B calls the sensual on its own "the instantaneous", which wants instant satisfaction.
4. **Love, its sensuous side included, carries a stamp of the eternal.** B says first love presupposes itself back "into all eternity" and forward into all eternity (PDF p. 352).
5. **The change happens through the ethical will, the intention and the decision.** The change is romantic love becoming married love, as in the note under step 4. B says the true eternity needs "a determination of the will" (PDF p. 343). Owen gives three pages for this step.
   - The lovers’ thanks, intention and obligation give their love "an absolute stamp of eternity" (Owen p. 411, PDF p. 358).
   - The Church exacts a vow, and the intention "makes the individual free" (Owen p. 432, PDF p. 376).
   - The third is the duty passage (Owen p. 468, PDF pp. 408–409).

### The note about civil marriage

- A note at the top left of the diagram, with an arrow to step 1, says that a civil marriage, one without religious officials, does not "require love".
- The likeliest reading is a contrast. The law can marry two people without asking about love, while step 1 is B’s claim about what makes a union a marriage at all.
- The note is the lecture’s, not B’s. B does not discuss civil weddings as such.
- B says a shared life without love is only the satisfaction of sensual desire or "an association, a partnership".
- B does use the word civil for a marriage. The reflective age turns marriage into "a civil arrangement" for a period, ended by notifying the authority the way one gives notice of a change of address.
- B says such unions "are not marriages", because they lack the consciousness of the eternal.
- The Church does not ask the couple whether they love each other either, but B says that is because it "takes it for granted".

### Why romantic love seeks to become conjugal love

- A note under step 4, with an arrow up to it, says that because love carries a stamp of the eternal, romantic love will seek to transform into conjugal love. "Conjugal" means married.
- The reasoning is that romantic love already means to last forever. On its own, though, its eternity is "illusory", because it has no inner history.
- B says romantic love, being non-historical, "goes astray or comes to a standstill" (PDF pp. 412–413).
- Only the will in marriage, the intention and the vow, gives love the true eternity it claims. So romantic love has to become married love to be what it says it is.
- B would not call this a replacement. He says first love enters marriage "without being changed".
- First love is taken up into "a higher concentricity", a larger circle around the same centre, so nothing is lost. B’s word for the result is "transfiguration".
- B’s usual word for the love that enters marriage is “first love”. “Romantic love” is his name for the immediate love of the old romances. Both are immediate love, which is what the diagram means.

### Why some readers call B a Hegelian

- Owen marks this as an aside. Some readers take B, Judge William, to be a Hegelian.
- It is a reading of B, not his claim. B does not name Hegel in the letter.
- The reading rests on B’s vocabulary, which is Hegel’s. B calls first love "an unreal An-sich" (the in-itself), marriage "the immediacy which has mediacy in itself", and love a unity of "the universal and the singular".
- B expects the label too. He imagines A begging him to keep away from "any attempt at mediation". Mediation is the Hegelians’ word for reconciling opposites.
- The reading also rests on the shape of B’s case. Hegel’s *Philosophy of Right* (1820) makes marriage the first ethical relationship, love given a lasting and lawful form, which is close to B’s marriage.
- Against the label, B is alarmed by the young men who cry "de omnibus dubitandum" (doubt everything). That was a slogan of the Hegelian philosophy then in fashion in Copenhagen.
