## Clarifications

### What makes guilt tragic

> **Definition.** Ancient tragic guilt is aesthetic. It lies between doing and suffering, it is inherited through piety, and it is guilt and yet not guilt.

- The friend's "they ended up that way" is a fair gloss.
- A calls it *inherited guilt* and compares it to original sin.
- On p. 147, A describes modern guilt as ethical. The isolated individual is his own creator, so his guilt is sin and his pain is repentance. With that, the tragic is cancelled.
- The spectator's response becomes "help yourself and heaven will help you," which is the loss of compassion. For A, compassion is the real expression of the tragic.
- The notes have the verdict right: A finds modern tragedy aesthetically deficient exactly to the extent that it is ethically dominated.

### Sorrow and pain

- The dictionary lookup in the notes gives *regret* as the synonym for sorrow. In A's vocabulary that is exactly the wrong neighbour.
- A's sorrow, *Sorg*, is the mild, deep, unreflected grief of ancient tragedy.
- Regret, or repentance, *Anger*, is what A names as the bitterest *pain*. He says it has ethical, not aesthetic, reality.
- Sorrow is the ancient side of A's distinction, and regret is the modern side.
- Sorrow goes with fate, family and innocence. Pain goes with reflection, guilt and the self.

> **Watch out.** An exam option that pairs "sorrow" with "regret" or with "your own fault" is wrong.

- The notes say modern sorrow "is painful because you caused it." A's formulation is sharper, and it is the one to memorise.
- In ancient tragedy the sorrow is deeper and the pain less.
- In modern tragedy the pain is greater and the sorrow less.
- Sorrow has something substantial in it. Pain is reflection on the suffering.
- A's image is a child watching an adult suffer. The child is not reflected enough to feel pain, yet feels infinitely deep sorrow. That is Greek sorrow, mild and deep at once.
- An adult watching a child suffer feels more pain and less sorrow, because the thought of guilt has entered.
- The section ends with a pairing. True tragic sorrow needs a moment of guilt and a moment of transparency.
- True tragic pain needs a moment of innocence and a moment of obscurity.

### How fate can heal, p. 144

- A finds "a melancholy and a healing" in the tragic.
- Aesthetically, the tragic is to human life what divine grace and mercy are. It is a motherly love that soothes the troubled.
- The ethical, by contrast, is strict and hard. The religious is a fatherly love that has the ethical in it, but softened.
- The notes gloss the healing as "you can make someone else responsible". That is too crude, because the healing is not blame-shifting.
- The healing is that the guilt is shared, inherited through the family and the age, so the individual is not left alone with it as its sole author.
- A says the individual is only happy when it has the tragic. In trying to win itself the supernatural way, as our age does, it loses itself and becomes comic.

> **Watch out.** "Absurd" in the notes is the friend's word. A's word is *ridiculous*, or comic.

- A's target is claiming to be *the absolute* inside all that relativity, not asserting that something is objectively true. This is the same correction as the one to your own note from Friday.

### Nietzsche and "genealogical"

- The link to Nietzsche is Kraal's, and it is a good one.
- Nietzsche's *amor fati*, or love of fate, first appears in *The Gay Science* (1882). It means seeing as beautiful what is necessary in things.
- That is forty years after A's essay. So the notes' "Nietzsche says this after Kierkegaard" is right, and the link is a parallel, not a debt.
- "Genealogical" is Nietzsche's word too, from *On the Genealogy of Morals* (1887).
- Kraal seems to mean that A traces how the concept of tragic guilt changed as the age changed, from the substantial ties of Greece to the isolated individual.
- That is a history of a concept rather than a definition of it. Take "genealogical" as Kraal's framing.

### What A and Kraal say about Christ

- A says two things about Christ, in two places.
- First, Christ's appearance is in one sense the deepest tragedy, because he came in the fullness of time and bore the sin of the whole world.
- Second, Christ's life is never called a tragedy, because it exceeds aesthetic categories. The unity of absolute innocence and absolute guilt is metaphysical, not aesthetic.
- In Christ, suffering is absolute because it is absolutely free action, and action is absolute suffering because it is absolute obedience.

> **Watch out.** "The crucifixion is the turning point from Greek to modern tragedy" is Kraal's reading, not a sentence in the text.

- Kraal's reading is defensible. Christianity brings in sin, responsibility and the reflected individual, which is what A says the modern age has and Greece lacked.
- If the exam asks what A says, the answer is the two points above, and "absolutely free" belongs to the second.

### Sophocles' Antigone and Kraal's example

- In Sophocles, Polynices dies attacking Thebes. Creon forbids his burial, but Antigone buries him. Creon condemns her to be walled up alive.
- Antigone's defence is the unwritten laws of the gods against a king's decree.
- A's use of the story is that, as an isolated clash of duty and decree, it would be modern.
- What makes it Greek is that Oedipus's fate echoes through it: "Not an individual who goes under, but a little world."
- Kraal's exercise was "what if Antigone's suffering came from her own decisions about the burial rather than from fate." That is the modern *tendency*, and A does name it.
- A says that if you take the burial as an isolated fact, a collision between sisterly love and an arbitrary ban, you have "an entirely modern tragic subject."
- Kraal's hypothetical Antigone is not A's Antigone.

### A's modern Antigone

- A's own modern Antigone, in the second half of the essay, does the opposite of Kraal's exercise. He keeps the inherited guilt and adds reflection.
- Everything is as in Sophocles, except that the secret of Oedipus, that he killed his father and married his mother, is known to nobody but her.
- Oedipus lives honoured, and she carries the secret alone.
- She does not know whether her father himself knew. A calls that "the unrest in her sorrow, the ambiguity in her pain."
- Anxiety, not sorrow, is her organ. A says anxiety belongs essentially to the modern tragic, because it looks to the past or the future, never the present.
- The collision is that she falls "mortally in love," and the beloved presses her for the secret. He adjures her by her love for her father at Oedipus's grave, working against himself without knowing it.
- She can only confess her love in the moment she dies. A's image is Epaminondas, who left the arrow in the wound until he heard the battle was won.
- So she is "sorrow's bride" and *virgo mater*, carrying the secret under her heart.
- Her scene is not outward but inward, "a scene of spirit."
- In "The Unhappiest One", this is the figure who has turned her back on hope and lives on memory's fidelity.
