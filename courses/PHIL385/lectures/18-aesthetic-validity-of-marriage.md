## Clarifications

### What the essay is
- "The Aesthetic Validity of Marriage" is the first of B's two letters, which make up Part II of *Either/Or*. B is Judge William (Assessor Vilhelm in the Danish), a married public official, and the letter is addressed to A. Hong's edition spells it "The Esthetic Validity of Marriage".
- It is written as a letter, not a treatise, because the letter form lets B speak in "admonitory and urgent tones" and stops A from treating it as a general debate.
- B's two tasks, stated near the start: to show the aesthetic significance of marriage, and to show how the aesthetic can be sustained against life's obstacles. Your "maintains the quality status over time" is the second task.
- Kraal's framing: even if you play the game of life on the aesthetic's own terms, the ethical wins, because marriage turns out more aesthetic than romantic love. B's own words for the conclusion: it is not true that marriage is respectable but tiresomely moral while love is poetry; "really it is marriage that is poetic".
- Full essay notes are on the [reading page](../readings/aesthetic-validity.md).

### The tone, and what B thinks A is doing with his life
- B says he loves A "like a son, a brother, a friend", with an aesthetic love, and also "with the fear and trembling of a religious love", because he sees how A has gone astray.
- The warning line: "I am warning you, therefore, not about the world but about yourself, and I am warning the world about you." He adds that he would warn a daughter against A.
- Wasted life: A's life "will be nothing but approach-runs"; conscience will one day unroll a record "not of genuine crimes, but of wasted possibilities"; A has stopped at destroying illusions, which is itself a new illusion, and "you accomplish nothing".
- A is not an enemy of marriage. He mocks it with irony and only observes it; what draws him is "the first rapture of love"; he "loves the accidental"; he wants to play fate (the five-dollar bill for the poorhouse woman); what he lacks "entirely, is faith".

### The vow
- Kraal's point: the marriage vow comes from Christianity, around 1100. The Church made marriage a sacrament in the twelfth century. B himself says marriage belongs to Christianity: neither the pagan nations, for all the sensuality of the Orient and the beauty of Greece, nor Judaism perfected it.
- In the essay the Church "exacts a vow", and the vow, the intention, is what gives marital love its "law of motion" and so its history.

### Love and lust
- B's line: "what distinguishes love from lust is its having the stamp of the eternal". The sensual is "the instantaneous", and it tries to make the moment of pleasure "into a little eternity".
- On A: B says A's "clever head" has conspired with his "lust", so that he renews the illusion of first love by finding it in new girls, which "betrays as much desperation as depravity".

### The argument, and "love is not the true victor"
- Your three steps are Kraal's reconstruction and they are right. Tightened: P1, if love is inconsistent with the ethical (duty), and duty always turns up eventually in a love relation, then love cannot last. P2, if love cannot last, love loses. C, if love is inconsistent with the ethical, love loses.
- B's own sentence: "If duty is the enemy of love, and love cannot defeat this enemy, then love is not the true victor."
- One fix to your last background bullet. B does not conclude that one should live by duty instead of love. The argument is aimed at A: A praises love as the highest thing, so A of all people must deny that duty is love's enemy, or else admit that his love loses and that he has "disparaged love and divested it of its majesty". B's own view is that there is no split: "You regard duty as the enemy of love, I regard it as its friend"; "duty here is just one thing, truly to love"; and the aesthetic, the ethical and the religious are "the three great allies". The reason not to follow love alone is that love held apart from duty is despair, not that duty outranks love.
- Your motivation-versus-determination comparison is close. B's word for the determination side is the "intention" (the resolve made in the vow), which turns marital love inward and gives it "inner history".
- "Do it or do not do it, you will regret both" is A's own line from the Diapsalmata. B says it is justified only for someone who cannot see the three spheres as allies, and then "life is without meaning".

### Your personal note
- B agrees with half of it. He admits that "far more natures are formed for conquering than for possessing", that A's kind of nature is the natural one, and that possessing (going downhill) takes more strength than conquering (going uphill).
- He denies the other half: A's "that's just how you are" is not a given. A keeps every mood "as a constant possibility" and so is answerable for it. B's "society" is not the point; the point is time, since possessing is the acquired, and "the great thing isn't the original but the acquired".

### From Owen's notes of the same lecture
- Owen's shared page (on the [lecture 19 page](19-the-ethical-as-the-more-aesthetic.md)) covers this lecture too, and his Background and argument match yours. These points add to your page.
- Owen writes the argument as six lines. He splits your P1 into its two conditions and adds a sixth: B argues he can vindicate love by joining it with the ethical. That sixth line is B's own answer, duty as love's "friend", as in the fix above.
- Owen glosses premise 2, that the ethical always comes up eventually in a love relation, as "the after 6 months things". The six months is the lecture's example, not the essay's. In the essay B tells A that A thinks duty must come into view "sooner or later", in romantic love as well as in married love.
- Owen adds that B is not angry with A. B says it is "impossible really to be angry with you", because A's evil has some good nature and childishness mixed in.
- Owen also has B telling A he still has time to change. B's lines are "You are still young" and "your life is passing", and he hopes A may one day find "a centre" for his eccentric movements.
- Owen, like you, gives page 443 for B's claim that A does not understand love. Owen's page numbers run about 50 to 60 higher than your PDF's file pages, so 443 is probably PDF pages 386–387, not the love-and-lust lines on PDF pages 342 and 396. This is inferred from the page offsets.
- On PDF pages 386–387 B tells A that he fears love ends once "the enigma" is gone, while B holds that only then does love begin, and that one truly loves only when one knows what one loves.
