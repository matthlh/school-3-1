Judge William (**B**) writes both letters in Part II of *Either/Or* to the young aesthete **A**. In the first letter, B asks how a couple can keep first love alive. In the second, he asks how a person like A can commit to a way of life at all. The shared idea is that commitment gives a person something they can do with their feelings and abilities over time.

## The Aesthetic Validity of Marriage

[Full reading](readings/aesthetic-validity.md) · [Lectures 18](lectures/18-aesthetic-validity-of-marriage.md), [19](lectures/19-the-ethical-as-the-more-aesthetic.md) and [20](lectures/20-aesthetic-validity-of-marriage-wrap-up.md)

- **B's two tasks.** Show why marriage is beautiful in the first place and how it can stay beautiful through time and hardship. He warns A about wasting his own life on beginnings. He writes out of affection for A and worries about what A is becoming (PDF pp. 334–336, 341).
- **Love and the vow.** The substance of marriage is already being in love. Love has the **stamp of the eternal**, the sense that it should last. Lust seeks satisfaction in the instant. The couple thanks God for first love and freely promises to care for it. The vow gives them a way to live out the feeling through time (PDF pp. 342–343, 348, 353, 376).
- **Duty is love's friend.** Responsibilities eventually arise in any lasting relationship. If those demands defeated love, A's idea of love would make it unable to last. B says that patience and fidelity are ways of loving. He calls the **aesthetic, the ethical and the religious** the three great allies (PDF pp. 408–409).
- **The beauty is acquired.** A romance often ends once the lovers win each other. Marriage gives them an **inner history**, the trust and fidelity they develop while already together. The knight waits fifteen years for his moment. The husband lives those years with his beloved and makes them part of his love. B says the husband **preserves time in eternity** (PDF pp. 377–378, 403–404).
- **Marriage has its purpose within itself.** B examines building character, having children and getting a home as reasons for marrying. Children can be a blessing and a home a responsibility. His point is that the shared life should be worth choosing in its own right.
- **Privacy and openness go together.** B values privacy around outsiders and honesty between spouses. A's castle with separate wings keeps the couple mysterious to each other. B thinks love deepens once they can reveal themselves.
- **The reflective age's two paths.** The **immoral path** keeps passion and gives up marriage. The **respectable path**, the marriage of reason, keeps marriage and gives up love. Both give up the eternal. In this letter, **egoistic melancholy** fears commitment for one's own sake. **Sympathetic melancholy** fears it for the other person's sake.

### Details tied to the examples

- The Page in Mozart's *Figaro* shows desire before it settles on one person. First love has a particular beloved and unites freedom with the sense of being irresistibly drawn to that person.
- Husbands who secretly wish they were still engaged are B's real traitors to marriage. B reserves **habit** for bad, unfree repetition. Faithfulness can be freely renewed every day.
- B contrasts the **conqueror**, who wins someone, with the **possessor**, who lives out the relationship through humility and patience. He closes with *Dixi et animam meam liberavi* and the story of a cottager sharing a meal with a giant after a prayer.

## Equilibrium — choice, Hegel and good and evil

Imagine you damage a friend’s bike while borrowing it. You could tell them what happened or say it was already broken.

1. **The first either/or** is whether to let good and evil matter in how you live. B calls taking up that responsibility the **choice to will**. Treating the bike incident only as a question of which story will work illustrates the aesthetic’s **indifference** to good and evil.
2. **The absolute either/or** is the choice you face once you take that responsibility seriously. You can still choose to tell the truth or lie. The first choice makes that distinction matter to you (PDF pp. 423–424, 428).
3. **Hannay p. 524.** You can compare how likely each story is to be believed. That is a **relative difference**. B says thought cannot establish the absolute difference between good and evil in the same way. Taking responsibility for acting on what is right takes a decision of the will. B says the good exists through his willing it in this sense. He still gives good and evil **absolute validity**. Preferring the easier story has no power to make it good (PDF pp. 456–457).

- **Kraal’s reason objection.** Suppose you see immediately that you should tell your friend. You still have to choose to trust that judgment and act on it.
- **Hegel and B.** For Hegel, freedom becomes actual and recognized in **family, civil society and the state**. Hegel calls marriage an ethical tie. His system has **Logic, Nature and Spirit**. B’s spheres for thought are **logic, nature and history**. A historical explanation can show where the choice fits after the fact. The person still had to make it.

## Equilibrium — how despair leads to the ethical

1. **The dependence.** A musician makes performing the one thing that gives their life worth. They may feel great while they are able to perform. Their life still depends on a condition they can lose. B already calls this **despair**.
2. **The loss.** An injury takes away their ability to perform. The injury exposes the dependence that was there before. A’s **thought-despair** is a version of this in which he sees that every source of satisfaction can give out, even though he has suffered no particular loss (PDF pp. 436–438).
3. **The choice.** The musician can deliberately face the dependence and ask how they will live this life even if they never perform again. That is what B asks A to do when he says **Despair!** The **crossroads** is the point where they have to decide how to live. B’s answer is to **choose oneself** as a free person responsible for that life (PDF pp. 423, 445, 447).

- **Finite and absolute despair.** The musician could make recovery or a new success the sole basis for their worth. That is finite despair. B calls it **absolute despair** when they give up putting their whole life at the mercy of any one gain or loss. They can still value music and work toward recovery. Choosing oneself in one’s **eternal validity** means taking responsibility for the actual life one has, through whatever happens to those abilities (PDF pp. 447–451, 454–455).
- **Doubt and despair.** Doubt concerns thought. Despair concerns the whole personality. B calls despair the true starting point for finding the absolute and says it can be salvation when it is **willed infinitely**.
- **Melancholy.** In this letter B calls it **hysteria of the spirit**. The person senses a need for a deeper way to live and holds back from willing it. Nero tries another amusement and gets bored again.
- **Repentance.** Choosing the actual self includes taking responsibility for one’s guilt and facing the history that connects one with other people and God. B calls repentance the one expression of love of God.

## Equilibrium — what an ethical life looks like

- **Equilibrium is the return of the aesthetic.** Interests, pleasures and talents still have a place in the person's life. The person decides how to use them. B calls this change a **transfiguration**. The gambler who can wait an hour before obeying an urge shows **continuity** through a changing mood.
- **Three ways to miss the concrete self.** The man like **Narcissus** treats himself as complete and gazes at himself. The **Greek paragon** develops virtues in isolation from civic life. The **mystic** seeks God by withdrawing from the life and people he is responsible for. B's objections to the mystic are importunity, softness and deceit. The mystic repents out of himself. Ethical repentance takes the person back into their life.
- **Beauty in a life.** A calls something beautiful when its purpose, or **teleology**, lies within it. B applies that to a person who gives their life direction through actions over time. Aesthetic qualities are how a person immediately is. The ethical is how they become who they become.
- **Tasks and duty.** The ethical person starts with their actual situation and asks what they can do with it. B uses the Danish *Pligt* for duty and connects it to what lies upon the person. **My duty** is a universal obligation made particular in my circumstances. B's example of learning ten lines from Balle's textbook shows the **intensity** of duty. One task can be enough to feel the full seriousness of it.
- **Work and vocation.** Work is a shared human task. Everyone has a particular **vocation**. One can do one's work fully, while what it **accomplishes** depends partly on things beyond one's control.
- **Marriage.** For B, someone who marries realizes something universally human. Ethics gives that general principle. Choosing whom to marry belongs to the person. In the story of the uncommon girl, the spouse's qualities matter, but the **relationship is the absolute**.
- **Woman and time.** The Dutch orientalist's wife blows a grain of snuff off a text, ending his search for the meaning of the dot. B uses this story to claim that woman clarifies finiteness and man chases after infinity.
- **Friendship and revelation.** Friendship requires full agreement in a **positive life-view** with an ethical basis. B notes that Aristotle linked friendship to justice. B's wider claim is that it is every man's duty **to become revealed**. A's habit of concealing himself makes him a mystery even to himself.
- **Ordinary life.** B writes as a **witness**, drawing on his life as a husband and judge. He calls the truly extraordinary person the truly ordinary one because realizing what is universally human in a particular life is itself an achievement.
