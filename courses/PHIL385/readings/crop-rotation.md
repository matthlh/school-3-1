## The case for crop rotation

### Title and epigraph

- The full heading is "Crop Rotation. An attempt at a theory of social prudence." The Hong edition titles the same essay "Rotation of Crops: A Venture in a Theory of Social Prudence".
- This is A's seventh piece and the last before "The Seducer's Diary". It is the closest the book comes to giving A's own programme for living.
- In the epigraph from Aristophanes' *Plutus*, Chremylos says one finally gets sick of everything, of love, music, honour, courage, ambition and command. His slave Karion names a food in reply to each item.
- **Satiety** is the note the essay opens on.

### Boredom as a cause of evil

- A starts from a principle because experienced people say that is sensible. His principle is that all people are boring.
- The principle has the repelling force of "the negative", which in Hegel is the principle of motion. A says that whoever has it behind him has infinite momentum.
- Then comes the central sentence, that boredom is a root of all evil.
- The Danish says *a* root, *en Rod*. The English copy and the notes say *the* root. Either is acceptable on an exam, but the original hedges.

> **Watch out.** A's central sentence is that boredom is a root of all evil. The Danish *en Rod* says *a* root; the common English rendering says *the* root. Either is accepted, but the original hedges.

- A uses children as evidence. As long as children are amused they are good, and they turn unruly when boredom is on its way.
- So a nursemaid is hired partly on whether she can amuse the children. She would be dismissed for failing at it, whatever her other virtues.
- A calls the nursemaid's employment the one relationship in life where aesthetics gets its due. Nobody gets to divorce a boring wife, depose a boring king, exile a boring priest, sack a boring minister or execute a boring journalist.
- A says habit and boredom have taken over, and that is why the world goes backwards.

### A's history of boredom

- The gods were bored, so they created human beings. Adam was bored alone, so Eve was created.
- Boredom then entered the world and grew with the population. It went from Adam alone, to Adam and Eve, to the family with Cain and Abel, and then to the peoples en masse.
- To amuse themselves, people built a tower into the clouds. A calls the idea as boring as the tower was tall.
- The people were scattered, the way people now travel abroad, and they stayed bored.
- A says bread and circuses held off Rome's fall. Today, instead of finding a diversion, people hasten the fall by convening an assembly of the estates and paying down the national debt through economies.
- A imagines Denmark borrowing millions and spending it all on public amusement, so that everything is free, from the theatre to the funeral. Copenhagen would become another Athens, the world's wealthy would move there and the emperor of Persia would be kidnapped and sold to the Turk if Persia replaced him.
- In A's proposal, the person who tells the best joke becomes king. A then catches himself and says his wisdom is not for everyone. He wants no disciples and would at most whisper it to one person on his deathbed.
- A says man is called a social animal but is really a beast of prey. He calls the talk of society inherited hypocrisy and calculated cunning.

## Idleness and boredom

### Who gets bored

- For A, "boring" can mean one who bores others or one who bores himself.
- Those who bore others are the **plebs**, the crowd. Those who bore themselves are the elect, the nobility.
- The odd thing is that people who never bore themselves bore everyone else, and people who bore themselves are entertaining. The busy are the worst.
- The nobility bore themselves so thoroughly that even at the peak of boredom they entertain the initiated. They either die of boredom, passively, or shoot themselves out of curiosity, actively.
- That line is the quote in your notes. It describes the elect at their extreme and is not a rule for everyone.

### Why work fails as a remedy

- **Idleness** means doing nothing. **Boredom** is emptiness, and it can hit the busiest person alive.
- The proverb says idleness is a root of all evil and prescribes work. A calls this plebeian.
- A says idleness is a divine life as long as one is not bored. The Olympian gods lived in happy idleness.
- A beauty who neither sews nor spins nor irons nor reads nor plays is happy in idleness because she is not bored.
- A says a person with no sense for idleness has not risen to the human. A restless activity puts a man among the animals, who must always be moving.
- A accepts that "Idleness is the devil's pillow", but says the devil gets no time to lay his head on it if one is not bored.
- People think man's destiny is to work, so they oppose idleness to work. A thinks man's destiny is to amuse himself, so he opposes boredom to amusement.
- Work annuls idleness, its opposite, but cannot touch boredom. Only amusement annuls boredom, so one ought to amuse oneself.
- A says the busiest workers are the most boring, and that if they are not bored it is only because they have no idea what boredom is.

### Why A calls boredom demonic pantheism

- A writes, "Boredom is the demonic pantheism." **Pantheism** means fullness. Boredom is built on emptiness and is pantheistic for that very reason, since it is everywhere as nothing.
- If one stays in boredom, it is the evil. Annulled, it is true.
- Boredom "rests on the nothing that winds through existence". A compares its dizziness to looking into an infinite abyss.
- The English are boredom's paradigm nation. The travelling Englishman is a heavy, immovable marmot whose entire language is one interjection.
- Their opposite number, the apostles of empty enthusiasm who shout at everything, are just as empty.

## The two kinds of rotation

- Everyone who is bored cries for change. A agrees, but on principle. His word for it is crop rotation, and it has two senses.

### The vulgar rotation changes the soil

- The vulgar rotation changes the soil. A's examples progress from the country to the city, from one's country to abroad, from being "tired of Europe" to going to America, and then to dreaming of travelling from star to star.
- The same method trades porcelain for silver and then gold, or leads Nero to burn half of Rome to see Troy burn. A says the method cancels itself.
- A calls this **the bad infinity**, Hegel's term for an endless series that never arrives.
- A says Marcus Aurelius was wiser. It is in your power to live again by seeing things again as you once saw them, and that is what living again is.

### The true rotation changes the method

- A's own is the true rotation. It keeps the soil and changes the method of cultivation and the crop. In life, it means varying how one uses the same surroundings.
- Here lies **the principle of limitation**, which A calls the only saving principle in the world. The more you limit yourself, the more inventive you become.

> **Definition.** **The principle of limitation** is A's only saving principle: the more you limit yourself, the more inventive you become. The true rotation keeps the same soil and varies the method, against the vulgar rotation that just changes the soil.

- A prisoner for life is endlessly inventive, and a spider is great entertainment to him.
- A's schooldays examples are a fly under a nutshell, a fly shut into a hole in the desk and watched through paper, and the monotonous drip from the roof.
- The contrast is **extensive against intensive**. Relief is sought not by extensity but by intensity.

## How to use the true rotation

### Remember and forget deliberately

- Every change of method falls under the relation between remembering and forgetting, the two currents all of life moves in. The whole art is to have both in your power.
- A advises throwing hope overboard first. Only then does one begin to live artistically, since as long as one hopes one cannot limit oneself.
- He calls hope a faithless pilot. Prometheus gave people hope in place of the immortals' foreknowledge, and A treats it as one of his dubious gifts. This is the same A who wrote "The Unhappiest One", and hope is again the thing to be rid of.
- *Nil admirari*, Horace's "wonder at nothing", is the real wisdom of life.
- No moment should mean so much that one cannot forget it at will, and each should mean enough that one can recall it at will.
- Childhood remembers best and forgets most easily. **Poetic remembering** is really forgetting, because the experience has already shed everything painful.
- A advises never enjoying to the last drop. Exhausting the pleasure leaves one unable to remember or forget it, only able to suffer a surfeit.
- When enjoyment sweeps one off, one should stop and remember. A also says never to set all sails.
- A says no weapon is as dangerous as the art of remembering, and remarks that the police only ban concealed weapons.
- Forgetting, as A means it, differs from forgetfulness. A applies it even to the pleasant, since the pleasant, once past, carries the unpleasantness of longing.
- He says every misfortune can be flattered, since even Cerberus took honey cakes.
- A describes forgetting as "the scissors with which one cuts away what one cannot use, under the highest supervision of memory". Remembering and forgetting are then identical.
- A calls that artificially produced identity the Archimedean point that lifts the world. It lets one play shuttlecock with the whole of existence.
- A measures a person's elasticity by his power to forget.

### Use moods and chance

- A says one must vary oneself constantly, and that is the secret.
- Moods cannot be produced at will, but prudence teaches one to use the moment and to see a mood coming, the way a sailor sees a squall.
- A calls teasing an excellent instrument of exploration.
- "The whole secret lies in arbitrariness." One enjoys not the thing itself but something quite different that one arbitrarily reads into it.
- A suggests watching only the middle of a play or reading the third part of a book. This can yield pleasure the author never intended.
- A was obliged to hear a man's philosophical chatter and was near despair. Then he noticed that the man sweated when he talked.
- A watched the sweat gather on the man's forehead, run down his nose and hang at the tip. From then on he could even egg the man on to lecture, just to watch.
- Baggesen objected to an otherwise decent man because nothing rhymed with his name.
- A advises making something accidental into the absolute and admiring it absolutely. He also advises treating all of life as a wager and constantly changing the eye one sees reality with.
- A says the degree of consistency shows whether one is an artist or a bungler.
- To arbitrariness inside corresponds the accidental outside. A recommends keeping an open eye for it and always being ready, since pleasures prepared a fortnight ahead mean little and the most trivial accident can become rich material.
- A says no theory can go further. Even the fullest theory is poverty next to what genius discovers on its own.

## Three commitments A would avoid

- All of this exists to keep one from running aground in any single relationship and to secure "perfect floating".

### Friendship

- A says a friend is not what philosophy calls the necessary other but the superfluous third.
- He names the rites of friendship, which are drinking to first names, opening a vein and mixing blood.
- The ancient mark of friendship is to want and not want the same things, which A calls extremely boring.
- A says one person can be nothing for another except in the way.
- This is not a life without people. It means always having more speed than the relationship, so that one can run from it.
- The farmer lets a field lie fallow, and social prudence does the same. Everything comes round again in a new form.

### Marriage and romantic love

- Lovers promise love for ever, which A considers easy and meaningless. Promising love "till Easter" or "till next May Day" would at least mean something one might keep.
- Soon one party sees the marriage was a mistake, and the other cries faithlessness. Then both reach the same point, and their mutual faithlessness cancels out into mutual contentment.
- Divorce is hard, so marriage has to be propped up with moral supports.
- A says calling the man who wants out a scoundrel is an indirect attack on marriage. Either marriage has reality, and he is punished by losing it, or it has none, and he is wiser than the rest.
- A warns against a relationship in which one becomes "several". Ordering travelling boots, for example, is difficult with a wife and impossible with a wife and children.
- A cites a gypsy woman who carried her husband on her back through life. He calls it rare and tiring in the long run, for the man.
- A thinks marriage ties one to "a fatal continuity with custom", which he finds as unpredictable as the weather.
- A says woman is and remains man's ruin the moment one contracts a lasting relationship with her. He calls a woman's love only pretence and weakness, and says a young man as fiery as an Arab horse is lost once he marries.
- The essay's one line about love rather than marriage is about breaking off. When two people fall in love and sense they are destined for each other, A advises having "the courage to break off".
- A's reason is that by continuing there is everything to lose and nothing to gain. That is a paradox for feeling but not for the understanding.
- A wants the erotic to have infinity, but poetic infinity, which fits in an hour as well as a month. A lasting relationship makes one "several" and costs one's freedom.
- The break is self-preservation for the aesthete, not a gift to the beloved.

### An official post

- Taking an official post makes one a mere Peter Somebody, a tiny peg in the machine of the state. One stops being the master of one's own cultivation.
- One also gets a title and never loses it again except by a crime that gets one flogged at the post. Even then a royal pardon may give it back.
- A does not recommend inactivity. He recommends cultivating every occupation identical with idleness, all the breadless arts, intensively rather than extensively.
- He says it takes little to please children.

## Clarifications on your questions

### What the notes and the essay say differently

- The notes say the quote about dying of boredom or shooting oneself out of curiosity "emphasises idleness as the true remedy". That is the wrong way round.
- Idleness is not a remedy for anything. It is the neutral condition in which one is either bored or amused. The remedy for boredom is amusement, brought about by rotation.
- The notes guess at embracing boredom through routine. A does the opposite. He never embraces boredom, which has to be annulled, and only amusement annuls it.
- Routine is what A runs from. He blames habit for the world's decline, and he condemns marriage partly because it lands one in "a fatal continuity with custom".
- The notes are right that the cure is not more work and not just "looking for change". It is change of a specific kind, a new method on the same soil. One limits oneself, then varies how one cultivates, and the prisoner with the spider is the model.
- The notes' last paragraph, about small arbitrary moments and accidental interest, matches A.
- The notes' line "We can't change it but change our perceptions of it" is close. The precise version is that boredom can be annulled by amusement produced from inside, through limitation, remembering and forgetting, and arbitrariness, not by changing one's surroundings.
- The notes' first two claims about marriage are A's. A says never to enter into marriage, and he advises lovers to have the courage to break off.
- The notes say, "if you truly loved them you would break it off". That is not A. His reason is not the other person's good but the aesthete's own freedom.
- The notes say, "Society making it more reasonable to deal with boredom, hence the aggravating evil". A says growing boredom makes the world go backwards, that bread and circuses at least helped Rome and that assemblies and thrift hasten the modern fall.
- The notes have "Panic and circenses". The phrase is *panem et circenses*, bread and circuses. Juvenal uses it for what the Roman people were reduced to wanting.
- The notes say "Slave to our emotions". That is not A's view. His advice is to have moods in one's power and use them.

### Whose views these are

- The sexist tone the notes noticed is real. It includes woman as man's ruin, a woman's love as pretence and weakness, the beauty who neither sews nor spins, and the gypsy woman as a burden to her husband.
- It is A's voice, meant to be heard as a life-view, not as Kierkegaard's own. On an exam, attribute every line of the essay to A.
- Judge William's first letter in volume 2, "The Aesthetic Validity of Marriage", replies directly. It is on the syllabus for November.

> **Watch out.** Every line of "Crop Rotation", the sexist claims included, is A's voice as a life-view, not Kierkegaard's own. Judge William's "The Aesthetic Validity of Marriage" in volume 2 is the direct reply.
