## The case for crop rotation

### Title and epigraph

- The full heading is "Crop Rotation. An attempt at a theory of social prudence." The Hong edition titles the same essay "Rotation of Crops: A Venture in a Theory of Social Prudence".
- This is A's seventh piece and the last before "The Seducer's Diary". It is the closest the book comes to giving A's programme for living.
- In the epigraph from Aristophanes' *Plutus*, Chremylos says people eventually tire of love, music, honour, courage, ambition and command. His slave Karion names a food in reply to each item. The exchange introduces **satiety**, the point at which people have had enough.

### Boredom as a cause of evil

- A starts from a principle because experienced people recommend it. His principle is that all people are boring.
- He compares its repelling force to "the negative", which Hegel calls the principle of motion. A says that anyone with this force behind him has infinite momentum.
- The Danish calls boredom *a* root of all evil, *en Rod*. The English copy and the notes call it *the* root. The two phrasings differ, and either is acceptable on an exam.
- A uses children as evidence. Amused children behave well, and boredom makes them unruly. A nursemaid can be hired for her ability to amuse children and dismissed for lacking it, regardless of her other virtues.
- A calls the nursemaid's employment the one relationship that gives aesthetic judgment its due. Nobody gets to divorce a boring wife, depose a boring king, exile a boring priest, sack a boring minister or execute a boring journalist. He blames the world's decline on habit and boredom taking over.

### A's history of boredom

- The bored gods create people. The bored Adam gets Eve, and boredom grows through their family with Cain and Abel and then through whole peoples.
- People build the tower into the clouds for amusement, but the project bores them. Scattering abroad leaves them bored, as A says travel does in his own day.
- A says bread and circuses delayed Rome's fall. He says modern assemblies of the estates and efforts to pay down the national debt through economies speed up the decline.
- A imagines Denmark borrowing millions to make all public amusements and services free, from the theatre to the funeral. Copenhagen would become another Athens, the world's wealthy would move there and the emperor of Persia would be kidnapped and sold to the Turk if Persia replaced him.
- In A's proposal, the person who tells the best joke becomes king. A then says his wisdom is unsuitable for everyone. He wants no disciples and would share it with at most one person on his deathbed.
- A calls man a beast of prey and dismisses the claim that man is a social animal as inherited hypocrisy and calculated cunning.

## Idleness and boredom

### Who gets bored

- A uses "Boring" in two senses. The crowd or **plebs** bore others. The elect or nobility bore themselves.
- In A's account, people who never bore themselves bore others, especially when they keep busy. The people who bore themselves can still amuse others.
- The nobility entertain the initiated even at their most bored. They can die passively from boredom or actively shoot themselves out of curiosity. A applies the line to the elect at the extreme.

### Why work fails as a remedy

- **Idleness** means doing nothing. **Boredom** is a feeling of emptiness that can affect even a busy person.
- The proverb blames idleness and prescribes work. A calls this plebeian and considers idleness divine as long as it is free of boredom.
- The Olympian gods live in happy idleness. A also describes a woman who neither sews, spins, irons, reads nor plays and remains happy because she is free of boredom.
- A says the ability to be idle is part of being human. Restless activity makes a person resemble an animal that must keep moving.
- A accepts that "Idleness is the devil's pillow", but says the devil gets no time to use it if one remains amused.
- People who treat work as man's purpose oppose work to idleness. A treats amusement as man's purpose, so he opposes amusement to boredom.
- Work ends idleness. Only amusement can end boredom. A claims the busiest workers bore others most, and that when they feel no boredom it is because they have no idea what it is.

### Why A calls boredom demonic pantheism

- A writes, "Boredom is the demonic pantheism." **Pantheism** means fullness here. A calls boredom pantheistic because its emptiness is everywhere.
- A calls boredom evil when one remains in it and true when amusement has annulled it.
- Boredom "rests on the nothing that winds through existence". A compares its dizziness to looking into an infinite abyss.
- A makes the travelling Englishman his example of boredom. He compares him to an immovable marmot whose whole language is one interjection. People who shout at everything out of empty enthusiasm are just as empty.

## Two ways to change

### Extensive rotation changes the surroundings

- The bored person asks for change, and A distinguishes two ways to get it.
- **Extensive rotation** means repeatedly changing one's surroundings. A's examples progress from the country to the city, from one's country to abroad, from being "tired of Europe" to going to America and then from star to star.
- The same method trades porcelain for silver and then gold, or leads Nero to burn half of Rome to see Troy burn. A says it cancels itself.
- A calls this **the bad infinity**, Hegel's term for an endless series that never arrives. He prefers Marcus Aurelius's advice that one can live again by seeing things as one used to see them.

### Intensive rotation changes what one does with the same surroundings

- **Intensive rotation** keeps the soil and varies the crop and cultivation. In life, it means varying how one uses the same surroundings.
- A calls **limitation** the only saving principle in the world. A narrower range of possibilities forces greater invention.
- A prisoner serving a life sentence can find a spider entertaining. A's school examples include a fly under a nutshell, another watched through paper after being shut in a hole in a desk, and the steady drip from a roof.

## How to use intensive rotation

### Remember and forget deliberately

- A says every change of method involves remembering and forgetting. The skill is to control both.
- A advises giving up hope first, because hoping makes it harder to limit oneself and therefore to live artistically.
- He compares hope to an unreliable pilot. Prometheus gave people hope in place of the immortals' foreknowledge, and A treats it as a dubious gift. A also advises getting rid of hope in "The Unhappiest One".
- *Nil admirari* is Horace's "wonder at nothing". A says a moment should matter enough to recall at will and remain easy enough to forget at will.
- Childhood remembers best and forgets most easily. **Poetic remembering** is a kind of forgetting because one remembers the experience after removing what was painful.
- A advises stopping an enjoyment while it lasts and remembering it. Exhausting the pleasure leads to a surfeit and leaves one unable to remember or forget it.
- A calls remembering more dangerous than any weapon and remarks that the police only ban concealed weapons.
- **Deliberate forgetting** differs from forgetfulness. A applies it even to past pleasures, since they can cause longing. He says one can flatter a misfortune as one might offer honey cakes to Cerberus.
- A describes forgetting as "the scissors with which one cuts away what one cannot use, under the highest supervision of memory". Memory and forgetting become one through practice.
- A calls this trained coordination the Archimedean point that lifts the world. He says it lets one play shuttlecock with existence, and measures a person's flexibility by how well they forget.

### Use moods and chance

- A's advice is to vary oneself continually. He says people cannot create moods at will, but can notice one coming and use it. He compares that skill to a sailor anticipating a squall.
- A sees teasing as a way to explore a situation.
- "The whole secret lies in arbitrariness." **Arbitrariness** means choosing what to attend to even when that choice has little to do with the work or experience itself.
- A suggests watching only the middle of a play or reading the third part of a book. This can yield pleasure the author never intended.
- A grew bored listening to a man's philosophical chatter. When he noticed sweat gather on the man's forehead, run down his nose and hang at the tip, watching it amused him so much he could encourage the man to continue.
- Baggesen objected to an otherwise decent man because nothing rhymed with his name.
- A advises giving full attention to some chance detail, treating life as a wager and changing how one sees things. He says the consistency of this practice distinguishes an artist from a bungler.
- Chance details in the world give one's arbitrariness something to work with. A recommends staying alert to them, since amusements planned a fortnight ahead can disappoint and a small accident can amuse.
- A says even the fullest theory falls short of what genius can discover independently.

## Three commitments A would avoid

- A wants to keep enough freedom to leave any relationship. He calls this "perfect floating".

### Friendship

- A calls a friend a superfluous third party. He contrasts this with the necessary other in philosophy.
- He names friendship rituals such as using first names, opening a vein and mixing blood. He calls the ancient ideal of wanting and rejecting the same things boring.
- A says one person can only be in another's way. He can spend time with others as long as he can leave the relationship freely.
- He compares this to letting a field lie fallow. Social prudence leaves relationships alone for a time, after which they can return in a new form.

### Marriage and romantic love

- A considers promises of eternal love easy to make and empty. Promising love "till Easter" or "till next May Day" at least gives a deadline one might keep.
- A imagines one spouse realising the mistake while the other protests the betrayal. When both become unfaithful, each can be content with the other's unfaithfulness.
- Divorce is difficult, so marriage relies on moral supports. A says calling the person who wants out a scoundrel indirectly attacks marriage. If marriage has value, leaving already punishes him. If it has no value, he has judged it better than the others.
- A warns against a relationship in which one becomes "several". Buying travelling boots, for example, becomes difficult with a wife and impossible with a wife and children.
- A cites a gypsy woman carrying her husband through life and calls it unusual and ultimately tiring for the man.
- A thinks marriage ties one to "a fatal continuity with custom", which he finds as unpredictable as weather.
- A claims a lasting relationship with a woman ruins a man, calls a woman's love pretence and weakness, and says marriage ruins even a young man as fiery as an Arab horse.
- Even when two lovers think they are destined for each other, A advises having "the courage to break off". He says continuing can only cost them, a paradox for feeling that makes sense to the understanding.
- A wants poetic infinity in erotic love, which can fit into an hour or a month. He believes ending the relationship preserves the aesthete's freedom.

### An official post

- Taking an official post makes one a Peter Somebody, a small part of the state, and takes away control over one's own cultivation.
- A says an officeholder keeps the title except for a crime punishable by flogging. A royal pardon can restore it even then.
- He recommends cultivating occupations compatible with idleness, especially the unpaid arts, intensively. He recalls how little it takes to please children.

## Clarifications on your questions

### What the notes and the essay say differently

- The notes say the elect's remark about death and curiosity "emphasises idleness as the true remedy". A treats idleness as neutral and treats amusement through rotation as the remedy for boredom.
- The notes suggest embracing boredom through routine, rule out more work or just "looking for change", and say, "We can't change it but change our perceptions of it". A calls for ending boredom through amusement. He blames habit and recommends limitation, deliberate remembering and forgetting, and attention to chance details within the same surroundings.
- The notes say, "if you truly loved them you would break it off". A advises ending it to preserve the aesthete's own freedom. The two reasons differ.
- The notes say, "Society making it more reasonable to deal with boredom, hence the aggravating evil". A says growing boredom makes the world decline, that bread and circuses helped Rome and that assemblies and thrift speed up the modern decline.
- The notes have "Panic and circenses". The phrase is *panem et circenses*, bread and circuses. Juvenal uses it for what the Roman people were reduced to wanting.
- The notes say "Slave to our emotions". A's advice is to recognise moods and use them.

### Whose views these are

- The remarks about the woman at leisure, women ruining men, and the woman who carries her husband are A's.
- The essay presents A's way of life under a pseudonym. On an exam, attribute its claims to A.
- Judge William's first letter in volume 2, "The Aesthetic Validity of Marriage", replies directly. It is assigned in November.
