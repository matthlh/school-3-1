# PHIL 385 — Summary: *Either/Or*, "The Aesthetic Validity of Marriage"

A short summary of the essay from the book, for reading before you read it. The detailed notes are on the [reading page](aesthetic-validity.md). Exam 4 (Fri Nov 20) covers it.

## What it is
- It is the first of B's two letters to A, and it opens Part II of *Either/Or*.
- B is Judge William, a married civil servant. A is the young aesthete whose papers make up Part I.
- A thinks marriage kills the poetry of love. B argues on A's own terms that marriage is more beautiful and more worth living than romance, not less.
- B sets himself two tasks.
  - Show that marriage has aesthetic worth.
  - Show that this worth survives time and hard times.

## B's picture of A
- B writes a letter rather than a treatise so he can be personal and urgent.
- He cares about A like a son or a brother, and he worries about him.
- A is brilliant but drifting. He enjoys only beginnings and the first rush of love.
- A treats people as experiments. He gives a poor woman five dollars just to see whether she thanks or curses providence.
- B says what A lacks is faith.
- B warns A about A himself, and says he would warn a daughter against him.

## Romantic love and how the modern age lost it
- Romantic love is love at first sight. It rests on nature and beauty, and it feels eternal.
- That sense of the eternal is what separates love from lust.
- Its weakness is that old romances end at the wedding, so they never show love lasting.
- The modern, reflective age gives up on romantic love in two ways.
  - The immoral way keeps the passion and drops marriage. Byron's view is that love is heaven and marriage is hell. Some want a marriage they can leave.
  - The respectable way is the marriage of convenience, a calculation with no love in it.
- Both ways lose the eternal. B calls the age's illness melancholy, a fear of committing.

## B's answer: marriage takes first love higher
- Marriage belongs to Christianity. Neither the pagans nor Judaism perfected it.
- Marriage does not doubt first love. It lifts first love into the religious.
- The couple thank God for each other, and their intention to stay together, the vow, brings love into the ethical.
- Marriage assumes love is already there. It cannot create love.
- The real traitors of marriage are husbands who secretly long for their engagement days.
- First love is wide awake and aimed at one real person. You love only once.

## Marriage has no "why"
- Marrying for a purpose turns marriage into a calculation, which B calls both unaesthetic and irreligious.
- The three respectable purposes he rejects are building character, having children and getting a home.
- Children are a blessing, not the reason to marry.

## The wedding ceremony
- It ties the couple to all of humanity, back to Adam and Eve.
- It names sin as everyone's lot, not as something the couple is about to do.
- It asks for a vow. The Church takes the couple's love for granted.
- End of the first task: marriage is first love transformed, and even more beautiful than it.

## The second task: time
- First love has no inner history. Marriage has one, because the couple's will is in it.
- B's key claim is that the aesthetic lies in what is acquired over time, not in the immediate moment. So marriage is the truly poetic thing, not romance.
- Spouses must be fully honest with each other, while keeping their life private from the world. Secrets wreck marriages, as Goethe's *Elective Affinities* shows.
- Outside troubles are turned inward and worked through together.
- "Habit" is a word only for bad things.
- A says he is a conqueror who cannot possess. B answers that possessing takes more strength: humility and patience instead of pride.
- A knight's heroism fits into one moment. A husband's faithfulness is lived every day, so he keeps time rather than killing it.

## Duty
- A fears that duty kills love. B says duty is love's friend, because the one duty is truly to love.
- If duty were love's enemy and love could not beat it, love would not be the winner A claims it is.
- The aesthetic, the ethical and the religious are allies, not rivals.
- B wrote the letter over two weeks of evenings, and A is always welcome at his home.

## Points worth knowing for the exam
- B is Judge William, and this is the first piece of Part II.
- The two tasks: marriage is aesthetic, and it stays aesthetic over time.
- The poorhouse woman and the five dollars.
- Romantic love against the two modern paths, the immoral one and the marriage of convenience.
- The three rejected reasons for marrying: character, children and a home.
- Conqueror against possessor.
- Goethe's *Elective Affinities* as the warning about secrets.
- Duty as love's friend.
- Kraal's reading: even judged on the aesthetic's own terms, the ethical wins.
