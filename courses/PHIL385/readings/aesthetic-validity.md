## B's argument for marriage

- "The Aesthetic Validity of Marriage" opens Part II of *Either/Or*. It is the first of B's two letters to A.
- B is Judge William, a married civil servant. A is the young aesthete whose papers make up Part I.

> **Watch out.** This essay opens Part II and is the first of B's two letters to A. B is Judge William, the married civil servant; A is the young aesthete of Part I. Do not reverse who argues for marriage.

- A thinks marriage kills the poetry of love. B argues that marriage is more beautiful and more worth living than romance even on A's terms.
- B has two tasks. First, he argues that marriage has aesthetic worth, or that it can be beautiful. Second, he explains how marriage can keep that beauty through time and hardship.
- Kraal's reading is that the ethical way of life wins even when you judge it by the aesthetic way of life's own standards.
- Exam 4 covers this essay on Fri Nov 20. The next reading is "Equilibrium" (Nov 13–18). The [question bank](../02-questions.md) has a section called "Reading: The Aesthetic Validity of Marriage".

### The letter (about p. 384, PDF p. 334)

- B is a public official "accustomed to writing in folio". He chooses a letter rather than a treatise because the letter form allows "admonitory and urgent tones" and keeps A from arguing in general terms.
- B wrote the opening lines last. Together with the closing lines, they form an envelope that shows the piece is a letter.
- B says, "To defend is to indict". A married man would say "marriage, that's life's aesthetic". A young man would say "love, you are life's aesthetic". Neither would see why marriage's aesthetic reputation needs saving.
- Each of B's tasks opens with a short "polemical prologue" that answers A's sarcasm.
- B takes it as his vocation as a husband to defend marriage "pro aris et focis", meaning for hearth and home. He would write a book if it could save a single marriage.
- B's wife is "the first" and only person he has loved. He calls their marriage "the continual rejuvenation of our first love". It is an activity, not a backward glance.

### B's portrait of A (about pp. 384–392, PDF pp. 334–341)

- B loves A "like a son, a brother, a friend", with an aesthetic love. He also loves him "with the fear and trembling of a religious love" because he thinks A has gone astray.
- B says A is "not really an enemy of marriage". A mocks it with an ironic glance.
- A is only an observer of marriage. He sneaks into husbands' confidence out of "hypochondriacal curiosity".
- A is brilliant but drifting, and enjoys only beginnings. B says his life "will be nothing but approach-runs". He is drawn to "the first rapture of love" and "loves the accidental". One example is a girl who blushed when their eyes met in the mirror.
- A is "child one moment, old man the next" and wants to play "a little Zeus".
- B says, "You live from theft". A steals other people's happiest moments as phantom-images. The people lose nothing, while A loses his time, peace of mind and patience to live.
- A treats people as experiments.
- One of the two poorhouse women wished for five dollars. A had already taken out the bill when he gave it to her. He wanted to play fate and see whether she would thank or curse providence.
- B says what A lacks "entirely, is faith" and that his behaviour "borders on insanity".
- B says, "I am warning you, therefore, not about the world but about yourself and I am warning the world about you." He would warn a daughter against A.
- A is "the epitome of all possibility". He keeps every mood as "a constant possibility" and so is "not available for any charge".
- B later calls A "a stranger and an alien in the world". A is alone in his kayak, like the Flying Dutchman. Every family welcomes him as a guest, which B calls a humiliation.
- B tells A, "You have come to a halt at the destroying of illusions". He calls that position "a new illusion" and concludes "you accomplish nothing".

## First love and the reflective age

### Romantic love (about pp. 393–397, PDF pp. 342–346)

- B sets out the criteria of romantic love.
- Romantic love is immediate. It is love at first sight: "to see her and to love her were one and the same".
- Romantic love rests on natural necessity and beauty. Its sense of the eternal gives it nobility. B says, "What distinguishes love from lust is its having the stamp of the eternal."
- Sensuality is "the instantaneous". It can make a moment "a little eternity", while true eternity requires "a determination of the will".
- The old romances end at the wedding, where B thinks they should begin. The lovers' struggle is entirely external. They face four acts of trials and unite in the fifth. B says "the reader is none the wiser" about how the love lasts.
- In more recent stories, money takes the place of the cruel father as an external obstacle. The usual solution is "a rich uncle dying in the fifth".

### The two responses to romantic love

- The modern, reflective age strikes out on two paths against romantic love.
- The **immoral path** keeps passion and gives up marriage. It recovers the temporal "refined again in a sensual eternity, in the eternal instant of the embrace". Byron treats love as heaven and marriage as hell.
- The immoral path has a new definition of unhappy love, which is being loved when one is no longer in love.
- The decent version of the immoral path wants a marriage for a period that you can leave by giving notice, like a change of address. B calls this "a formal protest against marriage".
- The **respectable path** is the marriage of convenience, really "the marriage of reason". It is a capitulation to life's complications.
- Its participants have learnt that love is an illusion and concern themselves with "the prose of life".
- B says the marriage of convenience offers only despair as solace. Calculation is temporal, so this marriage loses the eternal and is both immoral and unaesthetic.
- Both paths lose the eternal.
- The two paths set love and marriage against each other. One keeps love and rejects marriage. The other keeps marriage and gives up love.

> **Watch out.** Keep the two responses apart. The **immoral path** keeps passion and gives up marriage (Byron: love as heaven, marriage as hell). The **respectable path** is the marriage of convenience, the marriage of reason, which keeps marriage and gives up love. Both lose the eternal.

### Melancholy and the fear of commitment

- B calls the age's illness melancholy, a fear of committing. He calls melancholy "the defect of our time".
- **Egoistic melancholy** is fear for one's own sake, as in "perhaps fate will tie me to another".
- **Sympathetic melancholy** is fear for oneself for the other person's sake. B calls it "more painful and also somewhat nobler".

> **Definition.** **Egoistic melancholy** is fear of commitment for one's own sake. **Sympathetic melancholy** is fear for oneself for the other person's sake, which B calls more painful and somewhat nobler. Both treat each day as "examination day".

- Both forms of melancholy treat each day as "examination day".
- B says the age neutralizes marriage out of cowardice and self-indulgence. The Middle Ages also neutralized it, but because people thought the unmarried life was more perfect.

## How marriage preserves first love

### The religious and ethical elements (about pp. 398–400, PDF pp. 347–349)

- B says marriage belongs to Christianity. Neither the pagan nations, "for all the sensuality of the Orient and for all the beauty of Greece", nor Judaism perfected marriage, because neither reflected deeply enough on the contrast between the sexes.
- Romantic love's defect was its lack of reflection, so one might think conjugal love should begin with doubt. B says no, because that would divorce marriage from love.
- Instead, marriage takes first love up "into a higher concentric immediacy", which enriches it. The higher thing is the religious, "where rational reflection ends".
- The substance of marriage is "the being in love". Without love, sharing a life is "a satisfaction of sensual desire or an association, a partnership".
- Marriage does not evoke love. It presupposes love "as something present, not past". B likens people who marry and wait for love to follow to "dove-fanciers" shutting two doves in a cage.
- Marriage has an ethical and religious element and is based on resignation. Being in love alone lacks these features.
- B calls husbands who secretly long for their engagement the real traitors of marriage. They rebel only in thought, sitting "like lunatics each in his matrimonial cell" and sighing for the engagement.
- The seducers are not the traitors, because they never entered marriage. The divorced are not the traitors either, because they rebelled openly.
- If the husbands were right, love would belong to the engagement and no one should marry.

### What B means by first love (about pp. 401–415, PDF pp. 350–362)

- "First love" is "a battle-cry" for B and the signal to open fire for A.
- For one kind of person, the first experience remains "the propulsive, infinite impulse" of their life, the first as the present constantly renewed.
- For the other kind of person, the first becomes a repelling power that they drift away from.
- The less repeatable an experience is, the more its first occurrence matters. An experience that has the eternal in it cannot be repeated.
- The philistine who looks for a partner in the newspaper rules out first love. The state before first love is "love makes blind", which B sees in Mozart's Page in *Figaro*, in "this dreaming yet searching state".
- First love itself is "an absolute wakefulness". It is directed at "a single, definite, actual object" who is all that matters to the lover.
- First love has the sensual in it but is not simply sensual, because the sensual as such comes forth only in reflection.
- First love presupposes itself in both directions into eternity.
- First love is "the unity of freedom and necessity". The lover is drawn by an irresistible power and is free in it. It is also a unity of "the universal and the singular", and it has all this immediately.
- B offers to be "the noble father" and let the couple marry without hard-hearted fathers or sphinxes. He says, "in the name of God let them come together".
- The couple relate their love to God by thanking him. Their love is "assumed into a higher concentricity" without changing its character or adding reflection. B compares this to a swimming belt worn by someone who has never been in danger.
- Their intention to keep their love, expressed in the vow, brings it into the ethical.

### A's objections to the altar

- A objects that the Christian God is spirit and negates beauty and sensuality. B says flesh is not sensuality but selfishness, so even the spiritual can be carnal.
- B adds that Christianity can take up innocent sensuality, and that Scripture has "no special blessing for bachelors".
- A says the bride stands "as though to be chastised", the groom is made her master, and she is declared a sinner. B says A is already her master and the Church proclaims sin as woman's lot in general. He calls woman "meek" and "much nearer to God than man".
- A objects that a third power commands the lovers' fidelity. B says the religiously developed seek that power themselves. He points out that lovers already swear by the moon.
- B speaks of the lovers' "metamorphosis into bride and bridegroom". He says a man's weakness is to think he conquered the woman he loves. Thanking God humbles him.
- B says, "it is in truth far more beautiful to take the beloved as a gift from the hand of God than to have subdued the whole world to conquer her". Marriage involves "daily possession throughout a whole lifetime".
- B says the matrimonial "we" and "my" sound deeper than the erotic. He says, "Let Don Juan keep his leafy bower, the knight the dark heaven and its stars; marriage has its heaven even higher."
- B says, "One loves only once". The aesthetic, the religious and the ethical agree, and marriage puts the claim into practice. B says the Church looks with suspicion on a second marriage.
- B says the unhappy are of two kinds, those who long for the ideal and those unwilling to hold on to it. He calls the second group the real seducers.
- Marriage keeps first love's aesthetic qualities, its infinitude and unity of opposites, and has more. Its inner infinitude is "an eternal life", and it adds a further opposition, the spiritual.
- Earthly love is **preferential love** because it comes to rest in one person. B calls this both its defect and its advantage. Spiritual love loves everyone.

### Why B rejects reasons for marrying (about pp. 416–427, PDF pp. 363–372)

- B says marriage "has its teleology in itself". Its purpose is part of marriage itself. He says, "The less 'why' the more love." He calls finite reasons "a swarm".
- Marrying for a purpose turns marriage into a calculation.
- B gives three respectable reasons for marrying and calls each one-sided and "as ridiculous as they are anaesthetic and irreligious".
  - **Building character.** A encourages an official at the tea-table to say one marries "because marriage is a good school for character". B wishes every such husband "a Xanthippe for his wife". Marrying for any purpose is unaesthetic and irreligious, since "the fault is precisely that he has a purpose".
  - **Having children.** Genesis says God made marriage because "it was not good that man should be alone", and "He blessed them". States have rewarded fertility and Christianity has rewarded celibacy. B dismisses "Stud-farm purposes" while treating children as a blessing.
  - **Getting a home.** B says the bored bachelor marries for his comfort and makes a young woman a "galley-slave". He allows that ordinary people can marry for a home, because for them the home is a duty and vocation. A, "a stranger and an alien", has no home.
- On children as a blessing, B would paint the poor woman in the square with her baby if he were a painter. He gives the Chinese saying "bring your children up well, and you will come to know what you owe your parents".
- B calls money, jealousy, the prospect of her dying and inheritances despicable reasons to marry.
- To be aesthetic and religious, marriage must have no why. That puts it on the same footing as first love.
- B says, "The aesthetic in marriage is this: that it conceals a multitude of 'why's' which life reveals in all their blessedness."

### The wedding ceremony (about pp. 428–431, PDF pp. 373–376)

- The ceremony surveys the genesis of the human race and ties the new marriage to it.
- A objects that it is prosaic to be reminded of one's numerical significance, "Mr N.N. and the virtuous Miss N.N., at ten o'clock".
- B replies that love is a unity of the universal and the singular. The ceremony leads back to the first couple, Adam and Eve, so each marriage is "an individual and a symbol at the same time".
- The ceremony says that sin entered the world with marriage, but only as the lot of man in general. It does not say "you are now about to commit a sin". B calls being born in sin the highest expression of a child's dignity.
- The punishments are pain in childbirth, obedience of the woman to the man, and bread in the sweat of the brow. B says first love is not cowardly, so it does not fear the bread in the sweat of the brow.
- B calls woman "at once more perfect and more imperfect than the man". God made her "a helpmeet", and the man is the one who leaves father and mother.
- The ceremony does not bring the lovers to a standstill. It "lets what was already in motion proceed openly". Its questions make the relationship firm.
- The Church "is not a match-maker". It does not ask whether the couple love each other, because it takes that for granted. It requires a vow.
- B concludes the first task by calling marriage "the transfiguration of first love, and is even more beautiful than it".

## How marriage gains beauty over time

### The law of motion and what the spouses acquire (about pp. 433–436, PDF pp. 377–380)

- First love is "an unreal An-sich" with no inner history. B compares it to a sleepwalker who falls when someone says his name.
- Marital love is "armed" because the spouses' intention directs their will inward. Their love becomes historical by taking in what happens to them.
- B says, "The aesthetic lies not in the immediate but in the acquired." The beauty of marriage develops through the spouses' life together.
- Marriage is "the immediacy which has mediacy in itself, the infinity which has the finite in itself, an eternity which has the temporal in itself". B calls it ideal in both the classical and romantic sense.
- The young men cry "de omnibus dubitandum". B answers that personal doubt is about something specific, and that love is a province of giving, not acquiring.
- B says, "It is marriage that is poetic." So marriage, not romance, is the truly poetic thing. First love lacks "the second aesthetic ideal, the element of history".
- Similarly, a faith that worked miracles would have no history. The history of faith is its appropriation in life, making it one's own.
- Romantic love never imagines a history. Prosaic love has a short, pedestrian history. Experimental love has no continuous history.

### Secretiveness outside, openness inside (about pp. 437–450, PDF pp. 381–392)

- A fights "the congregation" like a madman who thought his room was full of flies. He would give every wedding guest a douceur because he thinks money removes relationships.
- The words are "before God and the congregation". B accepts a quiet wedding and says banns from the pulpit do not endanger love.
- Outwardly, B calls secretiveness "the absolute condition for the safeguarding of the aesthetic in marriage". Marital love does not run off to an uninhabited island. It keeps its secret among people.
- Within the marriage, B calls for "openheartedness, uprightness, publicity on the largest conceivable scale". This takes courage.
- In A's imagined castle, he lives on the ground floor and his wife lives in the left wing of the first floor. He wants them to stay strangers enough to be interesting.
- B replies, "You fear that when the enigma has gone love will cease, while I believe that only when it has gone does love begin." He says A clings to immediacy as a category of nature.
- B says the secrecy system never makes a happy marriage. His examples are the wife treated as a slave, the "misplaced solicitude" that calls woman weak, and the gifted young husband who arranged his wife's whole world like Potemkin's landscapes.
- Against calling woman weak, B says, "Falsehood! The woman is just as strong as the man, maybe stronger".
- The gifted young husband was proud that "she owes everything to me". B tells married people to "live on a good understanding".
- B says, "Honesty, openheartedness, revelation, understanding, that is the life-principle in marriage."
- The one case where B advises against marriage is a secret "which cannot be dragged out of you without costing you your life".
- Goethe's *Elective Affinities* shows what secretiveness leads to. If Edward had confided in his wife, B says the affair would have been "a divertissement in the drama of the marriage".

### Adversity, habit and possession (about pp. 451–466, PDF pp. 393–406)

- B says external adversities, the stuff of tearful dramas, must be turned inward and worked through together. He has experienced few adversities himself and believes "nothing is capable of crushing the aesthetic in a person".
- B recalls Luther's claim that one has never heard of a Christian dying of hunger. He says a husband (Aegtemand) proves himself a genuine man (ægte Mand).
- B says, "If love can be preserved, the aesthetic, too, can be preserved; for love itself is the aesthetic."
- The word "habit" belongs only to the bad, to something unfree. Of the good one can never talk of habit.
- B says monotony can be beautiful, as an even tempo in music is.
- B says despair "can never be a task; it is a convenience".
- B says A's error is to think abstractly, not historically. If A cannot endure marriage, "you will have to look about for another auditorium".
- A says he is "a conqueror who cannot possess". B says "possession is conquering in reverse" and compares its difficulty to going downhill, which takes more strength than going uphill.
- Conquest takes pride, violence, greed, eating and drinking. Possession takes humility, patience, contentment, praying and fasting. The religious element "will dethrone the conqueror and let the possessor come into view".

### Outer and inner history

- In **external history**, what one strives for lies outside. **Internal history** begins with the possession. B says, "Only internal history is true history."

> **Definition.** In **external history** what one strives for lies outside; it suits art and poetry and the moment. **Internal history** begins with the possession, and B says, "Only internal history is true history." Marriage is internal history lived every day.

- Art and poetry favour external history and the moment, because a moment can show a hero's pride, courage, conquest or death. It cannot show humility and patience, like the cross-bearer who takes up his cross each day.
- B orders the arts from space to time. Sculpture comes first, then painting (Schelling), then music, which vanishes in time, and then poetry, the most complete art.
- The aesthetic beyond representation is realized "by being lived". The highest is to feel oneself "a character in the play written by God", at once "composer and composition".
- The knight's heroism fits into one moment. He is faithful for fifteen years, and then his moment comes.
- The husband is faithful for fifteen years while possessing, and acquires the fidelity he has. His faithfulness is lived every day.
- The husband's enemy is "that most dangerous enemy which is time". The knight "has killed time". The husband "has not killed time but saved and preserved it in eternity". B recalls the man in hell who asked "What time is it?" and heard the devil answer "An eternity."
- B gives the predicates of married love: faithful, constant, humble, patient, forbearing, indulgent, sincere, contented, observant, persistent, willing and joyful. It is "the everyday and also the divine", or "the divine through being the everyday".

### Repetition, hope and recollection

- For visible signs of the erotic, B mentions a Paraguay tribe woken by a bell for marital duties, A's "look" stretched into an eternity, and "decies repetita placebunt".
- Healthy love rejuvenates itself through the signs and has another idea of repetition.
- People tend to live mainly in hope or mainly in recollection. B says a healthy person lives in both. Recollection "puts a cross on the note of the instant".
- The ages of the world run gold, silver, copper and iron. In marriage, "the silver wedding comes first and then the golden wedding". People find a bachelor's jubilee laughable.
- On uniformity, B compares A to a neurasthenic who needs noise. He says, "for you a turbulent sea is a picture of life, for me the still, deep water". He points to the small stream that is always the same and yet rich in change.
- B lets A have the whole verse from Oehlenschläger and keeps the last word, "and then, too, innocence".

## Duty and the end of the letter

### Why B calls duty love's friend (about pp. 467–473, PDF pp. 407–412)

- A imagines marriage behind closed doors, where "out comes the cane and we are told it's a duty". B distinguishes the corporal's cane of duty from the conductor's baton of first love.
- Duty need not compel while first love continues unchanged. B says A's fear of compulsion shows that he doubts the eternity of first love and that, for him, "love is clearly not the highest thing".
- B says, "You regard duty as the enemy of love, I regard it as its friend." If duty were an enemy that love could not defeat, "then love is not the true victor". On B's argument, A's defence of love would end up disparaging it.
- B calls the aesthetic, the ethical and the religious "the three great allies". He says life loses meaning if they come apart, and A's "do it or do not do it, you will regret both" would be justified.
- B says, "Duty is not for me one climate and love another; duty makes my love into the true, temperate climate."
- A husband feared his wife thought he loved her only from duty. He wanted to love her with all his soul "even at the moment when duty allowed him to refrain". B says this is what duty already asks, since "duty here is just one thing, truly to love".
- The "more" one can do is to do what duty enjoins, translating it from the external to the internal.
- B likens love and duty to the "t" and the "e" in the syllable "te".
- Duty loves true love and "has a mortal hatred of false love, yes, it kills it".
- The language of duty is in the future tense, to suggest the historical.
- A's "no mishmash" objection is that everything else is either duty or love, while marriage is a "hermaphroditic ambiguity". He asks where the boundary is, where the tribunal is and where the power to compel is.
- B says even an official's calling is more than a list of appointments. What matters in marriage is inward, and the check is "you yourself". He calls duty "our love's first and only confidant".

### The close (about pp. 474–475, PDF pp. 413–414)

- B says, "Dixi et animam meam liberavi", meaning I have spoken and freed my soul. He means a healthy breath (respiratio) instead of relief from a burden.
- In the Serbian legend, a giant with an immense appetite eats with a poor cottager. The cottager first says a prayer, "and behold! there is enough for them both".
- B wrote for a fortnight of evenings with A "outside my door". He gives A no standing invitation "for ever". A can come when he wants and is "always welcome".
