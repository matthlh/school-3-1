# PHIL 385 — Reading: *Either/Or*, "The Aesthetic Validity of Marriage"

The short summary comes first, then the essay section by section. Your own notes on it are on the [lecture 18 page](../lectures/18-aesthetic-validity-of-marriage.md), with Kraal's argument. Exam 4 (Fri Nov 20) covers it.

Page numbers: "p." is the Penguin Classics edition (Alastair Hannay's translation, 1992), the one Kraal cites, and "PDF p." is your file. The essay is PDF pp. 334–414. The PDF pages are exact. The Penguin pages marked "about" are worked out from three passages whose Penguin page is known (Kraal's p. 384, Owen's p. 399 and p. 443), so they can be off by a page or two.

## Summary

### What it is
- It is the first of B's two letters to A, and it opens Part II of *Either/Or*.
- B is Judge William, a married civil servant. A is the young aesthete whose papers make up Part I.
- A thinks marriage kills the poetry of love. B argues on A's own terms that marriage is more beautiful and more worth living than romance, not less.
- B sets himself two tasks.
  - Show that marriage has aesthetic worth.
  - Show that this worth survives time and hard times.

### B's picture of A
- B writes a letter rather than a treatise so he can be personal and urgent.
- He cares about A like a son or a brother, and he worries about him.
- A is brilliant but drifting. He enjoys only beginnings and the first rush of love.
- A treats people as experiments. He gives a poor woman five dollars just to see whether she thanks or curses providence.
- B says what A lacks is faith.
- B warns A about A himself, and says he would warn a daughter against him.

### Romantic love and how the modern age lost it
- Romantic love is love at first sight. It rests on nature and beauty, and it feels eternal.
- That sense of the eternal is what separates love from lust.
- Its weakness is that old romances end at the wedding, so they never show love lasting.
- The modern, reflective age gives up on romantic love in two ways.
  - The immoral way keeps the passion and drops marriage. Byron's view is that love is heaven and marriage is hell. Some want a marriage they can leave.
  - The respectable way is the marriage of convenience, a calculation with no love in it.
- Both ways lose the eternal. B calls the age's illness melancholy, a fear of committing.

### B's answer: marriage takes first love higher
- Marriage belongs to Christianity. Neither the pagans nor Judaism perfected it.
- Marriage does not doubt first love. It lifts first love into the religious.
- The couple thank God for each other, and their intention to stay together, the vow, brings love into the ethical.
- Marriage assumes love is already there. It cannot create love.
- The real traitors of marriage are husbands who secretly long for their engagement days.
- First love is wide awake and aimed at one real person. You love only once.

### Marriage has no "why"
- Marrying for a purpose turns marriage into a calculation, which B calls both unaesthetic and irreligious.
- The three respectable purposes he rejects are building character, having children and getting a home.
- Children are a blessing, not the reason to marry.

### The wedding ceremony
- It ties the couple to all of humanity, back to Adam and Eve.
- It names sin as everyone's lot, not as something the couple is about to do.
- It asks for a vow. The Church takes the couple's love for granted.
- End of the first task: marriage is first love transformed, and even more beautiful than it.

### The second task: time
- First love has no inner history. Marriage has one, because the couple's will is in it.
- B's key claim is that the aesthetic lies in what is acquired over time, not in the immediate moment. So marriage is the truly poetic thing, not romance.
- Spouses must be fully honest with each other, while keeping their life private from the world. Secrets wreck marriages, as Goethe's *Elective Affinities* shows.
- Outside troubles are turned inward and worked through together.
- "Habit" is a word only for bad things.
- A says he is a conqueror who cannot possess. B answers that possessing takes more strength: humility and patience instead of pride.
- A knight's heroism fits into one moment. A husband's faithfulness is lived every day, so he keeps time rather than killing it.

### Duty
- A fears that duty kills love. B says duty is love's friend, because the one duty is truly to love.
- If duty were love's enemy and love could not beat it, love would not be the winner A claims it is.
- The aesthetic, the ethical and the religious are allies, not rivals.
- B wrote the letter over two weeks of evenings, and A is always welcome at his home.

## Points worth knowing for the exam
- B is Judge William, and this is the first piece of Part II.
- The two tasks: marriage is aesthetic, and it stays aesthetic over time.
- The poorhouse woman and the five dollars.
- Romantic love against the two modern paths, the immoral one and the marriage of convenience.
- The three rejected reasons for marrying: character, children and a home.
- Conqueror against possessor.
- Goethe's *Elective Affinities* as the warning about secrets.
- Duty as love's friend.
- Kraal's reading: even judged on the aesthetic's own terms, the ethical wins.
- The next reading is "Equilibrium" (Nov 13–18).

## The essay, start to finish

### The letter and B's tasks (about p. 384, PDF p. 334)
- B is a public official "accustomed to writing in folio". He chose the letter form because it allows "admonitory and urgent tones" and stops A from arguing in general terms.
- The opening lines were written last and, with the closing lines, form an envelope to show it is a letter.
- "To defend is to indict": a married man would say "marriage, that's life's aesthetic" and a young man "love, you are life's aesthetic", and neither would see why marriage's aesthetic reputation needs saving.
- Two tasks: to show the aesthetic significance of marriage, and to show how the aesthetic can be sustained against life's obstacles. Each gets a short "polemical prologue" against A's sarcasm.
- B's vocation as a husband is to fight for marriage "pro aris et focis", for hearth and home. He would write a book if it could save a single marriage.
- B's wife is the only one he has ever loved, "the first". Their marriage is "the continual rejuvenation of our first love", an activity, not a backward glance.

### B's portrait of A (about pp. 384–392, PDF pp. 334–341)
- B loves A "like a son, a brother, a friend", with an aesthetic love, and "with the fear and trembling of a religious love", because A has gone astray.
- A is "not really an enemy of marriage"; he mocks it with his ironic glance. His life "will be nothing but approach-runs". He is drawn to "the first rapture of love" and "loves the accidental": the girl who blushed when their eyes met in the mirror.
- A is "child one moment, old man the next", wants to play "a little Zeus", and is only an observer of marriage, sneaking into husbands' confidence out of "hypochondriacal curiosity".
- "You live from theft": A steals people's happiest moments as phantom-images. The others lose nothing; A loses his time, his peace of mind and his patience to live.
- The two poorhouse women: one wished for five dollars, and A gave them, having already taken out the bill, so as to play fate and watch whether she would thank or curse providence. B: what A lacks "entirely, is faith"; acting as A does "borders on insanity".
- "I am warning you, therefore, not about the world but about yourself and I am warning the world about you." B would warn a daughter against him.
- A is "the epitome of all possibility", keeps every mood as "a constant possibility", and so is "not available for any charge".
- Later: A is "a stranger and an alien in the world", alone in his kayak like the Flying Dutchman; every family treats him as a welcome guest, which is a humiliation; "You have come to a halt at the destroying of illusions" and that is "a new illusion"; "you accomplish nothing".

### Romantic love, and what the reflective age put in its place (about pp. 393–397, PDF pp. 342–346)
- The old romances end where they should begin: after four acts of trials the lovers unite in the fifth and "the reader is none the wiser". Their struggle is entirely external, and today money has replaced cruel fathers, with "a rich uncle dying in the fifth".
- Criteria of romantic love: it is immediate, "to see her and to love her were one and the same"; it rests on natural necessity and on beauty; it has nobility from the consciousness of the eternal it takes up. "What distinguishes love from lust is its having the stamp of the eternal." The sensual is "the instantaneous" and makes the moment "a little eternity"; the true eternity needs "a determination of the will".
- The reflective age struck out on two paths against romantic love.
  - The immoral path keeps the temporal "refined again in a sensual eternity, in the eternal instant of the embrace". Byron: love is heaven, marriage hell. Its new definition of unhappy love is to be loved when one is no longer in love. Its decent version wants marriage for a period with a way out, like giving notice of a change of address: "a formal protest against marriage".
  - The respectable path is the marriage of convenience, really "the marriage of reason": a capitulation to life's complications, entered into by people who have learnt that love is an illusion, concerned with "the prose of life"; despair is its only solace; a calculation is always temporal, so the eternal is missing; it is as immoral as it is unaesthetic.
- Melancholy is "the defect of our time". Egoistic melancholy fears for itself ("perhaps fate will tie me to another"). Sympathetic melancholy, "more painful and also somewhat nobler", fears for itself for the other's sake. Both treat every day as "examination day". The age neutralizes marriage out of cowardice and self-indulgence, not, as the Middle Ages did, because the unmarried life was thought more perfect.
- So the reflective age declared itself for love against marriage, or for marriage against love.

### Marriage belongs to Christianity (about pp. 398–400, PDF pp. 347–349)
- Neither the pagan nations, "for all the sensuality of the Orient and for all the beauty of Greece", nor Judaism perfected marriage, because the contrast between the sexes was never reflected on deeply enough.
- Should conjugal love begin with doubt, since romantic love's defect was its lack of reflection? No: that would divorce marriage from love. Instead first love is taken up "into a higher concentric immediacy", which enriches it. The higher thing is the religious, "where rational reflection ends".
- The substance of marriage is love, "the being in love". Without it a shared life is "a satisfaction of sensual desire or an association, a partnership".
- Marriage does not evoke love; it presupposes it, "as something present, not past". Those who marry first and expect love to follow are "dove-fanciers" shutting two doves in a cage. Marriage has an ethical and religious factor and is based on resignation; being in love is not.
- The traitors of marriage are not the seducers, who never entered it, nor the divorced, who rebelled openly, but the husbands who rebel only in thought, sitting "like lunatics each in his matrimonial cell" and sighing for the engagement. If they were right, love would belong to the engagement and no one should marry.

### First love (about pp. 401–415, PDF pp. 350–362)
- "First love" is for B "a battle-cry"; for A it is the signal to open fire. Two kinds of people: those for whom the first is "the propulsive, infinite impulse" of their lives, the first as the present constantly renewed, and those for whom it becomes a repelling power they drift away from. The less repeatable a thing, the more its first matters; what has the eternal in it cannot be repeated.
- The philistine who looks for a partner in the newspaper has excluded himself from first love. The state before first love is "love makes blind": Mozart's Page in *Figaro*, "this dreaming yet searching state".
- First love, by contrast, is "an absolute wakefulness", aimed at "a single, definite, actual object" that is all there is for it. It has the sensual in it but is not simply sensual, because the sensual as such comes forth only in reflection. It presupposes itself backward and forward into eternity. It is "the unity of freedom and necessity" (drawn by an irresistible power and free in it) and of "the universal and the singular", and it has all this immediately.
- B offers himself as "the noble father": no hard-hearted fathers or sphinxes; "in the name of God let them come together".
- The lovers relate their love to God by thanking him. The thanks does not change first love; no reflection joins; it is "assumed into a higher concentricity", and the intention to hold on to it lifts it into the ethical. Image: a swimming belt worn by someone who has never been in danger.
- A's objection: the Christian God is spirit and negates beauty and the sensual; the altar scene, where the bride stands "as though to be chastised", the groom is made her master, she is declared a sinner, and a third power commands their fidelity. B's replies: flesh is not sensuality but selfishness, so even the spiritual can be carnal; the innocent sensual can be taken up into Christianity; Scripture has "no special blessing for bachelors"; A already is her master; the Church proclaims sin as woman's lot in general, and woman is "meek", "much nearer to God than man"; the religiously developed seek the third power themselves, and lovers swear by the moon anyway.
- The lovers' "metamorphosis into bride and bridegroom": a man's weakness is to think he has conquered the girl; thanking God humbles him, and "it is in truth far more beautiful to take the beloved as a gift from the hand of God than to have subdued the whole world to conquer her". Possession is "daily possession throughout a whole lifetime".
- The matrimonial "we" and "my" sound deeper than the erotic. "Let Don Juan keep his leafy bower, the knight the dark heaven and its stars; marriage has its heaven even higher."
- "One loves only once": the aesthetic, the religious and the ethical say the same thing, and marriage steps in to make it real. The Church looks with suspicion on a second marriage. The unhappy are of two kinds, those yearning for the ideal and those unwilling to hold on to it, the latter being the real seducers.
- Marriage has first love's aesthetic (infinitude, unity of opposites) and more: its inner infinitude is "an eternal life", and it has one opposition more, the spiritual. Earthly love is preferential love, which is its defect and its advantage: it ends in loving one; spiritual love loves all.

### Marriage has no "why" (about pp. 416–427, PDF pp. 363–372)
- Marriage "has its teleology in itself". "The less 'why' the more love." The finite why is "a swarm".
- Three reputable whys, each one-sided and so "as ridiculous as they are anaesthetic and irreligious":
  - A school for character. The official at the tea-table, egged on by A, said one marries "because marriage is a good school for character". B wishes every such husband "a Xanthippe for his wife". Marrying for any purpose is unaesthetic and irreligious, since "the fault is precisely that he has a purpose".
  - Children. Genesis says God made marriage because "it was not good that man should be alone", and "He blessed them"; states have rewarded fertility and Christianity has rewarded celibacy. "Stud-farm purposes" are irrelevant, yet children are a blessing: the poor woman in the square with her baby, whom B would paint if he were a painter; the Chinese saying "bring your children up well, and you will come to know what you owe your parents".
  - A home. The bored bachelor marries for comfort and makes a young girl a "galley-slave". Among common folk marrying for a home can be fine, because the home is a duty and vocation. A, "a stranger and an alien", has no home.
- Despicable whys: money, jealousy, prospects of her dying or of inheritances.
- Result: to be aesthetic and religious, marriage must have no why, which puts it level with first love. "The aesthetic in marriage is this: that it conceals a multitude of 'why's' which life reveals in all their blessedness."

### The wedding ceremony (about pp. 428–431, PDF pp. 373–376)
- It surveys the genesis of the human race and ties the new marriage to it. A objects that it is prosaic to be reminded of one's numerical significance, "Mr N.N. and the virtuous Miss N.N., at ten o'clock". B: love is a unity of the universal and the singular, and the ceremony leads back to the first couple, so each marriage is "an individual and a symbol at the same time".
- It proclaims that sin entered the world with marriage, but only as the lot of man in general, never "you are now about to commit a sin". Being born in sin is the highest expression of a child's dignity.
- The punishments: pain in childbirth, the woman obeying the man (woman is "at once more perfect and more imperfect than the man"; God made her "a helpmeet"; it is the man who leaves father and mother), and bread in the sweat of the brow, which first love, being not cowardly, does not fear.
- The ceremony does not bring the lovers to a standstill; it "lets what was already in motion proceed openly". Its questions make firm. The Church "is not a match-maker" and does not ask whether they love each other because it takes that for granted. It exacts a vow.
- Conclusion of the first task: marriage is "the transfiguration of first love, and is even more beautiful than it".

### The law of motion, and the aesthetic in the acquired (about pp. 433–436, PDF pp. 377–380)
- First love is "an unreal An-sich" with no inner history, like a sleepwalker who falls the moment you say his name. Marital love is "armed": in the intention the will is directed inward.
- "The aesthetic lies not in the immediate but in the acquired." Marriage is "the immediacy which has mediacy in itself, the infinity which has the finite in itself, an eternity which has the temporal in itself", so it is ideal in both the classical and the romantic sense.
- Against the young men who cry "de omnibus dubitandum": personal doubt is about something specific; love is a province of giving, not acquiring.
- "It is marriage that is poetic." First love lacks "the second aesthetic ideal, the element of history". Compare faith: a faith that worked miracles would have no history; the history of faith is its appropriation in life.
- Marital love is historical because it assimilates what it experiences. Romantic love never imagines a history; prosaic love's history is short and pedestrian; experimental love's has no continuity.

### Congregation, secretiveness and openness (about pp. 437–450, PDF pp. 381–392)
- A fights "the congregation" like the madman who thought his room was full of flies. Money removes relationships, so A would pay a douceur to every wedding guest. The words are "before God and the congregation"; a quiet wedding is fine. Banns from the pulpit do not endanger love.
- Outwardly, secretiveness is "the absolute condition for the safeguarding of the aesthetic in marriage": marital love does not run off to an uninhabited island but keeps its secret among people. Inwardly the condition is "openheartedness, uprightness, publicity on the largest conceivable scale", which takes courage.
- A's castle in the air: he on the ground floor, his wife in the left wing of the first floor, strangers enough to be interesting. B: "You fear that when the enigma has gone love will cease, while I believe that only when it has gone does love begin." A clings to immediacy as a category of nature.
- The secrecy system never makes a happy marriage. The wife as slave; the "misplaced solicitude" that calls woman weak ("Falsehood! The woman is just as strong as the man, maybe stronger"); and the gifted young husband who arranged his wife's whole world like Potemkin's landscapes, proud that "she owes everything to me". Married people should "live on a good understanding".
- "Honesty, openheartedness, revelation, understanding, that is the life-principle in marriage." The one case where B advises against marriage: a secret "which cannot be dragged out of you without costing you your life". Goethe's *Elective Affinities* shows what secretiveness leads to; had Edward confided in his wife, the affair would have been "a divertissement in the drama of the marriage".

### The difficulties: external and internal (about pp. 451–466, PDF pp. 393–406)
- External adversities (the stuff of tearful dramas) must be turned inward. B has not had many; he is convinced "nothing is capable of crushing the aesthetic in a person". Luther: one has never heard of a Christian dying of hunger. A husband (Aegtemand) proves himself a genuine man (ægte Mand). "If love can be preserved, the aesthetic, too, can be preserved; for love itself is the aesthetic."
- "Habit": the word belongs only to the bad, to something unfree; of the good one can never talk of habit. Monotony can be beautiful, as an even tempo in music. Despair "can never be a task; it is a convenience".
- A's error is to think abstractly, not historically. If marriage cannot be endured, "you will have to look about for another auditorium".
- A says he is "a conqueror who cannot possess". Going downhill takes more strength than going uphill; "possession is conquering in reverse"; to conquer needs pride, violence, greed, eating and drinking; to possess needs humility, patience, contentment, praying and fasting. The religious element "will dethrone the conqueror and let the possessor come into view".
- Two kinds of history: external, where what one strives for lies outside, and internal, which begins with the possession. "Only internal history is true history." Art and poetry prefer external history and the moment: pride, courage, a hero conquering or dying can be shown in a moment; humility, patience, the cross-bearer who takes up his cross every day cannot. The arts move from space to time: sculpture, painting (Schelling), music, which vanishes in time, poetry, the most complete. The aesthetic beyond representation is realized "by being lived"; the highest is to feel oneself "a character in the play written by God", at once "composer and composition".
- The knight is faithful for fifteen years and then comes his moment; the husband is faithful for fifteen years while possessing, and acquires the fidelity he has. His enemy is "that most dangerous enemy which is time". The knight "has killed time"; the husband "has not killed time but saved and preserved it in eternity". The man in hell asked "What time is it?" and the devil said "An eternity."
- Predicates of married love: faithful, constant, humble, patient, forbearing, indulgent, sincere, contented, observant, persistent, willing, joyful. Married love is "the everyday and also the divine", "the divine through being the everyday".
- The visible signs of the erotic: the Paraguay tribe woken by a bell for marital duties; A's "look" stretched into an eternity; "decies repetita placebunt". Healthy love rejuvenates itself through the signs and has another idea of repetition.
- People live mainly in hope or mainly in recollection; the healthy individual lives in both. Recollection "puts a cross on the note of the instant". In the world the ages run gold, silver, copper, iron; in marriage "the silver wedding comes first and then the golden wedding". A bachelor's jubilee is thought laughable.
- Uniformity: A is like the neurasthenic who needs noise; "for you a turbulent sea is a picture of life, for me the still, deep water", the small stream always the same and yet rich in change. Oehlenschläger's verse: A gets the whole verse, B the last word, "and then, too, innocence".

### Duty (about pp. 467–473, PDF pp. 407–412)
- A: behind the closed door "out comes the cane and we are told it's a duty". B: the corporal's cane of duty against the conductor's baton of first love. As long as first love is unchanged, duty need not compel; A's fear of being compelled shows he does not believe in the eternity of first love, so for him "love is clearly not the highest thing".
- "You regard duty as the enemy of love, I regard it as its friend." The argument: if duty is love's enemy and love cannot defeat it, "then love is not the true victor" and A has disparaged love. Unless one sees the aesthetic, the ethical and the religious as "the three great allies", life is without meaning and A's "do it or do not do it, you will regret both" is justified.
- "Duty is not for me one climate and love another; duty makes my love into the true, temperate climate."
- The husband who feared his wife thought he loved her only from duty wanted to love her with all his soul, "even at the moment when duty allowed him to refrain". But that is exactly what duty enjoins: "duty here is just one thing, truly to love". The "more" one can do is to do what duty enjoins, translating it from the external to the internal. Love and duty are like the "t" and the "e" in the syllable "te". Duty loves true love and "has a mortal hatred of false love, yes, it kills it". The language of duty is in the future tense, to suggest the historical.
- A's "no mishmash" objection: everything else is either duty or love, marriage alone is a "hermaphroditic ambiguity"; where is the boundary, the tribunal, the power to compel? B: even an official's calling is not a sum of appointments; in marriage the main thing is inward, and the check is "you yourself". Duty has been "our love's first and only confidant".

### The close (about pp. 474–475, PDF pp. 413–414)
- "Dixi et animam meam liberavi", I have spoken and freed my soul, meant as a healthy breath (respiratio), not an unburdening.
- The Serbian legend: the giant with the immense appetite sits down with the poor cottager, who first says a prayer, "and behold! there is enough for them both".
- B has written for a fortnight of evenings with A "outside my door". A gets no standing invitation "for ever", but may come when he wants, "always welcome".
