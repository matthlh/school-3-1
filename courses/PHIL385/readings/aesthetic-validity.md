# PHIL 385 — Reading: *Either/Or*, "The Aesthetic Validity of Marriage"

## B's argument for marriage

- This is the first letter in Part II of *Either/Or*. B, the married Judge William, writes to A, the young aesthete whose papers make up Part I.
- A thinks a romance is exciting at the beginning and becomes dull once the lovers marry. B takes that objection seriously. Here, **aesthetic** means the beauty or poetry the lovers find in their life together. The **ethical** enters when they promise to care for that love through whatever happens.
- B has two tasks. First, he asks what makes marriage beautiful in the first place. Second, he asks how the couple can sustain that beauty as their lives change. His answer is that their promise gives first love a history. Keeping it gives them a chance to develop trust and fidelity over the years.
- Kraal makes the argument this way. A lasting relationship will eventually demand patience, honesty and responsibility. If those demands were enemies of love, love would fail as soon as it faced demands it could not overcome. B thinks they help love last. On A's own standard of what makes life beautiful, the ethical commitments improve the relationship.

The essay is on PDF pp. 334–414. Hannay's Penguin page numbers below are approximate unless marked otherwise. Exam 4 covers both letters. ### Why B writes to A (Hannay around p. 384, PDF pp. 334–341)

- B writes a letter so that he can speak urgently to A about his own life. He cares for A as a son, a brother and a friend. He is also worried about what A is becoming. He says he is warning A about himself and warning the world about A.
- A is good at finding striking beginnings. He remembers catching a girl's eye in a mirror and seeing her blush. He studies other people's lives the same way. When a poorhouse woman wishes for five dollars, he gives her the money to see how she responds to what looks like a stroke of fate.
- B calls this living by theft. The people A observes lose nothing, but A spends his time collecting moments from their lives. He leaves his own commitments open, becomes a guest in everyone else's home and mistakes exposing illusions for accomplishing something.
- B's own marriage gives him a reason to write. His wife is his first and only love, and he sees their marriage as the continual renewal of that first love. He calls defending marriage part of his vocation as a husband, using *pro aris et focis*, for hearth and home. He would write a whole book if it could save even one marriage.

## First love and the reflective age

### What romantic love promises (Hannay around pp. 393–397, PDF pp. 342–346)

- Romantic love is **immediate**. The lovers experience it directly, sometimes at first sight, before they have weighed any reasons. B describes it in terms of natural necessity, beauty and the **stamp of the eternal**. The lovers feel drawn to each other and understand their love as something that should last.
- For B, this claim to last is what distinguishes love from lust. Sensual pleasure can make an instant feel like eternity. Carrying love through time takes a decision of the will.
- Old romances get their drama from obstacles outside the couple, such as a cruel father. A newer plot might solve the problem of money with a rich uncle's death in the fifth act. Both stories end with the wedding. B wants to know what the couple does once they can actually live together.

### How the reflective age responds

- The **immoral path** keeps passion and gives up marriage. Byron's picture of love as heaven and marriage as hell is B's example. When passion fades, being loved by someone you no longer love becomes the new form of unhappy love. A marriage that either person can cancel by giving notice follows the same logic.
- The **respectable path** keeps marriage and gives up romantic love. In a **marriage of reason**, or marriage of convenience, the couple calculates what arrangement will work. For B, both paths lose love's claim to the eternal. One trusts a passing intensity, and the other trusts circumstances that can change.
- B calls one reason for avoiding commitment **melancholy**. **Egoistic melancholy** worries about one's own future, as when someone wonders whether fate has another partner in store. **Sympathetic melancholy** worries on the other person's behalf, as when someone fears they might let a partner down. Both turn every day into another examination of whether to commit.
- B attributes the modern retreat from marriage to cowardice and self-indulgence. He gives a different reason for the medieval retreat. People then ranked the unmarried life as more perfect.

## How marriage preserves first love

### From falling in love to making a vow (Hannay around pp. 398–415, PDF pp. 347–362)

- Mozart's Page in *The Marriage of Figaro* shows what comes before first love. He feels desire and is looking for someone, but his longing has yet to settle on a particular person. B calls first love an **absolute wakefulness** because the lover is now drawn to one actual person.
- First love is sensual and feels eternal to the lovers. It unites **necessity and freedom** because the attraction feels irresistible and the lover freely embraces it. It also unites **the universal and the singular**. Falling in love is a shared human experience that each of these lovers lives with this one person.
- B thinks marriage begins with the couple already in love. He compares marrying in the hope that love will appear later to putting two doves in a cage. Suspicion or calculation would also undermine the love that marriage starts from.
- B calls the religious change **higher concentric immediacy**. The love still has the same centre, the person one already loves. Thanking God for it gives that same love a religious meaning. B's swimming-belt image makes the point that the lovers can accept this support before they face trouble.
- The vow adds the ethical element. The lovers freely undertake to keep loving the person they already love. Kraal dated the Christian marriage vow to around 1100 in lecture. This is how B puts **one loves only once** into practice. First love can keep shaping the present as the couple renews it. B accordingly describes the Church as suspicious of second marriages.
- B calls married love **preferential love** because it singles out one person. Spiritual love extends to everyone. The concentration of love on one person is both the strength and the limitation of preferential love.
- B calls husbands who secretly wish they were still engaged the real traitors to marriage. Their nostalgia treats the engagement as the only time they were genuinely in love and gives up on renewing it now. Seducers never entered marriage, and divorced spouses made their break openly.

### A's objections to the altar

- A thinks a religion that calls God spirit must reject sensual beauty. B understands **flesh** in the Christian sense as selfishness, which can also occur in spiritual life. He sees room for innocent sensual pleasure within Christian marriage.
- A objects that the ceremony makes the groom the bride's master and treats her as a sinner. B turns the charge of mastery back on A, who already seeks control through seduction. B takes the ceremony to speak about woman's lot in general and says that woman is nearer to God.
- A thinks fidelity loses its freedom if God commands it. B replies that lovers can freely ask God to witness their promise. People already look for a witness when they swear love by the moon. Thanking God also helps the lover see the beloved as a gift and give up the pride of conquest.

### Why B rejects reasons for marrying (Hannay around pp. 416–427, PDF pp. 363–372)

- B says marriage has its **teleology** within itself. Its aim is the shared life of the couple. A separate goal makes the marriage a means of getting something. That makes the value of the marriage depend on whether it can deliver the result.
- **Building character.** At the tea table, an official says marriage is a school for character. B wishes such a husband a Xanthippe for a wife. The joke turns his stated aim against him. A difficult marriage could give him exactly the training he asked for.
- **Having children.** B treats children as a blessing the couple can receive. He rejects treating procreation as the purpose for which they marry. He points to Genesis, where God says it is bad for man to be alone and then blesses the couple.
- **Getting a home.** B objects to the bored bachelor who wants a wife to provide his own comfort. He allows for the ordinary person who speaks of having a home as a calling and takes responsibility for it.
- This is what B means when he says that marriage should have no **why**. It can bring benefits such as children or a home. The couple discovers them in the life they have chosen together.

### What the wedding does (Hannay around pp. 428–431, PDF pp. 373–376)

- A thinks the ceremony makes an individual couple sound like another entry on a schedule. B thinks it connects this singular marriage with the history of marriage, going back to Adam and Eve. The couple is both unique and an instance of something shared.
- B reads the Genesis references to sin, childbirth, obedience and work as part of the human situation into which the couple enters. He thinks first love has the courage to face those conditions.
- The Church takes the couple's love for granted and asks for a vow before God and the congregation. B calls the result the **transfiguration of first love**. The love they already have takes on a religious meaning and a commitment for the future.

## How marriage gains beauty over time

### What the spouses acquire (Hannay around pp. 433–436, PDF pp. 377–380)

- First love has the intensity of a beginning. It has yet to develop an **inner history**, a record of how the lovers have shaped their relationship through what they live through together.
- The vow gives the spouses something to act on when hardship comes. For example, they can talk about a conflict honestly and learn how to trust each other through it. The trust they earn becomes part of the relationship. This is what B means when he says that the aesthetic lies **in the acquired**.
- B calls marriage poetic because the spouses can live the love that the romance promised at its beginning. The finite events of their lives give the promise specific occasions on which to keep it.

### Privacy outside and honesty inside (Hannay around pp. 437–450, PDF pp. 381–392)

- B wants the couple to keep the intimate parts of their marriage private among other people. They can still make their vow publicly and live among others.
- A imagines living in a castle where he and his wife occupy different parts of the building and remain mysterious to each other. B thinks love becomes possible at a deeper level when the partners know each other well enough to speak openly.
- B's examples of harmful secrecy include the husband who treats his wife as weak and the husband who carefully stages her whole world for her. In Goethe's *Elective Affinities*, B thinks that Edward could have changed the course of the affair by confiding in his wife. B advises against marrying if a person carries a secret they believe they could never reveal.

### Adversity, habit and possession (Hannay around pp. 451–466, PDF pp. 393–406)

- B thinks that the couple can work through hardships together and make the response part of their inner history. He calls **habit** unfree repetition and reserves the word for the bad. The same action can be freely renewed each day. He compares the beauty of regularity to an even tempo in music.
- A calls himself a conqueror who cannot possess. B calls **possession** conquering in reverse. Conquest takes pride and force. Continuing to share a life with someone takes humility, patience and contentment. B sees the religious element as helping a person make that change.
- In **external history**, the hero strives for an outward result, such as winning the beloved. A work of art can represent the decisive moment. **Internal history** starts when the couple already has the relationship. Patience and fidelity become visible in the life the couple lives day after day.
- The knight waits fifteen years for the moment he can be with his beloved. B says that the knight has killed time by waiting for it to pass. The husband is faithful through fifteen years of being together. His fidelity gives those years a place in the love, so B says he **preserves time in eternity**.
- Healthy love lives in both **hope** and **recollection**. The couple can look forward to its future and draw on its past. B's image is that the silver wedding anniversary comes before the golden one. Marriage gives passing years a chance to add value.

## Duty and the end of the letter

### Why B calls duty love's friend (Hannay around pp. 467–473, PDF pp. 407–412)

- A pictures the duty of marriage as a corporal's cane that comes out when affection fades. B pictures the duty of first love as a conductor's baton. The vow guides the love because the person accepts the duty as their own.
- B's example is a husband who wants his wife to know he loves her with his whole soul, even when he could get away with doing less. B says that this is precisely what his duty asks of him. The duty is **truly to love**. It takes more than going through a list of required acts.
- A asks where to draw the boundary between love and duty and who has the power to enforce it. B points to an official's calling, which also takes more than keeping a list of appointments. For B, the person who made the promise must examine their own motives. He calls the **aesthetic, the ethical and the religious** the three great allies.

### How B closes (Hannay around pp. 474–475, PDF pp. 413–414)

- B writes *Dixi et animam meam liberavi*, meaning that he has spoken and freed his soul. In the Serbian story he tells, a poor cottager prays before sharing a meal with a giant, and there is enough for them both.
- B spent a fortnight of evenings writing with A in mind. He ends by telling A that he can visit whenever he wants and will always be welcome.
