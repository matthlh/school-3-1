# PHIL 385 — Reading: *Either/Or*, "The Aesthetic Validity of Marriage"

The [lecture 18 page](../lectures/18-aesthetic-validity-of-marriage.md) has your notes and Kraal's argument. The essay is on PDF pp. 334–414. The exact PDF pages appear alongside approximate page numbers for Alastair Hannay's Penguin Classics translation (1992). The Penguin estimates use Kraal's p. 384 and Owen's pp. 399 and 443 as reference points and can be off by a page or two.

## B's argument for marriage

- "The Aesthetic Validity of Marriage" opens Part II of *Either/Or*. It is the first of B's two letters to A.
- B is Judge William, a married civil servant. A is the young aesthete whose papers make up Part I.
- A thinks marriage kills the poetry of love. B argues that marriage is more beautiful and more worth living than romance even on A's terms.
- B has two tasks. First, he argues that marriage has aesthetic worth, or that it can be beautiful. Second, he explains how marriage can keep that beauty through time and hardship.
- Kraal's reading is that the ethical way of life wins even when you judge it by the aesthetic way of life's own standards.
- Exam 4 covers this essay on Fri Nov 20. The next reading is "Equilibrium" (Nov 13–18). The [question bank](../02-questions.md) has a section called "Reading: The Aesthetic Validity of Marriage".

### The letter (about p. 384, PDF p. 334)

- B is a public official "accustomed to writing in folio". He chooses the letter form because it allows "admonitory and urgent tones" and keeps A from arguing in general terms.
- B wrote the opening lines last. Together with the closing lines, they frame the argument as a letter.
- B says, "To defend is to indict". A married man would say "marriage, that's life's aesthetic". A young man would say "love, you are life's aesthetic". Neither sees why marriage needs defending.
- Each of B's tasks opens with a short "polemical prologue" that answers A's sarcasm.
- B takes it as his vocation as a husband to defend marriage "pro aris et focis", meaning for hearth and home. He would write a book if it could save a single marriage.
- B's wife is "the first" and only person he has loved. He calls their marriage "the continual rejuvenation of our first love" because he renews it through action.

### B's portrait of A (about pp. 384–392, PDF pp. 334–341)

- B loves A "like a son, a brother, a friend". He also loves him "with the fear and trembling of a religious love" because he thinks A has gone astray.
- B says A is "not really an enemy of marriage". A mocks it with an ironic glance and watches other people's marriages out of "hypochondriacal curiosity".
- A is brilliant and drawn to beginnings. B predicts his life "will be nothing but approach-runs" because he wants "the first rapture of love" and "loves the accidental". One example is a girl who blushed when their eyes met in the mirror.
- A is "child one moment, old man the next" and wants to play "a little Zeus".
- B says, "You live from theft". A collects images of other people's happiest moments. The people lose nothing, while A loses his time, peace of mind and patience to live.
- One of the two poorhouse women wished for five dollars. A had already taken out the bill when he gave it to her. He wanted to play fate and see whether she would thank or curse providence.
- B says what A lacks "entirely, is faith" and that his behaviour "borders on insanity".
- B says, "I am warning you, therefore, not about the world but about yourself and I am warning the world about you." He would warn a daughter against A.
- A is "the epitome of all possibility". He keeps every mood as "a constant possibility" and so is "not available for any charge".
- B later calls A "a stranger and an alien in the world" and compares him to the Flying Dutchman alone in his kayak. Every family welcomes him as a guest, which B calls a humiliation.
- B tells A, "You have come to a halt at the destroying of illusions". He calls that position "a new illusion" and concludes "you accomplish nothing".

## First love and the reflective age

### Romantic love (about pp. 393–397, PDF pp. 342–346)

- Romantic love is immediate. In the old romances, "to see her and to love her were one and the same".
- Romantic love rests on natural necessity and beauty. Its sense of the eternal gives it nobility. B says, "What distinguishes love from lust is its having the stamp of the eternal."
- Sensuality is "the instantaneous". It can make a moment "a little eternity", while true eternity requires "a determination of the will".
- The old romances end where B thinks they should begin. The lovers face four acts of external trials and unite in the fifth. B says "the reader is none the wiser" about how the love lasts.
- In more recent stories, money takes the place of the cruel father as an external obstacle. The usual solution is "a rich uncle dying in the fifth".

### The two responses to romantic love

- The **immoral path** keeps passion and gives up marriage. It recovers the temporal "refined again in a sensual eternity, in the eternal instant of the embrace". Byron treats love as heaven and marriage as hell.
- On the immoral path, unhappy love means being loved by someone you no longer love. Its more respectable form is a temporary marriage you can leave by giving notice, like a change of address. B calls this "a formal protest against marriage".
- The **respectable path** is the marriage of convenience, or "the marriage of reason". Its participants take love to be an illusion and concern themselves with "the prose of life".
- B says the marriage of convenience offers only despair as solace. Calculation is temporal, so this marriage loses the eternal and is both immoral and unaesthetic.
- The two paths set love and marriage against each other. One keeps love and rejects marriage. The other keeps marriage and gives up love.

### Melancholy and the fear of commitment

- B calls melancholy "the defect of our time". **Egoistic melancholy** is fear for one's own sake, as in "perhaps fate will tie me to another".
- **Sympathetic melancholy** is fear for oneself for the other person's sake. B calls it "more painful and also somewhat nobler".
- Both forms of melancholy treat each day as "examination day". B attributes the age's rejection of marriage to cowardice and self-indulgence. He says the Middle Ages rejected it because people thought unmarried life was more perfect.

## How marriage preserves first love

### The religious and ethical elements (about pp. 398–400, PDF pp. 347–349)

- B credits Christianity with perfecting marriage. He says neither Judaism nor the pagan nations, "for all the sensuality of the Orient and for all the beauty of Greece", reflected deeply enough on the contrast between the sexes.
- Beginning conjugal love with doubt would separate it from romantic love. B says religion brings first love "into a higher concentric immediacy", a fuller form of the same love, "where rational reflection ends".
- The substance of marriage is "the being in love". Without love, sharing a life is "a satisfaction of sensual desire or an association, a partnership".
- Marriage presupposes love "as something present, not past". B likens people who marry and wait for love to follow to "dove-fanciers" shutting two doves in a cage.
- Marriage has an ethical and religious element and is based on resignation. Being in love alone lacks these features.
- B calls husbands who secretly long for their engagement the real traitors of marriage. They sit "like lunatics each in his matrimonial cell". Seducers never married, and the divorced rebelled openly.
- If the husbands were right, love would belong to the engagement and no one should marry.

### What B means by first love (about pp. 401–415, PDF pp. 350–362)

- "First love" is "a battle-cry" for B and the signal to open fire for A.
- For one kind of person, the first experience remains "the propulsive, infinite impulse" and is constantly renewed. The other kind moves farther away from it.
- The less repeatable an experience is, the more its first occurrence matters. An experience that has the eternal in it cannot be repeated.
- The philistine who looks for a partner in the newspaper rules out first love. The state before first love is "love makes blind", which B sees in Mozart's Page in *Figaro*, in "this dreaming yet searching state".
- First love itself is "an absolute wakefulness". It is directed at "a single, definite, actual object" who is all that matters to the lover.
- First love includes sensuality. Sensuality as a separate thing appears only in reflection. First love presupposes itself in both directions into eternity.
- First love is "the unity of freedom and necessity". The lover is drawn by an irresistible power and is free in it. It is also immediately a unity of "the universal and the singular".
- B offers to be "the noble father" and let the couple marry without hard-hearted fathers or sphinxes. He says, "in the name of God let them come together".
- The couple relate their love to God by thanking him. Their love is "assumed into a higher concentricity" without changing its character or adding reflection. B compares this to a swimming belt worn by someone who has never been in danger.
- Their intention to keep their love, expressed in the vow, brings it into the ethical.

### A's objections to the altar

- A objects that the Christian God is spirit and negates beauty and sensuality. B says flesh means selfishness, which can include spiritual conduct. Christianity can take up innocent sensuality, and Scripture has "no special blessing for bachelors".
- A says the bride stands "as though to be chastised", the groom is made her master, and she is declared a sinner. B says A is already her master and the Church proclaims sin as woman's lot in general. He calls woman "meek" and "much nearer to God than man".
- A objects that a third power commands the lovers' fidelity. B says the religiously developed seek that power themselves. He points out that lovers already swear by the moon.
- B calls the wedding the lovers' "metamorphosis into bride and bridegroom". He says a man's weakness is to think he conquered the woman he loves. Thanking God humbles him.
- B says, "it is in truth far more beautiful to take the beloved as a gift from the hand of God than to have subdued the whole world to conquer her". Marriage involves "daily possession throughout a whole lifetime".
- B says the matrimonial "we" and "my" sound deeper than the erotic. He says, "Let Don Juan keep his leafy bower, the knight the dark heaven and its stars; marriage has its heaven even higher."
- B says, "One loves only once". The aesthetic, the religious and the ethical agree, and marriage puts the claim into practice. B says the Church looks with suspicion on a second marriage.
- B distinguishes people who long for the ideal from those unwilling to hold on to it. He calls the second group the real seducers.
- Marriage keeps first love's infinitude and unity of opposites. Its inner infinitude is "an eternal life", and it adds a further opposition, the spiritual.
- Earthly love is **preferential love** because it comes to rest in one person. B calls this both its defect and its advantage. Spiritual love loves everyone.

### Why B rejects reasons for marrying (about pp. 416–427, PDF pp. 363–372)

- B says marriage "has its teleology in itself". Its purpose is part of marriage itself. He says, "The less 'why' the more love." He calls finite reasons "a swarm".
- B gives three respectable reasons for marrying and calls each one-sided and "as ridiculous as they are anaesthetic and irreligious".
  - **Building character.** A encourages an official at the tea-table to say one marries "because marriage is a good school for character". B wishes every such husband "a Xanthippe for his wife" and says "the fault is precisely that he has a purpose".
  - **Having children.** Genesis says "it was not good that man should be alone" and "He blessed them". States have rewarded fertility and Christianity has rewarded celibacy. B dismisses "Stud-farm purposes" while treating children as a blessing.
  - **Getting a home.** B says the bored bachelor marries for his comfort and makes a young woman a "galley-slave". He allows that ordinary people can marry for a home when it is a duty and vocation. A, "a stranger and an alien", has no home.
- B would paint the poor woman in the square with her baby if he were a painter. He gives the Chinese saying "bring your children up well, and you will come to know what you owe your parents".
- B calls money, jealousy, the prospect of a spouse dying and inheritances despicable reasons to marry.
- Marriage is on the same footing as first love when it needs no separate justification. B says, "The aesthetic in marriage is this: that it conceals a multitude of 'why's' which life reveals in all their blessedness."

### The wedding ceremony (about pp. 428–431, PDF pp. 373–376)

- A objects that the ceremony makes the couple sound like numbers, "Mr N.N. and the virtuous Miss N.N., at ten o'clock". B says it connects them to Adam and Eve and makes each marriage "an individual and a symbol at the same time".
- The ceremony says that sin entered the world with marriage and is everyone's lot. It does not say "you are now about to commit a sin". B calls being born in sin the highest expression of a child's dignity.
- The punishments are pain in childbirth, obedience of the woman to the man, and bread in the sweat of the brow. B says first love faces these without cowardice.
- B calls woman "at once more perfect and more imperfect than the man". God made her "a helpmeet", and the man is the one who leaves father and mother.
- The ceremony "lets what was already in motion proceed openly". Its questions make the relationship firm. The Church "is not a match-maker". It assumes the couple are in love and requires a vow.
- B concludes the first task by calling marriage "the transfiguration of first love, and is even more beautiful than it".

## How marriage gains beauty over time

### What the spouses acquire (about pp. 433–436, PDF pp. 377–380)

- First love is "an unreal An-sich" with no inner history. B compares it to a sleepwalker who falls when someone says his name.
- Marital love is "armed" because the spouses' intention directs their will inward. Their love becomes historical by taking in what happens to them.
- B says, "The aesthetic lies not in the immediate but in the acquired." The beauty of marriage develops through the spouses' life together.
- Marriage is "the immediacy which has mediacy in itself, the infinity which has the finite in itself, an eternity which has the temporal in itself". B calls it ideal in both the classical and romantic sense.
- The young men say "de omnibus dubitandum". B answers that personal doubt has a specific object and that love is about giving.
- B says, "It is marriage that is poetic." First love lacks "the second aesthetic ideal, the element of history". Similarly, a faith that worked miracles would have no history. The history of faith comes from making it one's own in life.
- Romantic love never imagines a history. Prosaic love has a short, pedestrian history. Experimental love has no continuous history.

### Privacy outside and honesty inside (about pp. 437–450, PDF pp. 381–392)

- A fights "the congregation" like a madman who thought his room was full of flies. He would give every wedding guest a douceur because he thinks money removes relationships.
- The words are "before God and the congregation". B accepts a quiet wedding and says banns from the pulpit do not endanger love.
- B calls outward privacy "the absolute condition for the safeguarding of the aesthetic in marriage". The couple can live among people while keeping their relationship private.
- Within the marriage, B calls for "openheartedness, uprightness, publicity on the largest conceivable scale". This takes courage.
- In A's imagined castle, he lives on the ground floor and his wife lives in the left wing of the first floor. He wants them to stay strangers enough to be interesting.
- B replies, "You fear that when the enigma has gone love will cease, while I believe that only when it has gone does love begin." He says A clings to immediacy as a category of nature.
- B says secrecy never makes a happy marriage. He gives the examples of treating the wife as a slave, calling her weak out of "misplaced solicitude", and arranging her world like Potemkin's landscapes.
- B says, "Falsehood! The woman is just as strong as the man, maybe stronger". The gifted young husband is proud that "she owes everything to me". B tells spouses to "live on a good understanding".
- B says, "Honesty, openheartedness, revelation, understanding, that is the life-principle in marriage." He advises against marrying if you have a secret "which cannot be dragged out of you without costing you your life".
- Goethe's *Elective Affinities* illustrates the consequences of secrecy. If Edward had confided in his wife, B says the affair would have been "a divertissement in the drama of the marriage".

### Adversity, habit and possession (about pp. 451–466, PDF pp. 393–406)

- B says spouses must turn outside troubles inward and work through them together. He has experienced few adversities himself and believes "nothing is capable of crushing the aesthetic in a person".
- B recalls Luther's claim that one has never heard of a Christian dying of hunger. He says a husband (Aegtemand) proves himself a genuine man (ægte Mand).
- B says, "If love can be preserved, the aesthetic, too, can be preserved; for love itself is the aesthetic."
- "Habit" means something bad and unfree. B says regularity can be beautiful, as in an even tempo in music. Despair "can never be a task; it is a convenience".
- B says A judges marriage abstractly and overlooks how it develops. If A cannot endure it, "you will have to look about for another auditorium".
- A says he is "a conqueror who cannot possess". B says "possession is conquering in reverse" and compares its difficulty to going downhill, which takes more strength than going uphill.
- Conquest takes pride, violence, greed, eating and drinking. Possession takes humility, patience, contentment, praying and fasting. Religion "will dethrone the conqueror and let the possessor come into view".

### Outer and inner history

- **External history** concerns a goal outside the person. **Internal history** begins with possession and concerns what the person becomes through it. B says, "Only internal history is true history."
- Art and poetry favour external history because a moment can show a hero's pride, courage, conquest or death. It cannot show a lifetime of humility and patience, like the cross-bearer who takes up his cross each day.
- B orders the arts from space to time. Sculpture comes first, then painting (Schelling), then music, which vanishes in time, and then poetry, the most complete art.
- Beauty beyond representation is realized "by being lived". The highest is to feel oneself "a character in the play written by God", at once "composer and composition".
- The knight is faithful for fifteen years until his decisive moment. The husband is faithful for fifteen years in the marriage itself and acquires his fidelity in the process.
- The husband's enemy is "that most dangerous enemy which is time". The knight "has killed time". The husband "has not killed time but saved and preserved it in eternity". B recalls the man in hell who asked "What time is it?" and heard the devil answer "An eternity."
- B describes married love as faithful, constant, humble, patient, forbearing, indulgent, sincere, contented, observant, persistent, willing and joyful. It is "the everyday and also the divine", or "the divine through being the everyday".

### Repetition, hope and recollection

- For visible signs of the erotic, B mentions a Paraguay tribe woken by a bell for marital duties, A's "look" stretched into an eternity, and "decies repetita placebunt". Repeated signs renew healthy love.
- People tend to live mainly in hope or mainly in recollection. B says a healthy person lives in both. Recollection "puts a cross on the note of the instant".
- The ages of the world run gold, silver, copper and iron. In marriage, "the silver wedding comes first and then the golden wedding". People find a bachelor's jubilee laughable.
- B compares A to a neurasthenic who needs noise. He says, "for you a turbulent sea is a picture of life, for me the still, deep water", and points to the small stream that stays the same while changing.
- B lets A have the whole verse from Oehlenschläger and keeps the last words, "and then, too, innocence".

## Duty and the end of the letter

### Why B calls duty love's friend (about pp. 467–473, PDF pp. 407–412)

- A imagines marriage behind closed doors, where "out comes the cane and we are told it's a duty". B distinguishes the corporal's cane of duty from the conductor's baton of first love.
- Duty need not compel while first love continues. B says A's fear of compulsion shows that he doubts the eternity of first love and that, for him, "love is clearly not the highest thing".
- B says, "You regard duty as the enemy of love, I regard it as its friend." If duty were an enemy that love could not defeat, "then love is not the true victor". On B's argument, A's defence of love would end up disparaging it.
- B calls the aesthetic, the ethical and the religious "the three great allies". He says life loses meaning if they come apart, and A's "do it or do not do it, you will regret both" would be justified.
- B says, "Duty is not for me one climate and love another; duty makes my love into the true, temperate climate."
- A husband feared his wife thought he loved her only from duty. He wanted to love her with all his soul "even at the moment when duty allowed him to refrain". B says this is what duty already asks, since "duty here is just one thing, truly to love".
- The "more" the husband can do is make an external duty his own. B likens love and duty to the "t" and the "e" in "te".
- Duty loves true love and "has a mortal hatred of false love, yes, it kills it". B uses the future tense for duty to suggest how it takes shape through history.
- A's "no mishmash" objection is that everything else is either duty or love, while marriage is a "hermaphroditic ambiguity". He asks where the boundary is, who judges it and who can compel it.
- B says even an official's calling is more than a list of appointments. What matters in marriage is inward, and the check is "you yourself". He calls duty "our love's first and only confidant".

### The close (about pp. 474–475, PDF pp. 413–414)

- B says, "Dixi et animam meam liberavi", meaning I have spoken and freed my soul. He means a healthy breath (respiratio) instead of relief from a burden.
- In the Serbian legend, a giant with an immense appetite eats with a poor cottager. The cottager says a prayer, "and behold! there is enough for them both".
- B wrote for a fortnight of evenings with A "outside my door". He gives A no standing invitation "for ever". A can come when he wants and is "always welcome".
