## What the Preface is doing
One idea per bullet. Read once, then let the quiz do the rest.

- **Your one idea is the right one.** The Preface opens by doubting the philosophical thesis that "the outer is the inner and the inner is the outer". That is Hegel's formula. Eremita says people carry secrets their outside never shows, so the outer does not reveal the inner.
- **The desk story acts that doubt out.** Seven years earlier Eremita saw an old writing desk (a secretaire) at a second-hand dealer's. He offered too little, was refused, and eventually bought it anyway. Before a trip to the country he needed money from a drawer that stuck. He hit the desk with a hatchet. The blow sprang open a hidden compartment full of papers. A desk he knew from the outside had an inside he never suspected.
- **The papers are two sets by two unknown men.** A's papers are aesthetic essays and aphorisms, on fine vellum in quarto with a wide margin. B's papers are letters to A, ethical in tone, on full sheets of beehive paper in split columns, the way legal documents are written. The papers give no information at all about the first author, so Eremita invents the name A. From the letters he learns that the second was called Wilhelm and was an assessor in a court, without it being said which court, which is why B is known as Judge William. He uses A and B rather than mixing a real name with an invented one.
- **Nested authors.** The last of A's papers is "The Seducer's Diary". A claims he only found it and copied it. Eremita does not believe him and suspects A wrote it. He compares the layers to a Chinese puzzle box: one author enclosed inside another. Count the layers: Kierkegaard, then Victor Eremita, then A, then Johannes the Seducer.
- **Ordering.** Eremita left A's essays in the order he found them and says he let chance rule, because he could not tell whether that order was chronological or meaningful. Only the loose slips of aphorisms had to be given a place. He put them first, titled Diapsalmata with the motto "ad se ipsum" (to himself), as a preliminary to the longer essays. The word and the motto were A's own, written on the slips; applying them to the whole set was Eremita's doing. B's letters were already in order, but had no titles, since letters do not carry titles, so Eremita supplied those. Volume 1 is A, volume 2 is B. Volume 2 ends with a sermon by a Jutland pastor, a friend of B, which B forwarded to A.
- **Why "Either/Or".** The thought came to him that the papers could belong to one man who had gone through both movements. He admits it is unhistorical and improbable, says he could not give it up, and it is what decided the title. The book gives no verdict. When you finish it, A and B are forgotten and only the two views of life stand facing each other, expecting no final decision in particular persons. The reader has to choose.
- **Signature.** The title page says "edited by Victor Eremita" (Latin: the victorious hermit). The Preface itself is signed "The Editor", Copenhagen, November 1842. The book came out in February 1843.
- **What it is prefacing, in one line.** It sets up the fiction that makes the whole book work: found papers, unknown authors, an editor who takes no side, and a reader left to decide. That is the move Kraal described in lecture 1: a pseudonym presents a perspective without endorsing it. The inner/outer doubt is the same point about people: you cannot read a person's view of life off their surface.
