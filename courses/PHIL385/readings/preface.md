## The desk and the hidden papers

- The inward and outward idea in your notes is the right one.
- Victor Eremita opens by doubting the philosophical thesis that "the outer is the inner and the inner is the outer". That is Hegel's formula.
- Eremita says people carry secrets their outward appearance never shows, so the outer does not reveal the inner.
- Seven years earlier, Eremita saw an old writing desk called a secretaire at a second-hand dealer's. He offered too little and was refused, and eventually he bought the desk anyway.
- Before a trip to the country, Eremita needed money from a drawer that had stuck. He struck the desk with a hatchet, and the blow sprang open a hidden compartment full of papers.
- The desk story acts out the opening doubt. Eremita knew the desk from the outside, and it had an inside he never suspected.

## The papers and their authors

### A and B

- The papers are two sets by two unknown men.
- A's papers are aesthetic essays and aphorisms. They are written on fine vellum in quarto with a wide margin.
- B's papers are letters to A with an ethical tone. They are written in split columns on full sheets of beehive paper, the way legal documents are written.
- The papers give no information at all about the first author, so Eremita invents the name A.
- The letters show that the second author was called Wilhelm and was an assessor in a court, without saying which court. This is why B is known as Judge William.
- Eremita uses A and B rather than mixing a real name with an invented one.

### The diary within A's papers

- A's last paper is "The Seducer's Diary". A claims he only found it and copied it.
- Eremita does not believe A and suspects A wrote the diary himself.
- Eremita compares the layers to a Chinese puzzle box, with one author enclosed inside another. The four layers are Kierkegaard, Victor Eremita, A and Johannes the Seducer, in that order.

## Eremita's editorial decisions

### The order of the two volumes

- Eremita kept A's essays in the order he found them. He says he let chance rule, because he could not tell whether that order was chronological or meaningful.
- Only the loose slips of aphorisms had to be given a place. Eremita put them first, as a preliminary to the longer essays.
- The title *Diapsalmata* and the motto "ad se ipsum", meaning to himself, were A's own words, written on the slips. Applying them to the whole set was Eremita's doing.
- B's letters were already in order. They had no titles, since letters do not carry titles, so Eremita supplied them.
- Volume 1 contains A's papers. Volume 2 contains B's papers.
- Volume 2 ends with a sermon by a Jutland pastor who was a friend of B. B forwarded the sermon to A.

### The title and the reader's choice

- The thought came to Eremita that both sets of papers could belong to one man who had gone through both movements. He admits the thought is unhistorical and improbable, but he could not give it up, and it decided the title *Either/Or*.
- The book gives no verdict.
- When you finish the book, A and B are forgotten. Only the two views of life stand facing each other, expecting no final decision in particular persons.
- The reader has to choose.

### The signature and publication

- The title page says "edited by Victor Eremita". The name is Latin for the victorious hermit.
- The Preface is signed "The Editor", Copenhagen, November 1842. *Either/Or* came out in February 1843.

## What the Preface is prefacing

- The Preface sets up the fiction that makes the whole book work. It gives found papers, unknown authors, an editor who takes no side, and a reader left to decide.
- In lecture 1, Kraal described the same move. A pseudonym presents a perspective without endorsing it.
- The inner and outer doubt makes the same point about people. You cannot read a person's view of life off their surface.
