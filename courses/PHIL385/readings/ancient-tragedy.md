# PHIL 385 — Reading: *Either/Or*, "Ancient Tragedy's Reflection in the Modern" — for Sep 25–28

This essay is on PDF pages 118–138. Kraal's page 143 is PDF page 122, and [lecture 8](../lectures/08-ancient-tragedy-guilt-sorrow-and-fate.md) has the Exam 1 review for the argument covered in lectures 7 and 8.

## The question A asks

- The full title is "Ancient Tragedy's Reflection in the Modern. An Essay in the Fragmentary Endeavour". It is the third of A's papers and follows "The Musical Erotic".
- A reads the paper before the Symparanekromenoi, the same society he addresses in "The Unhappiest One".
- A opens by refusing both extremes. The tragic has not stayed simply unchanged since Aristotle. Ancient and modern tragedy cannot be cut apart either, since the tragic itself joins them.
- Comedy illustrates the point. The criterion "comedy gives rise to laughter" has stayed the same, even though what people laugh at has changed completely. The same holds for the tragic.
- A's aim is not to compare the two conceptions. He wants to find the ancient tragic within the modern, so that true tragedy can come to light there.

## Why the modern age tends toward comedy

- A says his whole age tends toward comedy, because doubt and isolation undermine it. An isolated person asserts himself "numerically", as one person.
- The associations formed to fight isolation only confirm it. An association of a hundred makes the same move when it asserts itself as a hundred.
- A calls these associations "infusoria" in the body of the state. He sees them as a sign of its dissolution, like political clubs in Greece's decline.
- A says the modern age is more melancholy than Greece, so it recognizes responsibility and avoids it. He writes that "everyone wants to rule, no one wants the responsibility".
- A French statesman, for example, would take office again only if the secretary of state were made responsible.
- The isolated person is comic because he asserts his accidental individuality against necessity. A says the deepest comedy would be one accidental person who wanted to be the saviour of the world.
- A calls Christ's appearance the deepest tragedy in one sense and infinitely more in another, because Christ came in the fullness of time and bore the world's sins.

## What makes guilt tragic

### Action and suffering

- Aristotle identifies thought (*dianoia*) and character (*ethos*) as the sources of tragic action. He gives priority to the end (*telos*), so the characters exist for the action.
- In ancient tragedy the action does not come from character alone. It has an admixture of suffering and is "just as much event as action".
- The monologue and chorus retain parts of dialogue that reflection never absorbed.
- The reason is that ancient subjectivity was not fully reflective. The individual rested on the substantial categories of state, family and destiny.
- A calls that substantiality the "authentically fatalistic" element of Greek tragedy.
- So the ancient hero's downfall is partly suffering. The modern hero's downfall is action.
- Modern tragedy gives priority to the hero's situation and character and has no epic foreground. A says "the hero stands and falls entirely on his own deeds" (Kraal's page 143).

> **Key.** Ancient hero's downfall is partly suffering; modern hero's downfall is action. On Kraal's page 143, "the hero stands and falls entirely on his own deeds".

### Guilt between activity and passivity

- Aristotle requires the hero to have guilt (*hamartia*). In Greek tragedy, the guilt, like the action, lies between activity and passivity. That is the tragic collision.
- Greater reflection makes guilt more ethical. Without guilt, the collision disappears. Absolute guilt removes tragic interest. A writes, "Between these two extremes lies the tragic".
- A says the modern age makes the hero responsible for his whole life. Aesthetic guilt becomes ethical guilt, the hero becomes morally bad and evil becomes the object of tragedy.
- A considers evil aesthetically uninteresting and gives Grabbe's *Faust und Don Juan* as an example of a work built on evil.
- A says the modern audience would dismiss the story of a man ruined by an unhappy childhood. The age "won't listen to such effeminacy" and holds everyone responsible without further ado.
- A calls the energy that would make each person the creator of his own fortune an illusion. "In losing the tragic the age gains despair."

### Absolute and relative

- A says every individual is still a child of God, his age, his nation, his family and his friends. The individual has his truth only in these ties.
- A calls the attempt to be absolute inside all that relativity ridiculous. He compares it to a word used adverbially in one construction that then demands to be declined in all five cases like a noun.
- If the individual gives up the claim to be absolute and accepts being relative, the tragic is his. A says only then does he become happy.

## Pity, sorrow and pain

### Three kinds of leniency

- The tragic contains an "infinite leniency". A calls it a maternal love that soothes, and the aesthetic counterpart of divine love and mercy.
- The ethical is strict and harsh. A thief who pleads that his mother was also a thief gets the ruling that he is a thief, not a thief's mother.
- A compares the religious to paternal love. It contains the ethical, but mollified.
- What mollifies the religious is continuity, the same thing that gives the tragic its leniency.
- The aesthetic grants its pause before the opposition of sin is pressed home. The religious grants it only afterward.
- A says the age is conceited enough to disdain tragedy's tears and conceited enough to do without divine mercy. He asks what human life would be with both taken away.

### Aristotle and Hegel on pity

- Aristotle says tragedy arouses fear and pity. For A, fear accompanies the particular event, while pity gives the tragedy its total impression. That is why pity follows the same dialectic as tragic guilt.
- In his *Aesthetics*, Hegel distinguishes ordinary compassion for the finite side of suffering from true tragic pity.
- Hegel says true tragic pity is sympathy with the sufferer's justification. A takes this second point and connects the kinds of compassion to the kinds of tragic guilt.

### Sorrow and pain

- **Sorrow** is suffering without reflection. **Pain** is reflection on suffering.
- Ancient tragedy has deeper sorrow and less pain. Modern tragedy has greater pain and less sorrow.

> **Watch out.** Do not swap these. **Sorrow** is suffering without reflection; **pain** is reflection on suffering. Ancient tragedy has deeper sorrow and less pain; modern tragedy has greater pain and less sorrow.

- A child watching an older person suffer has too little reflection for pain but infinitely deep sorrow. An adult watching a child suffer has greater pain and less sorrow. A clearer idea of guilt brings more pain and less sorrow.
- A says that to feel Greek sorrow one must enter the Greek consciousness. He thinks most modern admiration of Greek tragedy is affectation.
- **Remorse** is the bitterest pain, but it is ethical, not aesthetic. Its complete transparency ends aesthetic interest, and it refuses to be looked at.

> **Definition.** **Remorse** is the bitterest pain, yet it is ethical rather than aesthetic. Because it is fully transparent and refuses to be looked at, it ends aesthetic interest and so lies outside tragedy.

- A says the modern age works toward remorse as its highest tragic interest. He calls this the same confusion that wants to be edified in the theatre and entertained in church.
- Once the individual is left wholly to himself, guilt becomes sin and pain becomes remorse. The tragic disappears, and the spectator, having lost his compassion, says "heaven helps those who help themselves".
- Greater innocence brings deeper sorrow, but pushed to the limit this leaves aesthetics.
- The synthesis of absolute innocence and absolute guilt is metaphysical. A gives this as the reason people have always been ashamed to call Christ's life a tragedy.

### Inherited guilt and Greek examples

- Tragic guilt is more than subjective guilt. It is inherited guilt, a substantial category like original sin.
- Sophocles' trilogy, *Oedipus at Colonus*, *Oedipus Rex* and *Antigone*, turns on inherited guilt.
- The Jews might seem the tragic people, since Jehovah visits the fathers' sins on the children. A says Judaism is too ethically developed for this, since its curses are righteous punishment. The wrath of the Greek gods is aesthetically ambiguous.
- *Philoctetes* is Greek tragedy's own transition from sorrow to pain. It is a tragedy of suffering with a high degree of reflection, yet it is still held by objectivity, since the hero rests in his fate.
- Philoctetes complains that no one understands his pain. A calls the complaint typically Greek. Reflective pain instead wants to be alone with itself.
- True tragic sorrow needs an element of guilt and needs transparency. True tragic pain needs an element of innocence and needs opaqueness.

## The Symparanekromenoi's method

- The society deliberately produces nothing coherent. Its genre is "the art of writing posthumous papers".
- Such papers should feel like a ruin. They are broken off and careless, with the writer's personality imagined as the missing part.
- A uses two related Danish words. The work is an inheritance (*Efterladenskab*), and the talent the society prizes is negligence (*Efterladenhed*). He calls inertia its natural law.

## A's modern Antigone

### The family secret

- A keeps Sophocles' names and facts but makes everything modern.
- He chooses a woman because as a woman she has the substantiality that sorrow needs. As a member of a reflective world, she has reflection enough for pain.
- Her reflection does not refract her out of her sorrow but into it, turning it into pain at every moment.
- Oedipus kills the sphinx and frees Thebes. He also kills his father and marries his mother. He lives happily with Jocasta and is honoured by Thebes.
- No one knows the rest except Antigone. A leaves how she learned it to the reader.
- Certainty throws her into anxiety. A calls **anxiety** the modern tragic.
- Anxiety is reflection. It is the organ by which the subject takes in sorrow gradually, "in constant becoming".

> **Definition.** A calls **anxiety** the modern tragic. It is reflection, the organ by which the subject takes sorrow in gradually, "in constant becoming", and it both loves and fears its object.

- Anxiety both loves and fears its object. A compares it to an erotic glance that desires what it sees.

### Two proofs that anxiety is reflection

1. In language, one is anxious "about something", so the anxiety is separated from its object. The phrase "my sorrow" can name what I sorrow over.
2. In time, one cannot be anxious about the present, only about the past or future, which are reflective. Greek sorrow lives in the present tense.

- A invokes the saying that God first makes mad those he wishes to destroy (*quem deus vult perdere, primum dementat*).
- A gives three modern examples of anxiety.
  - Hamlet is tragic because he suspects his mother's guilt.
  - Robert le Diable asks how he came to cause so much evil.
  - Hogne, begotten by a troll, sees his own shape in the water.

### What changes from the Greek Antigone

- The Greek Antigone is as carefree as any Greek girl and is untroubled by her father's fate. Her guilt lies in the single act of burying her brother.
- Taken alone, as sisterly love defying an arbitrary ban, her act would make a modern subject.
- What makes it Greek is that her fate echoes Oedipus's, so that not an individual but "a little world" goes under.
- A describes the modern Antigone's life as essentially over. The action takes place within her, "a scene of the spirit".
- She is vigorous and proud, not sickly. A says nothing ennobles a person like keeping a secret.
- A calls her the bride of sorrow and, in the aesthetic sense, *virgo mater*, because she carries her secret under her heart.
- Unlike Philoctetes, she wants no one to know her pain. Her solitude is only figurative, and A says that unreality makes her pain real. In his account, the Greeks did not speak figuratively.
- Antigone's guilt lies partly in the burial and partly in her father's fate.
- The dialectic that joins the family's guilt to the individual is objective, not subjective. Essentially it is **piety**.
- If the individual is isolated, the tragic is lost either way. Either he is the sole creator of his destiny and only evil is left, or individuals are mere modifications of eternal substance.

### The modern feature and the dramatic collision

- The modern feature is that Oedipus is now dead, and Antigone does not know whether he himself knew his guilt. She could never confide in him alive, and telling anyone else would disgrace him.
- Her only outlet is public enthusiasm for the honoured king. Even that causes pain because she fears someone will see her suffering.
- Antigone falls mortally in love. Her dowry is her pain, which she can neither hide from a husband nor confide to him.
- The first collision is between her love for her father and her love for herself.
- The second and deeper collision is the sympathetic love of her suitor. His every assurance drives the dart deeper.
- The suitor meets her at Oedipus's grave and pleads in the name of her love for her father. That appeal defeats him.
- Only death brings Antigone peace. A compares her to Epaminondas at Mantinea, who left the arrow in his wound until he heard the battle was won.
- She can say she belongs to her lover only at the instant she no longer does.
- The dead kill her in one sense, as was prophesied of Hercules, because the memory of her father is the cause. The living kill her in another sense, because her unhappy love is the occasion.
