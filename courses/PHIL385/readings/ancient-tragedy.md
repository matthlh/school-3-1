## The essay, start to finish
In my words. Short phrases in quotation marks are the wording worth carrying into an exam.

### Title and frame
- The full heading is "Ancient Tragedy's Reflection in the Modern. An Essay in the Fragmentary Endeavour", read before the Symparanekromenoi, the same society A addresses in "The Unhappiest One".
- It is the third of A's papers, right after "The Musical Erotic".
- A opens by refusing both extremes: the tragic is not simply unchanged from Aristotle, and ancient and modern tragedy cannot be cut apart either, since the tragic itself is what joins them.
- His comparison is with comedy. The criterion "comedy gives rise to laughter" has stayed fixed while what people laugh at has changed completely. The same holds for the tragic.
- The aim is not to compare the two conceptions but to find the ancient tragic inside the modern, so that true tragedy can come to light there.

### The age tends toward comedy
- A says his whole age tends toward comedy, because it is undermined by doubt and isolation.
- To be isolated is to assert oneself "numerically", as one. The associations formed to fight isolation only confirm it, since a hundred asserting themselves as a hundred is the same move. A calls them "infusoria" in the body of the state, a sign it is dissolving, like the political clubs of Greece in its decline.
- The age is more melancholy than Greece, so it knows there is such a thing as responsibility, and runs from it: "everyone wants to rule, no one wants the responsibility". His example is a French statesman who would take office again only if the secretary of state were made responsible.
- The isolated individual is comic because he asserts his accidental individuality against necessity. One accidental person wanting to be the saviour of the world would be the deepest comedy. Christ's appearance is in one sense the deepest tragedy, and in another infinitely more, because he came in the fullness of time and bore the sins of the world.

### Ancient and modern tragedy, with Aristotle's terms
- Aristotle names thought (*dianoia*) and character (*ethos*) as the sources of tragic action, but says the end (*telos*) is the main thing: the characters are there for the sake of the action.
- In ancient tragedy the action does not come from character alone. It has an admixture of suffering, and is "just as much event as action". The monologue and the chorus hold the parts of dialogue that reflection never absorbed.
- The reason is that ancient subjectivity was not fully reflective. The individual rested on the substantial categories of state, family and destiny, and that substantiality is the "authentically fatalistic" element of Greek tragedy.
- So the ancient hero's downfall is partly suffering. The modern hero's downfall is action: situation and character predominate, there is no epic foreground, and "the hero stands and falls entirely on his own deeds" (Kraal's page 143).

### Tragic guilt
- Aristotle requires the hero to have guilt (*hamartia*). In Greek tragedy the guilt, like the action, lies between activity and passivity, and that is the tragic collision.
- The more reflective the subject, the more the guilt becomes ethical. "Between these two extremes lies the tragic": no guilt at all removes the collision, absolute guilt removes tragic interest.
- The modern age throws the hero's whole life on his own shoulders. That turns aesthetic guilt into ethical guilt, the hero becomes bad, and evil, which has no aesthetic interest, becomes the object of tragedy. Grabbe's *Faust und Don Juan* is his example of a work built on evil.
- A second example is the age's taste: a story of a man ruined by an unhappy childhood would get no hearing, because the age "won't listen to such effeminacy" and holds everyone responsible without further ado.
- The energy that would make each person the creator of his own fortune is an illusion. "In losing the tragic the age gains despair."

### Absolute and relative
- Every individual is still a child of God, his age, his nation, his family and his friends, and has his truth only there. To try to be absolute inside all that relativity is ridiculous.
- A's image is a word that was used adverbially in one construction and then demands to be declined in all five cases like a noun.
- If the individual gives up the claim to be absolute and accepts being relative, the tragic is his, and A says only then does he become happy.

### Three kinds of leniency
- The tragic contains an "infinite leniency". From the aesthetic side it is what divine love and mercy are, and A calls it a maternal love that soothes.
- The ethical is strict and harsh. A thief who pleads that his mother was a thief gets a ruling that he is a thief and not a thief's mother.
- The religious is a paternal love: it contains the ethical but mollified, and what mollifies it is the same thing that gives the tragic its leniency, continuity. The aesthetic grants its pause before the opposition of sin is pressed home; the religious grants it only after.
- The age is conceited enough to disdain tragedy's tears and conceited enough to do without divine mercy, and A asks what human life is with both taken away.

### Fear and pity, and Hegel
- Aristotle says tragedy arouses fear and pity. A reads fear as the mood that goes with the particular event and pity as the mood that forms the total impression, which is why pity has the same dialectic as tragic guilt.
- Hegel's *Aesthetics* separates ordinary compassion, concerned with the finite side of suffering, from true tragic pity, and says true pity is sympathy with the sufferer's justification. A takes the second point and ties the kinds of compassion to the kinds of tragic guilt.

### Sorrow and pain
- In ancient tragedy the sorrow is deeper and the pain less; in modern tragedy the pain is greater and the sorrow less. Pain means reflection on suffering; sorrow has no reflection in it.
- The image is a child watching an older person suffer: too little reflection for pain, but infinitely deep sorrow. An adult watching a child suffer has more pain and less sorrow. The clearer the idea of guilt, the more pain and the less sorrow.
- To feel Greek sorrow you must enter the Greek consciousness. A thinks most modern admiration for Greek tragedy is affectation.
- Remorse is the bitterest pain, but it is ethical, not aesthetic. Its total transparency kills aesthetic interest, and it refuses to be looked at. The modern age works toward it as its highest tragic interest, which is the same confusion that wants to be edified in the theatre and entertained in church.
- Once the individual is left wholly to himself, his guilt is sin and his pain is remorse, and the tragic is gone. The spectator, having lost his compassion, says "heaven helps those who help themselves".
- The more innocence, the deeper the sorrow, but pushed to the limit this leaves aesthetics. The synthesis of absolute innocence and absolute guilt is metaphysical, which is why people have always been ashamed to call Christ's life a tragedy.
- Tragic guilt is more than subjective guilt; it is inherited guilt, a substantial category like original sin. Sophocles' trilogy, *Oedipus at Colonus*, *Oedipus Rex* and *Antigone*, turns on it.
- The Jews might seem the tragic people, since Jehovah visits the fathers' sins on the children, but Judaism is too ethically developed: the curses are righteous punishment, while the wrath of the Greek gods is aesthetically ambiguous.
- *Philoctetes* is Greek tragedy's own transition from sorrow to pain, a tragedy of suffering with a high degree of reflection, yet still held by objectivity: the hero rests in his fate. Philoctetes complains that no one understands his pain, which A calls typically Greek. Reflective pain wants to be alone with itself.
- The formula: true tragic sorrow needs an element of guilt, true tragic pain an element of innocence; sorrow needs transparency, pain opaqueness.

### The Symparanekromenoi's method
- The society produces nothing coherent on purpose. Its genre is "the art of writing posthumous papers", which should feel like a ruin: broken off, careless, with the personality imagined as a missing part.
- A plays on two Danish words: what the society produces is an inheritance (*Efterladenskab*), and the genius it prizes is negligence (*Efterladenhed*), with inertia as its natural law.

### The modern Antigone
- A keeps the name and the facts of Sophocles but makes everything modern. He chooses a woman because as a woman she has the substantiality that sorrow needs, and as a member of a reflective world she has reflection enough for pain. Her reflection does not refract her out of her sorrow but into it, turning it into pain at every moment.
- In his version Oedipus has killed the sphinx, freed Thebes, killed his father and married his mother, and lives honoured and happy with Jocasta. No one knows the rest. Only Antigone knows, and how she found out is left to the reader.
- Certainty throws her into anxiety, and anxiety is A's definition of the modern tragic. Anxiety is reflection; it is the organ by which the subject takes sorrow in, gradually, "in constant becoming". It both loves and fears its object, as an erotic glance desires its object.
- Two proofs that anxiety is reflection. Language: one is anxious "about something", so the anxiety is separated from its object, whereas "my sorrow" can name what I sorrow over. Time: one cannot be anxious about the present, only the past or the future, which are reflective, while Greek sorrow lives in the present tense.
- The old saying fits here: whom God wishes to destroy he first makes mad (*quem deus vult perdere, primum dementat*).
- Modern examples of anxiety: Hamlet, tragic because he suspects his mother's guilt; Robert le Diable asking how he came to cause so much evil; Hogne, begotten by a troll, seeing his shape in the water.
- The Greek Antigone is not troubled by her father's fate and lives as carefree as any Greek girl. Her guilt is the single act of burying her brother. Taken alone, as sisterly love against an arbitrary ban, that would be a modern subject; what makes it Greek is that her fate echoes Oedipus's, so that not an individual but "a little world" goes under.
- The modern Antigone's life is essentially over. Her scene is internal, "a scene of the spirit". She is vigorous and proud, not sickly. A says nothing ennobles a person like keeping a secret.
- A's names for her: the bride of sorrow, and in the aesthetic sense *virgo mater*, because she carries her secret under her heart.
- She does not want anyone to know her pain, unlike Philoctetes. Her solitude is only figurative, and that unreality is what makes her pain real; the Greeks did not speak figuratively.
- Her guilt lies partly in the burial and partly in her father's fate. The dialectic that joins family guilt to the individual is objective, not subjective; essentially it is piety. If the individual is isolated, either he is the sole creator of his destiny, leaving only evil, or individuals are mere modifications of eternal substance, and either way the tragic is lost.
- The modern feature: Oedipus is now dead, and Antigone does not know whether he himself knew his guilt. She could never confide in him alive, and confiding in anyone else would disgrace him. Her public enthusiasm for the honoured king is her only outlet, and even that is pain, since she fears anyone seeing her suffer.
- The dramatic collision: Antigone falls mortally in love. Her dowry is her pain, which she can neither hide from a husband nor confide. The first collision is her love for her father against love for herself; the second, the deeper one, is the sympathetic love of her suitor, whose every assurance drives the dart deeper. He meets her at Oedipus's grave and pleads in the name of her love for her father, and so defeats himself.
- Only death gives her peace. The image is Epaminondas at Mantinea, who left the arrow in the wound until he heard the battle was won. She can admit she belongs to her lover only at the instant she no longer does.
- Who kills her? In one sense the dead, as was prophesied of Hercules, since the memory of her father is the cause; in another the living, since her unhappy love is the occasion.
