# PHIL 385 — Reading: *Either/Or*, "Ancient Tragedy's Reflection in the Modern" — for Sep 25–28

This essay is on PDF pages 118–138. Kraal's page 143 is PDF page 122, and [lecture 8](../lectures/08-ancient-tragedy-guilt-sorrow-and-fate.md) has the Exam 1 review for the argument covered in lectures 7 and 8.

## The question A asks

- The full title is "Ancient Tragedy's Reflection in the Modern. An Essay in the Fragmentary Endeavour". It is the third of A's papers and follows "The Musical Erotic".
- A reads the paper before the Symparanekromenoi, the same society he addresses in "The Unhappiest One".
- A wants to find the ancient tragic within the modern so that true tragedy can appear there. He says tragedy has changed since Aristotle, while the tragic itself still connects the two forms.
- Comedy illustrates the point. The criterion "comedy gives rise to laughter" has stayed the same, even though what people laugh at has changed completely.

## Why the modern age tends toward comedy

- A says doubt and isolation push his age toward comedy. An isolated person asserts himself "numerically", as one person.
- An association of a hundred does the same thing when it asserts itself as a hundred. A calls these associations "infusoria" in the body of the state. He sees them as a sign of its dissolution, like political clubs in Greece's decline.
- A says the modern age is more melancholy than Greece, so it recognizes responsibility and avoids it. He writes that "everyone wants to rule, no one wants the responsibility".
- A French statesman, for example, would take office again only if the secretary of state took responsibility.
- The isolated person is comic when he asserts his accidental individuality against necessity. A says the deepest comedy would be one accidental person who wanted to save the world.
- A calls Christ's appearance the deepest tragedy in one sense and infinitely more in another. Christ came in the fullness of time and bore the world's sins.

## What makes guilt tragic

### Action and suffering

- Aristotle identifies thought (*dianoia*) and character (*ethos*) as the sources of tragic action. He gives priority to the end (*telos*), so the characters exist for the action.
- Ancient tragic action includes suffering and is "just as much event as action". The monologue and chorus retain parts of dialogue that reflection never absorbed.
- In A's account, the ancient individual had less fully developed reflection and depended on the state, family and destiny. A calls those ties the "authentically fatalistic" element of Greek tragedy.
- The ancient hero partly suffers a downfall brought about by those ties. Modern tragedy gives priority to the hero's situation and character and dispenses with the epic foreground. As Kraal's page 143 puts it, "the hero stands and falls entirely on his own deeds".

### Guilt between activity and passivity

- Aristotle requires the hero to have guilt (*hamartia*). In Greek tragedy, both the guilt and the action involve what the hero does and what the hero suffers. Their combination produces the tragic collision.
- Greater reflection makes guilt more ethical. Without guilt, the collision disappears. Absolute guilt removes tragic interest. A writes, "Between these two extremes lies the tragic".
- A says the modern age makes the hero responsible for his whole life. Aesthetic guilt becomes ethical guilt, the hero becomes morally bad and evil becomes the subject. A considers evil aesthetically uninteresting and gives Grabbe's *Faust und Don Juan* as an example.
- A says the modern audience would dismiss the story of a man ruined by an unhappy childhood. The age "won't listen to such effeminacy" and simply holds the man responsible.
- A calls the idea that each person creates his own fortune an illusion. "In losing the tragic the age gains despair."

### The individual and his ties

- A says every individual is a child of God, his age, his nation, his family and his friends. These ties give the individual his truth.
- A compares someone claiming to be absolute despite those ties to a word used adverbially that demands to be declined in all five cases like a noun.
- A says a person gains access to the tragic and can become happy only by accepting that he depends on others.

## Pity, sorrow and pain

### Aesthetic, ethical and religious responses

- The tragic offers "infinite leniency". A compares it to a mother's soothing love and calls it the aesthetic counterpart of divine love and mercy.
- The ethical judges strictly. A thief's claim that his mother was also a thief does not alter the ruling about his own theft.
- A compares the religious to paternal love. It softens the ethical through continuity, the same quality that gives the tragic its leniency.
- The aesthetic grants relief before the opposition of sin is pressed home. The religious grants it afterward.
- A says the age is proud enough to reject tragedy's tears and divine mercy. He asks what human life would be with both removed.

### Aristotle and Hegel on pity

- Aristotle says tragedy arouses fear and pity. For A, fear accompanies the particular event, while pity gives the tragedy its overall impression. That is why pity follows the same dialectic as tragic guilt.
- In his *Aesthetics*, Hegel distinguishes ordinary compassion for the finite side of suffering from true tragic pity.
- True tragic pity is sympathy with the sufferer's justification. A adopts this idea and connects the kinds of compassion to the kinds of guilt.

### Sorrow and pain

- **Sorrow** is suffering without reflection. **Pain** is reflection on suffering.
- Ancient tragedy has deeper sorrow and less pain. Modern tragedy has greater pain and less sorrow.
- A child watching an older person suffer has too little reflection for pain and can feel infinitely deep sorrow. An adult watching a child suffer has greater pain and less sorrow. A clearer sense of guilt increases pain and reduces sorrow.
- A says Greek sorrow makes sense from within the Greek consciousness. He thinks most modern admiration of Greek tragedy is affected.
- **Remorse** is the bitterest pain and belongs to the ethical. Its complete transparency ends aesthetic interest, and it refuses to be looked at.
- A says the modern age treats remorse as the height of tragedy. He compares this confusion to looking for edification in a theatre and entertainment in a church.
- Once the individual bears all responsibility, guilt becomes sin and pain becomes remorse. The tragic disappears, and the spectator abandons compassion with the phrase "heaven helps those who help themselves".
- Greater innocence brings deeper sorrow up to a limit. The synthesis of absolute innocence and absolute guilt belongs to metaphysics, which A gives as the reason people have hesitated to call Christ's life a tragedy.

### Inherited guilt and Greek examples

- Tragic guilt can be inherited and therefore exceeds subjective guilt. It is a substantial category like original sin.
- Inherited guilt connects Sophocles' *Oedipus at Colonus*, *Oedipus Rex* and *Antigone*.
- Jehovah's punishment of children for their fathers' sins could make the Jews seem tragic. A says Judaism is too ethically developed for this to work, since the curses are righteous punishment. The Greek gods' anger remains aesthetically ambiguous.
- *Philoctetes* moves from sorrow toward pain within Greek tragedy. The play gives suffering a high degree of reflection, while its hero still accepts his fate.
- Philoctetes complains that no one understands his pain. A calls the complaint typically Greek. Reflective pain instead seeks privacy.
- True tragic sorrow needs some guilt and transparency. True tragic pain needs some innocence and opaqueness.

## The Symparanekromenoi's method

- The society deliberately avoids producing a coherent whole. It practises "the art of writing posthumous papers", which should feel unfinished and careless, like a ruin whose missing part is the writer's personality.
- A uses two related Danish words. The work is an inheritance (*Efterladenskab*), and the talent the society prizes is negligence (*Efterladenhed*). He calls inertia its natural law.

## A's modern Antigone

### The family secret

- A keeps Sophocles' names and facts but places Antigone in a reflective world. He chooses a woman because he associates womanhood with the substantiality sorrow needs. Her reflection draws her further into sorrow and makes it pain at each moment.
- Oedipus kills the sphinx and frees Thebes. He also kills his father and marries his mother. He lives happily with Jocasta and is honoured by Thebes.
- Only Antigone knows the family secret. A leaves how she learned it to the reader.
- Certainty makes her anxious. A calls **anxiety** the modern tragic because it is how reflection takes in sorrow gradually, "in constant becoming".
- Anxiety both loves and fears its object. A compares it to an erotic glance that desires what it sees.

### Two ways to see that anxiety involves reflection

1. In language, being anxious "about something" separates the feeling from its object. The phrase "my sorrow" can name what I grieve over.
2. Anxiety concerns the past or future, both of which require reflection. Greek sorrow concerns the present.

- A invokes the saying that God first makes mad those he wishes to destroy (*quem deus vult perdere, primum dementat*).
- Hamlet becomes tragic when he suspects his mother's guilt. Robert le Diable asks how he caused so much evil. Hogne, begotten by a troll, sees his own shape in the water.

### What changes from the Greek Antigone

- The Greek Antigone is as carefree as any Greek girl and is untroubled by her father's fate. Her guilt lies in the single act of burying her brother.
- In isolation, a sister's love defying an arbitrary ban would make a modern subject. The Greek tragedy connects her fate to Oedipus's, so "a little world" goes under with her.
- A describes the modern Antigone's life as essentially over. The action takes place within her, "a scene of the spirit". She remains vigorous and proud, and A says keeping a secret ennobles a person.
- A calls her the bride of sorrow and, in the aesthetic sense, *virgo mater*, because she carries her secret under her heart.
- Unlike Philoctetes, she wants no one to know her pain. Her solitude is figurative, and A says that unreality makes her pain real. In his account, the Greeks did not speak figuratively.
- The modern Antigone's guilt lies partly in the burial and partly in her father's fate. **Piety** is the objective bond that connects the individual's guilt to the family's.
- Isolating the individual removes the tragic. It leaves either an individual who creates his own destiny and bears all the evil, or individuals who are merely modifications of eternal substance.

### Why she cannot marry

- Oedipus is now dead, and Antigone does not know whether he understood his own guilt. She could never confide in him alive, and telling anyone else would disgrace him.
- Her only outlet is public enthusiasm for the honoured king. It also causes pain because she fears someone will see her suffering.
- Antigone falls mortally in love. A calls her pain her dowry because she can neither hide it from a husband nor tell him about it.
- The first collision is between her love for her father and her love for herself. The deeper collision comes from her suitor's sympathy, since his reassurance increases her pain.
- The suitor pleads at Oedipus's grave in the name of her love for her father. That appeal defeats his own aim.
- Only death brings Antigone peace. A compares her to Epaminondas at Mantinea, who left the arrow in his wound until he heard the battle was won.
- She can say she belongs to her lover only at the instant she no longer does.
- The dead kill her in one sense, as prophesied of Hercules, because the memory of her father is the cause. The living kill her in another sense, because her unhappy love is the occasion.
