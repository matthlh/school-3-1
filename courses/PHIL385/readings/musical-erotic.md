## The last part (pages 100–117)

### Molière's *Don Juan* (end of section 2)

- Sganarelle's long opening speech corresponds to the Catalogue Aria, but its only effect is comic.
- Only music can let us hear the power of seduction while the list unrolls.
- A calls the statue in Molière a dramatic stumbling block. If Don Juan is force and passion, heaven itself must be set in motion. If he is not, such drastic means are ill-advised, and modern comedy would simply put him in a debtor's gaol.
- In Mozart's opera, the Commendatore's return is right, because the music makes him more than an individual. His voice becomes the voice of a spirit.
- No power on earth can coerce Don Giovanni. Only a ghost can, because he is sensual life in its **immediacy**, or before reflection, and the negation of immediacy is spirit.

> **Definition.** **Immediacy** means sensual life before reflection. Don Giovanni is immediacy, so no earthly power can coerce him; only a ghost can, because the negation of immediacy is spirit.

- Leporello's bond to his master makes sense. Through an obscure sympathy he is absorbed in a force, and that makes him a musical figure.
- Sganarelle relates to an individual. A sees no rational ground for him to stay when he is not even paid.

> **Watch out.** Keep Molière and Mozart apart. In Molière, Elvira is Don Juan's wife and he is an ordinary deceiver who promises marriage, a seducer historically rather than dramatically. In Mozart, the music makes him more than an individual.

- Molière's Elvira is Don Juan's wife. He is an ordinary deceiver who promises marriage.
- The two peasant girls are deceived offstage. The scene with Charlotte shows a lewd fellow looking over a girl like a horse dealer.
- We learn that Molière's Don Juan is a seducer historically, not dramatically.
- A says the remark that Molière's Don Juan is more moral than Mozart's is, properly understood, praise of the opera.
- Don Giovanni is a seducer and the music is seductive, yet the opera's aspiration is extremely moral, because everything in it is big and has genuine pathos.
- A calls the charge that the opera is immoral the foolishness of people caught up in details.

### Opera and drama (section 3)

- A thought gives drama its unity. Drama converts everything into action and situation and hurries toward the fall.
- A mood gives opera its unity. Opera lingers, expands in time and space, and moves horizontally.
- Opera is not reflective enough for character and action. What it expresses is unreflective, substantial passion.
- An action in the strict sense, meaning a deed done with a conscious purpose, cannot be set to music. Operatic action can only be immediate action.
- Because the action is immediate, irony runs through the opera, since A calls irony "the taskmaster of the immediate life".
- The Commendatore's return is "a monstrous irony". Don Giovanni can beat every obstacle, but he cannot kill a ghost.

### Don Giovanni and the other figures

- Don Giovanni is the opera's "common denominator". The other figures are not characters but passions he posits.
- A compares the opera to a solar system. The other figures are lit only on the side turned toward Don Giovanni.

| Figure | Passion or response |
|---|---|
| The Commendatore | Earnestness. |
| Elvira | Anger. |
| Anna | Hatred. |
| Ottavio | Self-importance. |
| Zerlina | Anxiety. |
| Masetto | Indignation. |
| Leporello | Confusion. |

- Everyone except the Commendatore has an erotic relation to Don Giovanni, and so is in his power. Elvira loves him, Anna hates him and Zerlina fears him. Ottavio and Masetto go along for the sake of brotherhood-in-law.
- The Commendatore is consciousness, so Don Giovanni has no power over him. He is kept in the background and appears only twice.
- The Commendatore falls to Don Giovanni's sword unseen at night. A says he is already spirit before he dies. He returns as spirit, when "he speaks no more, he judges".
- The Commendatore is the antecedent and the consequent. Don Giovanni's life is the middle premiss and the substance of the opera.
- Leporello is the next most important figure. Without music his bond to Don Giovanni is inexplicable.
- Leporello assimilates Don Giovanni until he is almost one of his master's working parts. We hear Don Giovanni in Leporello whenever Leporello is alone. When both are present, Leporello unconsciously parodies him.
- A listens with his eyes shut, and finally from the corridor outside the auditorium, because the opera "wants to be understood at a distance".

### Arias and the overture

- Elvira's first aria is A's example of a musical situation. Don Giovanni stays in the background, unseen even by the audience, and his jeering laughter fills the pause in her aria.
- Elvira's passion is "love's hatred", and we hear Don Giovanni inside her.
- If Elvira and Don Giovanni were both characters, their encounter would be the situation and this staging would be a mistake.
- A says two arias must go, Ottavio's and Anna's. They are concert pieces, not dramatic music, and the two figures are too slight to hold up the action.
- A says the overture should be composed last, so the composer is steeped in the music.
- A calls Mozart's overture a perfect masterpiece. He says it is enough on its own to prove *Don Giovanni* a classic.

> **Key.** A says the overture should be composed last, so the composer is steeped in the music. He calls Mozart's overture a perfect masterpiece, enough on its own to prove *Don Giovanni* a classic.

- The overture's focus is Don Giovanni, not Don Giovanni and the Commendatore. The deep voice of the opening grows weaker and is dragged into the race. The finale is where earnestness returns.
- The overture's first hint of Don Giovanni is like lightning on a dark horizon.
- Don Giovanni's life is born in dread. It is a substantial dread, not despair, and it is his energy and his demonic joy of life.
- He skims over the abyss like a stone skimming over water, until it stops and sinks.
- The opera rightly begins with Leporello, and his first aria belongs with the overture. In that aria Leporello reproduces Don Giovanni.
- Leporello's second aria, the list he sings to Elvira, is the most epic moment. Leporello is an epic narrator who forgets himself in Don Giovanni.
- The German version tells Elvira she is one of the 1,003. A calls this foolishly indecent.
- The champagne aria is the most lyrical moment. In it Don Giovanni dissolves into music, and A calls it "his life, foaming like champagne". The keynote of the opera sounds in it.
- The banquet, by contrast, is more a situation. In it Don Giovanni is driven to life's furthest point.
- The Platitudinous Postlude returns to A's favourite theme, that *Don Giovanni* stands highest among the classics, and to Mozart's good fortune.
