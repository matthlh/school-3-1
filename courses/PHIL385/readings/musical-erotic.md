## The last part, skimmed (pages 100–117)
Kraal said the rest can be skimmed. These are the claims in it that could be asked.

### Molière's *Don Juan* (end of section 2)
- Sganarelle's long opening speech corresponds to the Catalogue Aria but has no effect except comedy. Only music can let us hear the power of seduction while the list unrolls.
- The statue in Molière is a dramatic stumbling block. If Don Juan is force and passion, heaven itself must be set in motion; if he is not, such drastic means are ill-advised, and modern comedy would just put him in a debtor's gaol.
- In the opera the Commendatore's return is right, because the music makes him more than an individual: his voice becomes that of a spirit. No power on earth can coerce Don Giovanni; only a ghost can, because he is sensual life in its immediacy, and the negation of immediacy is spirit.
- Leporello's bond to his master makes sense: he is absorbed in a force, through an obscure sympathy, and that makes him a musical figure. Sganarelle relates to an individual, and there is no rational ground for his staying when he is not even paid.
- In Molière, Elvira is Don Juan's wife and he is an ordinary deceiver with promises of marriage; the two peasant girls are deceived off stage; the scene with Charlotte shows a lewd fellow looking over a girl like a horse dealer. We learn he is a seducer historically, not dramatically.
- The remark that Molière's Don Juan is more moral than Mozart's is, properly understood, praise of the opera. Don Giovanni is a seducer, the music is seductive, and the opera's aspiration is extremely moral because everything in it is big and has genuine pathos. Calling it immoral is the foolishness of people caught by details.

### Opera against drama (section 3)
- Drama's unity is a thought: everything is converted into action and situation, and it hurries toward the fall. Opera's unity is a mood: it lingers, expands in time and space, and moves horizontally.
- Opera is not reflective enough for character and action; what it expresses is unreflective, substantial passion. An action in the strict sense, a deed done with a conscious purpose, cannot be set to music; opera's action can only be immediate action.
- Because the action is immediate, irony runs through the piece, since irony is "the taskmaster of the immediate life". The return of the Commendatore is "a monstrous irony": Don Giovanni can beat every obstacle, but you cannot kill a ghost.

### Don Giovanni as the centre
- Don Giovanni is the opera's "common denominator". The other figures are not characters but passions he posits: the Commendatore's earnest, Elvira's anger, Anna's hate, Ottavio's self-importance, Zerlina's anxiety, Masetto's indignation, Leporello's confusion.
- A's image is a solar system: the other figures are lit only on the side turned toward him.
- Everyone but the Commendatore is in some erotic relation to him and so in his power: Elvira loves him, Anna hates him, Zerlina fears him, Ottavio and Masetto go along for the sake of brotherhood-in-law. The Commendatore is consciousness, so Don Giovanni has no power over him.
- The Commendatore is kept in the background and appears only twice: he falls to Don Giovanni's sword unseen at night, already spirit before he dies, and returns as spirit, when "he speaks no more, he judges". He is the antecedent and the consequent; Don Giovanni's life is the middle premiss and the substance of the opera.
- Leporello is the next most important figure. Without music his bond is inexplicable; as it is, he assimilates Don Giovanni until he is almost one of his working parts. Whenever Leporello is alone we hear Don Giovanni in him, and when both are present Leporello unconsciously parodies him.
- A listens with his eyes shut, and finally from the corridor outside the auditorium, because the opera "wants to be understood at a distance".

### Situations and pieces
- Elvira's first aria is A's example of a musical situation. Don Giovanni stays in the background, unseen even by the audience, and his jeering laughter fills the pause in her aria; her passion is "love's hatred", and we hear him inside her. If both were characters, the encounter would be the situation and the staging a mistake.
- Two arias must go, Ottavio's and Anna's: they are concert pieces, not dramatic music, and the figures are too slight to hold up the action.
- The overture should be composed last, so the composer is steeped in the music. Mozart's is a perfect masterpiece, enough on its own to prove the opera a classic. Its focus is Don Giovanni, not Don Giovanni and the Commendatore: the deep voice of the opening grows weaker and is dragged into the race, and the finale is where earnest returns.
- Don Giovanni's first hint in the overture is like lightning on a dark horizon. His life is born in dread, a substantial dread that is his energy and his demonic joy of life, and not despair. He skims over the abyss like a stone over water, until it stops and sinks.
- The opera rightly begins with Leporello, and his first aria belongs with the overture; Leporello reproduces Don Giovanni in it.
- The most epic moment is Leporello's second aria, the list, sung to Elvira. The German version tells her she is one of the 1,003, which A calls foolishly indecent. Leporello is an epic narrator who forgets himself in Don Giovanni.
- The most lyrical moment is the champagne aria. The banquet is more a situation, Don Giovanni driven to life's furthest point. In the champagne aria he dissolves into music; it is "his life, foaming like champagne", and the keynote of the opera sounds in it.
- The Platitudinous Postlude returns to A's favourite theme, that *Don Giovanni* stands highest among the classics, and to Mozart's good fortune.
