## The last part (pages 100–117)

### Molière's *Don Juan* (end of section 2)

- Sganarelle's long opening speech corresponds to the Catalogue Aria, but its only effect is comic. In the aria, the music lets us hear Don Giovanni's power of seduction as Leporello lists his conquests.
- A thinks the statue poses a problem for Molière's play. If Don Juan embodies force and passion, it takes heaven to stop him. If he is an ordinary deceiver, modern comedy would simply put him in a debtor's gaol.
- In Mozart's opera, the Commendatore's return fits. Music gives him the voice of a spirit instead of an individual's voice.
- Only a ghost can stop Don Giovanni. He embodies sensual life in its **immediacy**, or before reflection, and spirit is the negation of immediacy.
- Leporello is a musical figure because his obscure sympathy with Don Giovanni absorbs him in his master's power.
- Sganarelle serves an individual. A sees no rational reason for him to stay without pay.
- Molière's Elvira is Don Juan's wife. He is an ordinary deceiver who promises marriage.
- The two peasant girls are deceived offstage. In the scene with Charlotte, Don Juan appraises her like a horse dealer.
- Molière tells us Don Juan is a seducer through the history of his behaviour. The drama does not show his power.
- A takes the claim that Molière's Don Juan is more moral as praise for Mozart's opera. Don Giovanni seduces, and so does the music, but the opera has a moral aspiration because of its scale and genuine pathos. A thinks the charge of immorality focuses on individual details.

### Opera and drama (section 3)

- A thought gives drama its unity. Drama turns everything into action and situation and moves quickly toward the fall.
- A mood gives opera its unity. Opera takes time to develop that mood and extends it across time and space.
- Opera expresses unreflective, substantial passion. An action in the strict sense is a deed with a conscious purpose, so it cannot be set to music. Operatic action is immediate.
- Irony runs through the opera because A calls it "the taskmaster of the immediate life".
- The Commendatore's return is "a monstrous irony". Don Giovanni can overcome any ordinary obstacle, but he cannot kill a ghost.

### Don Giovanni and the other figures

- Don Giovanni is the opera's "common denominator". The other figures show the passions he provokes instead of functioning as independent characters.
- A compares the opera to a solar system. The other figures are lit only on the side that faces Don Giovanni.

| Figure | Passion or response |
|---|---|
| The Commendatore | Earnestness. |
| Elvira | Anger. |
| Anna | Hatred. |
| Ottavio | Self-importance. |
| Zerlina | Anxiety. |
| Masetto | Indignation. |
| Leporello | Confusion. |

- Everyone except the Commendatore has an erotic relation to Don Giovanni and is in his power. Elvira loves him, Anna hates him and Zerlina fears him. Ottavio and Masetto go along out of loyalty to a brother-in-law.
- The Commendatore represents consciousness, which Don Giovanni cannot control. He stays in the background and appears only twice.
- The Commendatore falls to Don Giovanni's sword unseen at night. A says he is already spirit before he dies. He returns as spirit, when "he speaks no more, he judges".
- The Commendatore supplies the beginning and end, the antecedent and consequent. Don Giovanni's life is the middle premiss and substance of the opera.
- Leporello is the next most important figure. The music makes his attachment intelligible. Leporello takes on Don Giovanni's qualities until he almost functions as part of him. We hear Don Giovanni when Leporello is alone. In scenes with his master, Leporello unconsciously parodies him.
- A listens with his eyes closed and eventually moves to the corridor because the opera "wants to be understood at a distance".

### Arias and the overture

- Elvira's first aria shows how a musical situation works. Don Giovanni stays unseen in the background, even from the audience, but his jeering laughter fills her pause. Her passion is "love's hatred", and the music lets us hear him in her response.
- If Elvira and Don Giovanni were independent dramatic characters, their encounter would be the situation and keeping him out of sight would be a mistake.
- A would cut two arias, Ottavio's and Anna's. He considers them concert pieces that give too much time to minor figures and stall the action.
- A says the composer should write the overture last, once thoroughly familiar with the music. He considers Mozart's overture enough on its own to establish *Don Giovanni* as a classic.
- The overture focuses on Don Giovanni. The deep voice at the opening gradually weakens as it is drawn into the music. Earnestness returns in the finale.
- The overture first hints at Don Giovanni like lightning on a dark horizon. A calls the underlying feeling dread and distinguishes it from despair. Dread gives Don Giovanni energy and demonic joy in life. He compares Don Giovanni's course to a stone skipping over water until it sinks.
- The opera properly opens with Leporello. His first aria belongs with the overture because he reproduces Don Giovanni in it.
- Leporello's second aria, the list he sings to Elvira, is the most epic moment. He narrates and forgets himself in Don Giovanni's story.
- The German version tells Elvira she is one of the 1,003. A calls this foolishly indecent.
- The champagne aria is the most lyrical moment. Don Giovanni becomes music in what A calls "his life, foaming like champagne". The opera's central mood is audible in it.
- The banquet is more a situation than an aria. It takes Don Giovanni to the furthest point of his life.
- The Platitudinous Postlude returns to Mozart's good fortune and to A's claim that *Don Giovanni* ranks highest among the classics.
