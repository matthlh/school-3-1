## The opening, and why choice cannot wait

### Either/or, and A's version of it (about pp. 475–478, PDF pp. 415–419)

- B opens by shouting what he has said so often: either/or, *aut/aut*. A single *aut* offered as a caution does not make matters clear.
- There is only one situation where the phrase has its absolute meaning. On one side stand truth, righteousness and holiness, on the other desire, dim passions and perdition.
- B says the words work on him "like an incantation". He becomes serious, sometimes shaken.
- To A the phrase means nothing at all. B lists A's own descriptions of it: a twinkling of the eye, a snap of the fingers, a *coup de main*, an abracadabra. Its effect on A is "that of a strong drink on a neurasthenic", and A calls the result "the higher lunacy".
- A's favourite "great thinker" told a man who had tossed his hat on the floor that he would get a beating if he picked it up and a beating if he did not, "now you can choose!".
- A "comforts" people in crisis with the line "do it or don't do it, you will regret both". B calls this "a deep mockery of yourself" and proof of "how loose the joints of your soul are".
- A says life is a masquerade and no one has succeeded in knowing him. B says A is nothing and exists only in relation to others, and that A's own mask is the most enigmatic of all.
- A wants either/or written as one word, an interjection shouted at mankind, not a disjunction.
- B warns that a midnight hour comes when everyone has to take off his mask. A could end by dissolving into a multitude, becoming "a legion", and losing the unifying power of personality.
- The line to keep: "he who cannot reveal himself cannot love, and he who cannot love is the unhappiest of all". The letter ends on the same point, the duty to become revealed.
- B's thought experiment: a gifted young man who mistook A for a serious person asks A for guidance. A would not say "I say only either/or". Out of love for the young man A would acknowledge the importance of either/or and urge him to use his time well. So A's nature contradicts itself, and only an either/or gets him out of it.
- The main thing in life, B says, is "to win yourself, to take possession of yourself".

### Why the choice cannot wait (about pp. 478–480, PDF pp. 419–423)

- Choice is decisive for a personality's content. In choosing, the personality immerses itself in what is chosen; when it does not choose, it "wastes consumptively away".
- The instant of deliberation, like the Platonic instant, has no existence. The longer one stares at it, the less it exists.
- The helmsman about to tack can say he could do this or that, but the ship keeps its headway, so there is only an instant when it is immaterial which he does. A person who forgets his own headway reaches a moment when there is no either/or left, not because he chose but because he refrained, which means others chose for him and "he has lost himself".
- The personality already has an interest in the choice before one chooses. A postponed choice is made unconsciously, or by "the dark powers" within.
- In the fairy-tale, people captivated by mermaids' music had to play the same piece backwards without a single mistake to break the spell. B uses it for having to retract what was lived while one deliberated.
- A's method: faced with either priest or actor, A's "reflection with its hundred arms" studies the priesthood for half a year, then art, then a third option appears (lawyer, or barrister), and after a year and a half he has not moved a step. He ends with "Or a hairdresser, or a bank teller, I say only either/or."
- A has become what he despises most, "a critic, a universal critic in all departments". His "vanitas vanitatum vanitas, hurrah!" is no choice. B calls it a case of "Drop it!", or a mediation "like letting five become an even number".

## The choice, and what philosophy cannot do

### The aesthetic choice is no choice (about pp. 480–482, PDF pp. 423–424)

- "Your choice is an aesthetic choice, but an aesthetic choice is no choice."
- The act of choosing is "a literal and strict expression of the ethical". Wherever there is an either/or in the stricter sense, the ethical is involved.
- The only absolute either/or is the choice between good and evil, and that one is absolutely ethical.
- The aesthetic choice is either wholly immediate, and so no choice, or it loses itself in multiplicity. A young girl who follows her heart chooses immediately. A, surveying life-tasks, arrives at a multiplicity.
- The ethical choice is easier in one sense, since the selection is insignificant, and infinitely harder in another, since the act itself means far more.
- In choice "it is less a matter of choosing correctly than of the energy, earnest and feeling with which one chooses". Even someone who chose wrongly would, through that energy, discover it. The merely aesthetic chooser's rhythm is only a *spiritus lenis*.
- B's answer to his own question: "What is it, then, that I separate in my either/or? Is it good and evil? No, I simply want to bring you to the point where that choice truly acquires meaning for you." If the moment of choice comes while A is reading, he should throw the rest of the letter away.
- The alternative is to live aesthetically or to live ethically. Someone who lives aesthetically does not choose. Someone who chooses the aesthetic once the ethical has appeared to him sins, and so comes under the ethical, even though his life is unethical.
- "My either/or does not denote in the first instance the choice between good and evil, it denotes the choice whereby one chooses good and evil or excludes them." The question is under what categories one wants to see the world and live.
- "the aesthetic is not evil but indifference". So it is the ethical that constitutes choice: less a choice between willing good or evil than a choice to will, with which good and evil are posited.
- Choosing the ethical posits the good, but only abstractly; the chooser can still choose evil afterwards. What is crucial is "the baptism of choice".

> **Definition.** For B the **aesthetic choice** is either wholly immediate (no choice) or lost in multiplicity. The **ethical choice** is the choice to will at all, by which good and evil first appear as options. The **absolute either/or** is the choice between good and evil, and it only arises after the first choice.

### Philosophy, mediation and the two spheres (about pp. 482–485, PDF pp. 424–427)

- A's "victory over life" resembles recent philosophy's pet theory that the principle of contradiction is annulled. B says philosophy makes the same error as A.
- A's domain is action, philosophy's is contemplation. A mediates oppositions "in a higher lunacy", philosophy "in a higher unity".
- A turns to the future and says whichever he does is equally wrong, "*ergo* I do nothing at all". Philosophy turns to the past: "For the philosopher world history is concluded, and he mediates."
- B mocks young men who can mediate Christianity and paganism but cannot tell a plain man what to do in life.
- "As truly as there is a future, just as truly is there an either/or."
- The philosopher's time is not absolute time. Our age will look like a discursive moment to a later philosopher, who will mediate it in turn. So absolute mediation is possible only when history is finished, and "the System is in constant process of becoming".
- The difficulty: if mediation is admitted there is no absolute choice. B blames a confusion of two spheres, thought and freedom. "For thought the opposition has no substance, it goes over into the other"; "For freedom the opposition does have substance, because the one opposite now excludes the other."
- B does not mean *liberum arbitrium*, bare freedom to pick either way. True positive freedom has evil outside itself for all eternity, as an impotent possibility, and perfects itself by excluding it. Exclusion is the opposite of mediation. He also does not assume a notion of radical evil.
- The spheres proper to thought are "logic, nature and history". Necessity rules there, so mediation is valid there.
- History is the hard case, since freedom is said to rule in it. B's answer: the individual acts, but the action enters an order of things that "digests the free actions" into its eternal laws, and that higher order is necessity. So the philosopher may use mediation on history, but only "relative mediation".
- B separates the outward works, through which a man belongs to history, from his "inward works". Philosophy has nothing to do with inward work, and inward work "is the true life of freedom".
- Philosophy is therefore helpless at getting a man to act. What it really demands is that one act from necessity, "and that is a contradiction".
- Even the humblest individual has a dual existence: a history that is not only his free actions, and an inward work that is his for eternity. "In this world there rules an absolute either/or, but it is a world philosophy has nothing to do with."
- An old man looking back can mediate his history for thought, but deep inside an either/or still separates what his choice separated. If anything is a mediation there it is repentance, and "repentance is no mediation": it consumes the thing in its wrath, which is like exclusion.
- "So it is freedom I am fighting for … for the future, for either/or." The treasure B would leave his little son is "an either/or which makes a man greater than the angels", deposited in the son's own inner being, so he owes no one for it.

### Choosing oneself, and the two definitions (about pp. 485–487, PDF pp. 427–428)

- What stands out in B's either/or is the ethical. It is not yet a matter of the reality of the thing chosen but of "the reality of choosing". One person can help another to this point, and no further.
- As having loved gives a man's nature a harmony that is never lost (the first letter), choosing gives it "a solemnity, a quiet dignity" that is never lost.
- The instant of choice: all is still, "solemn as a starlit night", the soul is alone, and "the eternal power itself" appears before it. "the I chooses itself – or, more correctly, it accepts itself". The personality receives "the accolade of knighthood".
- He does not become someone else, "he becomes himself; consciousness unites". The richest personality is nothing before it has chosen itself, like an heir before coming of age. "the great thing is not to be this or that, but to be oneself; and every person can be that if he wants."
- It is not a choice of something, since what stands on the other side is the aesthetic, which is indifference. Yet it is an absolute choice, since only by choosing absolutely can one choose the ethical.
- The aesthetic is excluded as the absolute, but "relatively it always stays behind". Since the person chooses himself and stays himself, "the whole of the aesthetic returns in its relativity".
- The either/or is absolute in one sense, because its options are choosing and not choosing. In another sense the absolute either/or first appears with the choice, when good and evil become options.
- B is "no ethical rigorist". Once the choice is posited the whole of the aesthetic returns, and only then is life beautiful. The aim is to use the world and not abuse it.
- The two definitions: "The aesthetic factor in a person is that by which he is immediately what he is; the ethical factor is that by which he becomes what he becomes."

## Every aesthetic life is despair

### The stages of the aesthetic life-view (about pp. 487–497, PDF pp. 428–436)

- B says A cannot explain living aesthetically in a higher sense, because A is caught up in it and lives in the moment. Only someone a step higher, living ethically, can explain it. Within the ethical B is "raised above the instant, I am in freedom", and no one can be caught up in being free.
- Every person forms a view of life. The aesthetic view, in every age and at every stage, says "one must enjoy life".
- The careful phrasing B asks A to hold on to: the person who wants to enjoy life "always posits a condition which is either outside the individual or in the individual but not posited by the individual himself".
- All the stages share one thing: spirit appears not as spirit but as immediacy. Even at the most refined stage spirit has the form of talent.
- First stage, the condition in the individual but not posited by him: health as the most precious good. Its more poetic form is beauty as the highest thing. Beauty is fragile and the view is rarely lived out.
- B's example is the old count and countess he visited as a student. Watching her husband walk up the avenue in his court uniform, she said, "Little Vilhelm, isn't my Ditlev the handsomest man in the whole kingdom?" and that together they were the handsomest couple in the land. "Little Vilhelm" is B himself.
- Second stage, the condition outside the individual: wealth, glory, nobility and the like. B adds a certain infatuation here, the girl head over heels in love for whom nothing has meaning except her beloved. He says he will explain later why he cannot approve of it.
- Third stage, the condition in the individual but not posited by him: talent, practical, mercantile, mathematical, poetic, artistic or philosophical. These are the tireless people A scorns. B says their lives are far more innocent than A's, and the essential thing is the same: wanting to enjoy life.
- These views all rest on one definite thing, so they cohere. The next view does not: "Enjoy life" read as "Live for your desire". Desire is inherently diverse, so such a life splits into endless diversity unless it has settled since childhood into one bent, such as fishing, hunting or horses. It lies in reflection, but a finite reflection, and the personality stays immediate.
- Nero is B's example of the life of desire with everything in its favour. A once said one could not blame Nero for setting fire to Rome to get an idea of the conflagration of Troy, only ask whether he had enough of the artist in him to enjoy it.
- Nero's nature, in B's one word, was melancholy. B subscribes to the earlier Church doctrine that counted melancholy among the cardinal sins. Sorrow can pursue a person all his life blamelessly, but "a person becomes melancholy only by his own fault".
- B's Nero: the immediacy of spirit cannot break through yet demands a breakthrough, and he lacks the courage, so he grabs at new pleasures and yawns in boredom when they end. He does not have himself. He wants to make people anxious, since to himself he is a riddle and anxiety his being. He slinks in weak and impotent, which is more disquieting than imperial dignity. Caligula's wish for all men's heads on one neck and a man's suicide are the same expression.

> **Definition.** **Melancholy** is "hysteria of the spirit". It sets in when immediacy has ripened and spirit demands a higher form in which it will apprehend itself as spirit, but the movement halts and is pressed back. The melancholic cannot say what weighs on him ("I don't know what it is"), unlike the griever, and that is melancholy's infinitude. It is sin, "the sin of not willing deeply and sincerely", and a mother to all sins. Only spirit can cure it. Do not confuse it with the first letter's egoistic and sympathetic melancholy, which were fear of committing.

- Melancholy is common in the age, and "the whole of German and French youth groan" under it. Only the most gifted natures are afflicted. For the person who owns himself eternally it is "neither too early nor too late that he comes to the world".
- After desire comes a higher reflection: "Enjoy yourself; it is you yourself in the enjoyment that you must enjoy." This Epicureanism still depends on an external condition, since one enjoys oneself only in the enjoyment.
- A callousness of reason then says "Enjoy yourself in constantly discarding the conditions." B replies that the person who enjoys discarding the conditions depends on them just as much, and hollows himself out, since the enjoyment is given as little content as possible.
- Every stage has this in common: "one lives for that in which one is immediately what one is", and reflection never reaches beyond it.

### Every aesthetic life-view is despair, and A's own (about pp. 497–507, PDF pp. 436–443)

- B's argument. Suppose every aesthetic life succeeds: the man of health is "as quick as ever when he died", the count and countess dance at their golden wedding, the gold mines never run out, the girl gets the one she loved, the mercantile talent joins all five continents, Nero never yawns, the Epicurean and the Cynic always have something to enjoy or discard. Then suppose the opposite, that none of it happens. They despair.
- Why did they despair? Because what they built on proved transitory. But nothing essential changed: the transitory was transitory before. So "If they do despair, then, that must be because they were in despair beforehand." The difference is only that they did not know it.
- Conclusion: "every aesthetic view of life is despair, and everyone who lives aesthetically is in despair whether he knows it or not." If one does know it, as A does, "a higher form of existence is an inescapable requirement".
- One stage remains, the most refined, and now it is A's turn. This last aesthetic life-view "is despair itself": the personality stays in its immediacy, but it has admitted to itself the nothingness of such a life-view.
- There is despair and despair. A painter who goes blind despairs over something particular, and his despair ends if his sight returns. A has lost nothing: he has wealth, independence, health, a vigorous mind, and no girl has refused him. "Yours is no current despair but a despair in thought." B calls it thought-despair.
- A's thought has hurried ahead and seen through the vanity of everything, but gone no further. He is "constantly beyond yourself, that is to say, in despair". His life swings between two antitheses, intemperately energetic and immoderately indolent.
- A's life lacks continuity, so he hates activity. He stands idle in the market-place like the workers in the Gospel. He says he would not get out of the way of a tile falling from a roof, dies daily, and reckons his lifetime "from one day's notice to quit to the next".
- When something grips him A is "in touch" and works with a dying man's supernatural energy for a day or a month, six months at most, then breaks off: "and that's the end of the story". He flatters himself he could have gone on, which B calls an immense deception.
- A hovers above himself in "the nothing of despair" and uses learning and moods to decorate "the palace of mental profusion" he occasionally lives in.
- Musaeus's legend of Roland's three pages: one page got a thimble of invisibility, courted the princess Urraca unseen, and when she made him reveal himself a common everyday face appeared and the fascination vanished. A is shrewder: he sees it cannot pay to reveal oneself, passes off an ideal picture of himself and withdraws, with the pleasure of having fooled someone.
- A has no view of life, only something resembling one. His composure is only in contrast to the pleasure-chaser. He is "content with absolute discontent".
- Offered the world's glories, he would give them a day; being a millionaire, a month; the most beautiful girl's love, half a year; honour and admiration, his weakest point, a short while. He would rather be the most foolish of men worshipped as the wisest, as the deeper mockery. The one thing he might wish for is a divining-rod, and he would use it to scrape out his pipe.
- Yet A cannot hold out on this extremity. The next instant some triviality captivates him. Towards people his nature shows a high degree of faithlessness, for which B cannot blame him morally, since A is outside the category of the ethical.
- B would not even take a drive in the woods with A, because A's participation is always a falsehood. He forgives him, since A says himself that he is "like a woman in confinement".
- The spirit takes its revenge and fetters A in melancholy. "this is the way to become a Nero", B says, were it not for the original seriousness, depth and magnanimity in A, and were A Emperor of Rome.
- A's next refuge is the life-view of sadness and sorrow. His thought is too healthy for it: aesthetic sorrow passes as much as joy, and the thought that sorrow passes is as comfortless as the thought that joy passes. So A chooses instead "a joy which is sorrow's changeling", "this laughter of despair", and triumphs over the people he makes laugh: "If only you knew what you are laughing at!"
- Pleasure does not tempt A, which B calls providential. It shows A must go forward, not back. Pride is "but a fragile security".

### "Despair!" (about pp. 507–512, PDF pp. 443–447)

- "this life is despair", whether A hides it from others or not. In another sense it is not despair: A is "too frivolous to despair" and "too melancholy not to come in contact with despair".
- A is like a woman in labour who keeps putting off the moment. What a person gives birth to spiritually is a creative urge of the will, which is in his own power. "you will give birth only to yourself."
- To be conscious of oneself in one's eternal validity is a moment more significant than everything in the world. It feels like being trapped for ever, binding oneself for an eternity to an eternal power. "And yet, one can still let it be!"
- The either/or B puts to A: go to Paris, take up journalism, court voluptuous women, and when wit falls silent "there is water enough in the Seine and gunpowder in the general store". Or, since A neither can nor will do that, pull himself together, respect every honourable effort, and "show a little more respect for woman".
- B's claim about women: of a hundred men who go astray, ninety-nine are saved by women and one by direct divine grace.
- Others might say "Get married" or "Apply for some office" to give A something else to think about. B rejects both, since marrying or taking a post as a distraction is a kind of falsehood, and the melancholy would erupt again.
- "I have only one answer: 'Despair!'" B shouts it as a married man, not as a mocking demon. It is "not as a comfort, not as a state in which you are to remain, but as an action requiring all the soul's strength". Anyone who has not tasted the bitterness of despair "has missed the meaning of life". B has despaired too.
- A is fortunate, because he will not despair over something particular. The despairer who blames the complex world outside him despairs falsely and comes to hate the world. Once one finds oneself in despair one loves the world for being what it is.
- B's image for delay is the woman who offered Tarquin a collection of books, burnt a third and demanded the same sum, then burnt another third and demanded the same sum, until he paid the original price for the last third.
- An even better condition for despair: a gifted young man who loves a girl as much as himself feels the difference between them (the intellectual authority is his) and despairs for her sake until he finds himself in his eternal validity, and so finds her too. "he who despairs finds the eternally human being and in that we are all equal."
- The poet-existence, which A calls a human sacrifice, lies in the obscurity of despair not carried through. "The poetic ideal is always an untrue ideal, for the true ideal is always what is actual." The poet is higher than the finite yet not the infinite, and must flee the world to enjoy his ideals.
- "if you do not want to be a poet there is no other way for you than the one I have shown you: Despair!"

## Choosing oneself

### Doubt and despair, the self, and repentance (about pp. 512–520, PDF pp. 447–453)

- "choose despair, since despair is itself a choice, for one can doubt without choosing to, but despair one cannot without choosing to do so."
- When one despairs one chooses again, and what one chooses is oneself, "not in one's immediacy, not as this contingent individual", but "in one's eternal validity".
- B says recent philosophy talks of speculation beginning with doubt but never says how doubt differs from despair. His formula: "Doubt is a despair of thought, despair is a doubt of the personality."
- Doubt belongs to necessity. When thought has doubted and finds the absolute, it rests there by the same necessity with which it doubted. So doubt moves only thought, not the whole personality. A doubter can still be a conscientious person with no doubts about duty.
- Certain German philosophers have conquered doubt yet have despair in their hearts and distract themselves with objective thinking. "scarcely any sedative is more effective than abstract thinking."
- Despair is deeper and more complete than doubt. "despair is an expression of the whole personality, doubt only of thought." "Doubt, therefore, lies in difference, despair in the absolute." It takes talent to doubt and none to despair; the lowliest person, a young girl, can despair.
- One cannot despair without wanting to, and when one truly wills despair one is beyond it, since one has chosen what despair chooses, oneself in one's eternal validity. B predicts the age will learn that "the true point of departure for finding the absolute is not doubt but despair".
- Choice is B's one category, "the choice of both my heart and my mind".
- In choosing absolutely I choose despair, and in despair I choose the absolute, "for I myself am the absolute". The same thing said the other way: "I choose the absolute which chooses me, I posit the absolute which posits me." The category of choice is the identity of both. What is chosen *is*, or I could not choose it, and *is not*, since it only comes to be by my choosing it.
- What do I choose? Not this or that thing but the absolute, "myself in my eternal validity". Anything else I would choose as something finite. "Even the Jew who chose God did not choose absolutely", since he chose the absolute but not absolutely, and so it became something finite.
- What is this self? At a first definition "it is the most abstract thing of all which yet, at the same time, is the most concrete thing of all – it is freedom".
- B's psychological observation: people wish for another man's mind or talents, but no one seriously wishes to become someone else. Even the unfortunate cling to themselves, and even a suicide only wishes another form of his self. B calls this a highly plausible proof of the eternal validity of personal existence.
- The person who has not chosen himself treats his inmost being as "an algebraic entity" that could stand for anything. The person who chooses himself chooses himself absolutely, and the self he chooses is "infinitely concrete" and yet absolutely different from his former self: it did not exist before the choice and yet it has always been he himself.
- The choice makes two dialectical movements at once. "I do not create myself, I choose myself." As free spirit he is "born of the principle of contradiction".
- The chosen self has a history that links him to other individuals and to the whole race, and there is something painful in it. It takes courage to choose oneself, because just when he seems most isolated he enters most deeply into the roots that link him with the whole.
- The expression of this fight is repentance. "He repents himself back into himself, back into the family, back into the race, until he finds himself in God."
- There is a love with which one loves God, and "there is only one expression for this in language: repentance". Thought's love of the absolute is not free love, since it loves necessarily. One reason repentance is the expression of love of God: "he loved me first".
- "it is only if I choose myself as guilty that I choose myself absolutely". If the sins of the father are inherited by the son, he repents them too, since only so can he choose himself.
- B learnt this in the living-room, watching his little son and thinking that one day the boy will choose himself and repent whatever guilt of B's rests on him. "the highest he can only do for himself."
- "here absolute isolation is identical with the deepest continuity".
- Repentance first found its true expression in Christianity. The pious Jew felt the sins of the fathers weigh on him but could not repent them, because he could not choose himself absolutely. "The greater the freedom the greater the guilt, and that is the secret of blessedness." Not wanting to repent the sins of the fathers is faintheartedness.
- The either/or between living aesthetically and ethically is "not a perfect dilemma, because there is really only one option". Through choosing the good I choose the option between good and evil. "The original choice is constantly present in every subsequent choice."
- B could break off here, but that might look like quietism, like winning a sword that conquers the world and thrusting it into the scabbard. The ethical life-view that follows is "a frugal offering", since frugality marks everything ethical: *nil ad ostentationem, omnia ad conscientiam* (nothing for show, everything for conscience).

### Suffering harm to one's soul (about pp. 520–523, PDF pp. 453–455)

- The reef one can run aground on in the moment of despair. The Scripture's "What is a man profited, if he shall gain the whole world and suffer harm to his own soul?" is an ethical expression, and only someone who has despaired can interpret it.
- The "whole world" includes every finitude I have through my immediacy. I can lose wealth, esteem and mental powers without harming my soul, and gain them all and suffer harm. The soul is the same abstract self found earlier, the self that cannot wish to be someone else.
- Willing one's despair infinitely is "identical with absolute self-surrender". Willing it finitely harms the soul: "finite despair is a hardening, absolute despair an infinitization".
- Every finite despair is a choice of the finite, whether I despair over gaining the world or over losing it, so it is an unfree despair.
- A person can stand at this dangerous point without yet having suffered harm. He suffers harm only when he hardens himself in finite despair and, on coming round, chooses a finite way out. Then he isolates himself and becomes "a beast of prey that shuns no means".
- No one can judge another here, since the harm lies in the inmost being, "like the rot in the core of the fruit" under an appetizing outside.
- The chosen self is not an abstraction. It is "the whole aesthetic self which is chosen ethically", a rich concretion of determinate qualities. Freedom "is jealous of itself" and will not leave undecided what belongs to it. In the first instant of choice the person is as naked as a newborn child; the next instant he is concrete. He becomes himself "down to the least significant peculiarity" and yet another, since the choice transforms everything.

### Good and evil are not for thought (p. 524, PDF pp. 456–457)

- Once he owns himself as chosen, "an absolute difference appears, that between good and evil". Before the choice it remains latent.
- Can the difference be thought? No. Whenever I think, my relation to what I think is one of necessity, and that is exactly why the difference between good and evil does not arise. "You always think relative differences, never the absolute difference."
- Philosophy may be right that it cannot think an absolute contradiction, but it does not follow that none exists. It seems to have abolished the principle of contradiction only because it has not yet reached it.
- In thinking I make myself infinite but not absolutely, since I disappear into the absolute. Only in choosing myself absolutely do I make myself infinite absolutely, and only then have I posited the absolute difference.
- Philosophy says "The absolute *is* by virtue of my thinking it", then corrects itself to "the absolute's self-thinking in me", because my thought of the absolute is necessary. "Things are different with the good. The good *is* by virtue of my willing it, and otherwise it has no existence." The same holds for evil.
- This does not make good and evil subjective. "On the contrary, it is to assert the absolute validity of these categories." "The good is the in-and-for-itself posited by the in-and-for-itself, and that is freedom."
- To avoid the misreading that he chose good and evil equally absolutely, B used the phrase "repenting myself out of the whole of existence". Repentance expresses that evil is an essential part of me and at the same time not essentially a part of me. If evil were not part of me I could not choose it; if there were something in me I could not choose, I would not be the absolute myself but only a product.
- The [lecture 22 page](../lectures/22-equilibrium-hegel-and-the-choice-of-good-and-evil.md) has Kraal's handling of this passage and your questions on it.

## The ethical life-view

### How it begins: being, mood, and the false path of Narcissus (about pp. 525–530, PDF pp. 457–461)

- Every aesthetic life-view was despair because it was built on "what may or may not be". The ethical life-view builds life on "what has being as its essential property".
- The aesthete does develop, but "with necessity, not with freedom". There is no metamorphosis. His soul is like soil where every herb has an equal right to thrive, and when he chooses what to cultivate, a more-or-less decides, a relative difference. Someone untouched by the ethical could say "I have it in me to be a Don Juan, a Faust, a robber chief" and cultivate that trait with aesthetic seriousness.
- Aesthetic development is "a development like that of the plant": the individual becomes something, but what it becomes is what it already is immediately.
- For the ethical person there is from the first an absolute difference, good and evil. If he finds more evil than good in himself, evil is to be suppressed and the good brought forward. Even when he gives rein to the aesthetic, it "has nevertheless been removed from its throne".
- It takes ethical courage "not to be distinguished by difference but to be content with the universal". The aesthete secretly dreads despair, because despair brings out the universal and his life rests on a difference he will not give up.
- Aesthetes call despair "a break". That fits only if life is a necessary unfolding of the immediate. Otherwise "despair is no break but a transfiguration". Only the person who despairs over something particular suffers a break, because he does not despair fully.
- In despair nothing is destroyed. All a person's aesthetic qualities remain, but "they become ancillary and are preserved for that very reason". The ethicist only carries to its conclusion the doubt the higher aesthete started and broke off.
- Mood is the aesthetic expression of pleasure in relation to personal existence. The aesthete tries to be wholly taken up in the mood, so he is in the moment and swings violently. The ethical person also has moods but sees them beneath him, since he has chosen himself infinitely; the remainder that will not fit a mood is his continuity. He "has a memory of his life", and the aesthete has not.
- The art of mastering desire is not giving it up but "determining the instant". The gambler whose desire is roused is cured if he can say "Not just now; in an hour's time", because "This hour is the continuity that saves him".
- The aesthete's mood is eccentric, since "he has his centre at the periphery". The ethical person has his mood centralized and works for continuity, which is always master over mood. His total mood is acquired equability, which no one has by nature.
- The false path. Someone who has chosen himself and then says "I am the one I am" has chosen from outside, "in respect of his necessity, not of his freedom", and "had taken the ethical option in vain, aesthetically". His eternal validity overwhelms him, the temporal vanishes, and he feels he has "reached eternity before his time". He gazes at himself, apathy sets in, and suicide can seem the only way out. "like Narcissus, he has fallen in love with himself."
- The right choice puts him in motion. He has chosen himself in respect of his possibility, and "he can only stay in his freedom by constantly realizing it".

### Sorrow as the meaning of life (about pp. 530–535, PDF pp. 461–465)

- A's most agreeable life-view, as teacher and sometimes practitioner: the real meaning of life is sorrow, and "being the unhappiest is the happiest thing of all". Its slogan is not pleasure, so it does not look aesthetic, but it is not ethical either. It lies "at the dangerous transition between the aesthetic and the ethical", where the soul is seduced by some theory of predestination.
- A uses it to draw young women: enigmatic hints that everything is explained by a remote melancholy, opened only to her and never explained. A girl once said A would end up a Jesuit.
- The age prefers sorrow to joy and calls that the higher view. Joy obliges one to be thankful; sorrow absolves one. Having found life vain, the age believes in sorrow because it must believe in something.
- A's sorrow is aesthetic, reflected sorrow, based on misfortune, fate, disposition or others, never on guilt. It is not repentance.
- Why it offers no comfort: someone who makes sorrow the meaning of life has joy outside him, as the joy-seeker has sorrow outside him, so his life-view depends on a condition he does not control. "any life-view with a condition outside it is despair." Wanting to sorrow is despair in exactly the same sense as wanting to seek joy.
- When A speaks of sorrow for a whole life he thinks of unhappy characters and tragic heroes, over whom fate broods. That is plain fatalism, and A's claim to be the unhappiest man is "the proudest and most defiant" thought a man can have.
- B's answer "as you deserve": "First of all, you do not sorrow." A's saying that the unhappiest is the happiest is a falsification that turns against the eternal power, "a rebellion against God", and treason against the human race, since it says the great men simply escaped the hardest trials.
- B does not despise sorrow. To sorrow is beautiful and "there is mettle in tears", but one shall not sorrow as one who has no hope. A and B are in "an absolute opposition" that cannot be abolished, because B cannot live in aesthetic categories.
- If B's own life were full of blameless sorrows, he would still divest himself of the hero's apparel and tragedy's pathos. "I have only one expression for what I suffer – guilt; one expression for my pain – repentance; one hope before my eyes – forgiveness". The one sorrow that could bring him to despair would be that repentance was a delusion in the accountability it presupposes.
- B deposits his sorrow within his being and does not take it out every day. He masters the aesthetic in sorrow ethically: when sorrow turns vehement and sophistical he brooks no rebellion. He does not forget it; he repents.
- The Christian view helps where the philosophers do not, because "It assigns everything to sin, something philosophy is too aesthetic to have the ethical courage to do".

### Two failed ethical lives: the Greek paragon and the mystic (about pp. 535–544, PDF pp. 465–472)

- The first form the choice takes is "a complete isolation". The individual is active, but his action has no relation to the surrounding world.
- The Greek version: an individual develops himself into "a paragon of virtue". Later, the Christian anchorites did the same. They remained among life's diversity only because contact with it was pedagogically necessary; civic life meant nothing to them. The virtues were personal (courage, perseverance, temperance, moderation), not civic.
- Its defect: "the individual has chosen himself altogether abstractly", so the perfection he attained was abstract, and his blessedness is a lonely, transient self-contentment. That is why B insists that choosing oneself and repenting oneself are identical, since repentance connects the individual with the surrounding world.
- The mystic's life is far deeper. He has chosen himself absolutely, although he says he has chosen God, which comes to the same. He calls God "the absolute Thou". Christian piety lies in freedom, so he must have chosen himself absolutely to be in a free relation to God.
- The mystic chooses himself in complete isolation. The whole world is dead for him and "the weary soul chooses God, or itself". His weariness is not boredom with life but his repentance at not having chosen God earlier. That shows how little his life is given to the ethical: he repents that he did not choose God while he was still abstract, as a child.
- The mystic is active, but his action is inner action. His life has movement but no development. Dull moments are followed by moments of light, and the only continuity is a feeling, longing. His history is a history only as a plant has one.
- "For the mystic, the whole world is dead, he has fallen in love with God." He contemplates God until the divine image is reflected in him. His virtues are contemplative, not personal, and his real life is prayer. His prayer counts for more "the more erotic it is", and it is most blessed when it has least content, when he vanishes in his sigh.
- B's three personal objections to the mystic. First, a certain "importunity in his relationship to God": he disdains the reality God placed him in, and so disdains God's love. Samuel's saying applies, "To obey is better than sacrifice". It is worse when a mystic thinks some accidental trait makes him God's favourite, which debases God into a false god and himself into a courtier.
- Second, "softness and weakness": he tests his love of God at every moment instead of believing in his own love and continuing happily in the situation assigned to him, which is the surest expression of his love and humility.
- Third, his life is "a deceit against the world in which he lives". He chooses solitude, and the question is whether he has the right to. "It is especially as a husband, as a father, that I am an enemy of mysticism." B's household has its *adyton*, its inner sanctum, but a mystic would need another all for himself and would be a bad husband. Since B holds it every man's duty to marry, he must oppose mysticism.
- The one-sided mystic becomes indifferent to every relationship, even the tenderest. That is not what loving God more than father and mother means. God is not a poet who torments men with collisions between love of God and love of the people he lodged in our hearts.
- Young Ludvig Blackfeldt, whom both knew, was absorbed one-sidedly in Indian rather than Christian mysticism and took his own life. In the Middle Ages he would have found a monastery; the age has no such relative salvation. He was not religiously affected but mystically, since "what is peculiar to the mystical is not the religious but the isolation", the attempt at immediate rapport with the eternal.
- In church the individual feels isolated because his God-relationship is so strong. For a healthy person that instant passes and deepens his earthly relationships. Developed one-sidedly it is "a most critical sickness".
- The path is not just dangerous but false. The inconsistency: the mystic distrusts reality, yet not the moment in reality when he was affected by higher things.
- The mystic's fault "is not that he chooses himself" but "that he does not choose himself properly". He chooses in respect of his freedom yet not ethically, so he chooses himself abstractly and lacks transparency. "the abstract is the opaque, the indistinct." His love of God becomes a feeling, a mood, melting into God in the evening twilight.
- Only when one has penetrated oneself so totally that every movement carries "the consciousness of a responsibility for oneself" has one chosen oneself ethically, and only then is one, in total isolation, "in absolute continuity with the reality one belongs to".
- "The mystic too repents, but he repents himself out of himself, not into himself, he repents metaphysically, not ethically." To repent aesthetically is detestable, because effeminate; to repent metaphysically is a misplaced superfluity, since the individual did not create the world. The mystic's verdict that existence is vanity, illusion or sin is a metaphysical judgement, not an ethical relation.
- "Ethical repentance has but two movements; either it abolishes its object or it bears it." Fleeing is an abstract relation.
- The mystic chooses himself out of the world and cannot choose himself back. "The true concrete choice is that in which in the very moment I choose myself out of the world I choose myself back into it."
- The mystic can see the temporal only as a period of probation. B says the temporal is "the possibility of the finite spirit's glorification". It exists for man's sake and is "the greatest of all gifts of grace". Man's eternal dignity is that he can acquire a history, and the divine element in him is that he can give that history continuity by making it his own work, translating what befalls him "from necessity to freedom". A man can "come to the aid of the Deity".

### The ethical individual: concrete, tasks, transparency, duty inside (about pp. 544–551, PDF pp. 472–478)

- Both the Greek and the mystic failed because they chose themselves in isolation or abstractly, which is to say not ethically, so they were not connected with reality.
- The person who chooses himself ethically "chooses himself concretely as this definite individual", with these aptitudes, tendencies, instincts and passions, influenced by these surroundings, "this definite product of a definite outside world". He takes responsibility for all of it.
- At the instant of choice he is in complete isolation and at the same time in absolute continuity, since he chooses himself as product, and because it is freedom's choice "he can just as well be said to produce himself". He is at the conclusion and at the beginning at once. He transforms his outwardness into inwardness.
- His concretion is his reality, and since he chooses it in freedom it is his possibility, or better "his task". "the person who lives aesthetically sees only possibilities everywhere … the person who lives ethically sees tasks everywhere."
- This gives the ethical individual a sovereignty over himself and a security the aesthete lacks. The aesthete expects everything from outside, hence the anxiety about not having found one's place in the world.
- Three superstitions, each with a true core: that one's place makes one happy, that getting exactly the ideal girl makes one happy, that great events make a man into something. If his place or his choice disappoints him, the ethical person does not lose courage; "the art is not to wish but to will".
- "everywhere there is a dance floor", even the lowliest man has his own, and his dance can be as beautiful as that of those assigned a place in history. This suppleness is "the immortal life of the ethical".
- "to be or not to be" applies to the aesthete: if the least of his demands is not met, he is dead. The ethical person always has a way out, "there is always a point he keeps hold of, and it is – himself".
- Once the ethicist's gymnastics become experimentation he has stopped living ethically; that is sophistry in the realm of action.
- The ethical wants not to make the individual into another but into himself, "not to do away with the aesthetic but to transfigure it". It wants him aware of himself so totally that no accidental feature escapes him. People dread the ethical as abstract, the way they fear death, and both fears come from a reluctance to be transparent to oneself. "the ethical wants nothing else" than transparency.
- "Living for the fulfilment of life's duties" is a very imperfect expression of the ethical, almost as if invented to discredit it. Its mistake is to place the individual "in an external relation to duty", with duty as "a mass of particular propositions".
- The Danish word *Pligt* (duty) shows an internal relation: duty is not an imposition (*Paalæg*) but "something which imposes" (*paaligger*). Seeing duty this way shows the individual has found his bearings within himself.
- The truly ethical individual has duty inside him, not outside, and so has inner calm. He does not talk of duty every instant or confer with others about what his duty is. B can imagine no unhappier life than that of someone "who has got his duty outside him and yet is constantly wanting to realize it".
- If the ethical lies outside one's personality, one has despaired: the aesthetic as such is despair, and the ethical taken abstractly can bring about nothing. People straining after an ethical that runs away like a shadow are both comic and tragic.
- "The ethical is the universal and as such the abstract." In complete abstraction it is always interdictory and appears as law. As soon as it gives directives it contains something aesthetic. The Jews, "the people of the Law", understood the commandments but not the one Christianity took up, "Thou shalt love God with all thine heart", which is positive and concrete in the highest degree.
- Only when the individual himself is the universal can the ethical be realized. "This is the secret of conscience": the individual life is at once itself and the universal, at least in possibility.
- The aesthete is "the accidental man" and thinks he is the perfect man by being the only man. For the man aesthetically in love it matters that no one has loved with just his nuances. The ethical person works at becoming "the universal man", not by stripping off his concretion but by clothing himself in it and permeating it with the universal. If he marries, he realizes the universal, and sees in his love "a revelation of the universal human".
- "the person who lives ethically has himself as his task": to work the accidental and the universal together. He is like still waters that run deep; the aesthete is agitated on the surface.
- The goal, once the good fight is fought: to be "the only man", unlike any other, and at the same time the universal man. "that is the true art of living."
- Someone living ethically can behave exactly like an aesthete for a long time, until a moment shows he has a boundary the other does not recognize. He keeps "a blank space for what is ethically indifferent" and does not press the ethical on every detail. Those who cannot finish with the totality spend their lives "straining at a gnat". Likewise one believes in providence without applying it to every accident.
- He has himself as a task, "not as a possibility, not as a plaything for his caprice". He does not erase his multiplicity but repents himself firmly into it. The Danish for "he gives himself airs" literally says he creates himself, which language itself treats with contempt.

### Self-knowledge, the ideal self, and the universal man (about pp. 551–554, PDF pp. 478–481)

- The main difference between the ethical and the aesthetic individual, from which everything follows: "the ethical individual is transparent to himself and does not live 'out in the blue'". He is not like a witch's letter that shows a different picture depending on how the pages are turned.
- *Gnothi seauton* (know yourself) cannot be the goal of striving unless it is also the beginning. The ethical individual's self-knowledge "is a reflection on himself, which is itself an action", which is why B says "to choose oneself" rather than "to know oneself". As Adam knew Eve, the individual gets himself with child and gives himself birth.
- The self he knows is at once the actual self and the ideal self, an image he has outside him to form himself by, yet within him, since he is that self. If he thinks the universal man will come to meet him from outside, his method becomes "an abstract annihilation of the original self".
- The ideal self is only a prophecy. As he realizes it, it disappears into him, like a man's shadow: in front of him in the morning, beside him at noon, behind him in the evening.
- Copying another person and copying "the normal man" both go wrong in different ways.
- Essential and accidental. The aesthete distinguishes them, but the distinction is relative, since for him everything is equally accidental. The ethical individual learnt the distinction in despair: "Everything posited with his freedom belongs to him essentially, however accidental it may seem; everything else he sees as accidental, however essential it may seem."
- He is "his own editor" but with full editorial responsibility to himself, to the scheme of things he lives in and to God. What belongs to me essentially is what I undertake ethically as a task; if I refuse a task, the refusing belongs to me essentially.
- The aesthete who says his talent for painting is accidental but his wit and acuity essential is under an illusion. Unless he takes the wit upon himself as a task he is responsible for, it does not belong to him essentially, because his whole aesthetic life is unessential.
- The aesthete who sets a life-task with "aesthetic seriousness" only absorbs himself further in his contingency and becomes "a grimace of a man". B scorns the craze for originality.
- The ethical individual's task is to transform himself into the universal individual, which is possible only because he already has the universal in him *kata dunamin*, potentially. The universal can live in the singular "like the fire that burned in the bush without consuming it".
- If the universal lay outside me, the only method would be to strip off all concretion, like the Hussite sect who thought the normal man went naked like Adam and Eve. B's grammar image: a language has many more paradigm verbs than the grammars show, and any regular verb would do. "Everyone can, if he wants, become a paradigm man, not by wiping out his contingency but by remaining in it and ennobling it. But he ennobles it by choosing it."
- In the course of his life the ethical individual develops the personal, the civic and the religious virtues, "constantly translating himself from one stage to the next". Stopping one-sidedly at one stage means he has not grasped that the truth lies in the identity of isolation and continuity.

### Duty: the universal and the particular, intensity, and Balle's textbook (about pp. 554–561, PDF pp. 481–487)

- The task is "ordering, tempering, kindling, repressing", bringing about a proportionality in the soul, the harmony that is the fruit of the personal virtues. The self that is the aim is not an abstract self but a concrete one in living interaction with these surroundings, so it is also "a social, a civic self". His task is not only to mould himself but to exert an influence.
- Someone who thinks the knack is to begin as a Robinson Crusoe "will remain an adventurer all his life". The ethical individual translates himself from the personal life into the civic and back, and the personal life comes back in a higher form. "Personal being proves to be the absolute that has its teleology in itself."
- Two sceptical arguments against duty. The weaker: laws change, which touches only the civic virtues, while negative morals stay unchanged. The deeper: I cannot do my duty at all, since duty is the universal and I can only do the particular. B says this deeper argument proves that personal being is itself the absolute.
- Language shows it: one never says someone "does duty", one says he does *his* duty, I do *my* duty. The individual is at once the universal and the particular. "I can perform duties without doing *my* duty, and I can do *my* duty without performing duties."
- Personal being is not lawless and does not give itself its laws; the category of duty remains. It proves to be "the unity of the universal and the particular". Another man can never say what my duty is, but he can always say what his is.
- Making duty external, fixed and definite does not remove scepticism, because the doubt lies in the external, not in my relation to the universal. The dialectic of duty is within me.
- This does not abolish good and evil. No one has ever held it a duty to do evil; the evildoer tries to convince himself it was the good, and cannot keep that conviction, since he is himself the universal and has the enemy inside him, not outside. If duty were external, the difference between good and evil would be abolished.
- Freethinkers point to peoples who put their aged parents to death. B replies that the ethical lies in the internal, in the consciousness of the act, and the question is whether they thought they were doing evil. Opening the door to "historical infinity" is the easiest way to volatilize the ethical; empirical reality has no beginning, like the source of the Niger. When personal being is the absolute it is "the Archimedean point from which one can lift the world", and only as the particular is he the absolute, which saves him from revolutionary radicalism.
- B will not expound a doctrine of duties to God, oneself and one's neighbour, as in Balle's textbook. "what matters with the ethical is not the multiplicity of duty but its intensity." Once a person has felt duty's intensity in his whole being, he is ethically mature and duty breaks out in him. The saving thing is that a man, in relation to his own life, "is not his uncle but his father".
- The example from B's childhood. At five he was put in school and given the first ten lines of Balle's textbook to learn by heart. Everything else vanished; he tested himself before sleep and woke at five to read them again. "it was as though heaven and earth would collapse if I were not to learn my lines", and even if they collapsed, that would not exempt him from his assignment.
- At that age he had one duty, his homework, "and nevertheless from this impression I can derive the whole of my ethical view of life". He owes the impression to his father's serious-mindedness, which alone would put him eternally in his debt.
- In education what matters is not what the child learns but that the spirit is matured and energy aroused. "Give a man energy, passion, and he has everything." A silly, hysterical chatterbox who falls deeply in love grows shrewd and witty of herself.
- Without energy B cannot become ethically conscious, and he cannot become ethically conscious without becoming conscious of his eternal being. "That is the true proof of the immortality of the soul." The homework was the first proof produced for him; its incompleteness lay not in his energy but in the accidental nature of the task.
- Expressed negatively duty is easy to state; positively it is tedious and, beyond a point, impossible. B's aim was only to show "the absolute importance of duty, the eternal validity of the relationship of duty for personal being".
- Since personal being has not created itself but chosen itself, duty "is the expression of the identity of its absolute dependency and absolute freedom". One learns the particular duty oneself, "autodidact by virtue of being theodidact", self-taught by being God-taught.

## The aesthetic returns in the ethical

### Does the ethical rob life of beauty? (about pp. 561–565, PDF pp. 487–490)

- Aesthetes concede that living for one's duties is respectable, even good for the majority, but will not embark on the ethical because it would deprive life of meaning and beauty, and once it appears it destroys the aesthetic.
- If that were true B would still choose the ethical. In despair it looks that way for an instant, and the despair of someone who has not felt it so was fraudulent. "But that is not how it is, and therefore, in the next instant, despair proves not to be a breach but a metamorphosis. Everything returns, but transfigured."
- Only regarded ethically does life acquire "beauty, truth, meaning, substance", and only lived ethically does one's own life acquire beauty, truth, meaning and security. Doubt about one's own life and doubt about others' are the same doubt and are stilled by the same thing.
- B borrows A's definition: "The beautiful is that which has its teleology within itself." A's examples are a young girl, complete in herself; nature; and art and poetry, guarded by the poet's "*Procul o, procul este, profani*" (keep far off, you uninitiated) against anyone who gives them an outside purpose.
- B's reply. Art and poetry give only an imperfect reconciliation with life, and A's gaze on them is not a gaze on reality. In life A's beauty is really "the individually beautiful": he sees each person as an element in the whole, so each person's teleology lies outside him, and the parts are not beautiful in themselves.
- Teleology needs movement. Beauty in nature exists all at once, and when I look at a work of art the movement occurs in me, not in it. So A's phrase is only negative: it says the beautiful is not for something else. The moment A says "inner teleology" or "immanent teleology", he requires movement and history, and he has left nature and art "and [is] in that of freedom, and so of ethics".
- The individual has his teleology within him and "is himself his own teleology". His self is absolutely concrete, so he cannot relate negatively to the world around him. "his movement will be from himself, through the world, to himself." That movement is the work of freedom and immanent teleology at once, and only here can there first be any question of beauty.
- The individual stands higher than any relationship and is still in it. B humbles himself before his marriage and knows that in another sense he is higher than it, and that exactly the same applies to his wife.
- This beauty can be seen "in the trace of the historical", as in *Loquere ut videam te* (speak, so that I may see you). What B sees is not the consummation but the struggle, and it takes courage to see it.
- B at his window sees every passer-by, however humble, as "this individual person who is at the same time the universal man", with a concrete task, "even if he were the humblest hired waiter". "the man of courage does not see ghosts, on the contrary he sees conquering heroes; while the coward does not see heroes but only ghosts."
- B would fight to the death for this faith in the victory of the beautiful. The beauty he sees "is joyful and triumphant", without the sadness that belongs to all beauty in nature and art, even to the eternal youth of the Greek gods.

### Work, vocation and accomplishment (about pp. 566–572, PDF pp. 491–496)

- B now takes "some real-life situations" where the aesthetic and the ethical meet, with a prosaic hero who has to live and clothe himself.
- The aesthete's advice: when single one needs three thousand dollars a year, four is better, and marriage needs at least six thousand. Money is "the absolute condition of life"; without it one is a plebeian. If the hero has nothing, "there's nothing for it but to put up with the workhouse", unless he tries the makeshifts: marry a rich girl, play the lottery, go to the colonies, or curry favour with an old bachelor to become his heir. B calls this heartless, since it murders all joy in life for everyone without money.
- The ethicist's answer: "It is every man's duty to work in order to live." He addressed the hero politely and made the aesthete the exception, since whoever need not work is an exception, and "there is nothing great, only inferior, in being an exception". Ethically, having money is a humiliation, because every special favour is one.
- Clamour about money being the main thing comes partly from workers who lack the ethical energy to acknowledge the significance of working. "Marriage is not harmed by seducers but by cowardly husbands." So too the good cause is harmed by those who work and sigh that "The finest thing, after all, is to be independent."
- A world where work was unnecessary is a fiction, and asking about it belittles the ethical. If working were a lamentable necessity, duty would express only what is general, not the universally human. It would be an imperfection of existence if man had no need to work: "The lower the level of human life, the less the necessity of work is apparent; the higher, the more obvious it becomes."
- The duty to work expresses the universally human, and the universal in a second way, because it expresses freedom: "through work he becomes master of nature, through work he shows he is higher than nature." No struggle is more formative than making ends meet.
- Two advantages of the ethical view of work: it accords with reality and explains something universal in it, while the aesthetic view proposes something accidental and explains nothing; and it sees man in his perfection and true beauty.
- The third adviser: "One must work in order to live, that's just the way life is." Sleep seven hours, work five dull hours for a living, and never make one's talent one's livelihood; coddle the talent in the twelve hours left and be "a non-human for five". The hero has no such talent and already thinks better of work.
- Back to the ethicist, who can only repeat that it is every man's duty to work, since the ethical is abstract. There is no abstract vocation for all men; he assumes "every person has a special vocation".
- The aesthete's life-views rest on difference: some have talent, others not, a more-or-less, a quantitative difference, and it is arbitrary where he stops. The ethicist reconciles man with life: "Everyone has a vocation." "Behind all differences there remains the universal, and it is a vocation." The most eminent talent is a vocation, and the least significant individual has one and is not sent to live next to the beasts. Instead of an aristocracy by accident, "one has a kingdom of gods".
- The hero's work is now his vocation, so it satisfies his whole personality, and it puts him on the same footing as everyone else, since in doing his work he does essentially what all others do.
- Two advantages of vocation over talent: it makes transparent the universal, not something accidental; and it shows the universal in its true beauty. "Talent is beautiful only when it becomes transparent in a vocation, and existence is beautiful only when everyone has a vocation."
- B's empirical observation: a vocation gives a standard outside oneself that apportions time and offers a fresh start after a failure. Someone without one must work without a break, and if he fails everything fails, so he is tempted to become a pedant, "unless he becomes a loafer". People who perform definite functions can hardly become pedants.
- On "accomplish": "Essentially everyone accomplishes as much as anyone else." A once joked he had studied integral, differential and infinitesimal calculus to work out what part of the whole a competent junior Admiralty clerk accomplished. B tells him to use ridicule on the self-important only.
- The word indicates a relation between my action and something outside me, over which I have no authority. The most talented and the least able each can complete his job and neither can do more. What I accomplish is my good fortune, which I may enjoy but not credit to myself. B's image is the beech that grows its crown whether or not anyone comes to sit in its shade.

### Marriage and the uncommon girl (about pp. 572–577, PDF pp. 496–500)

- The hero now has a living, and A sneers that "with a living comes a wife". The hero wants to keep love free of the ethicist's seriousness and goes back to the aesthetes once more.
- He learns what not to do. He sees through the seducer as a liar who deceives doubly, the woman he feigns feelings for and the one they once belonged to. He despises the ridicule that makes love a childish prank and the distrust of life that says never plan a whole life.
- What he now counts as insults: to love someone on the basis of what is obscure in oneself rather than what is conscious; to love with one part of the soul and take the whole of another's love; to want to be a riddle; to wish for a hundred arms to embrace many; to attach oneself conditionally, so one could change if difficulties arose. Love demands "a holy tax which is paid in its own currency".
- He accepts the ethicist's statement that "it is every man's duty to marry", understood correctly: not that it is a sin not to marry, unless by one's own fault, but that "he who marries realizes the universal".
- The ethicist can bring him no further, because the ethical is abstract and cannot tell him whom to marry. That would need knowledge of all his aesthetic characteristics, and choosing for him would make nonsense of the theory. Once he has chosen, the ethical sanctions and elevates his love, and it helps by freeing him from "a superstitious belief in the accidental".
- The sharp corner: a man the hero respects says that since marriage binds one for life, one must choose "an uncommon girl" whose uncommon qualities give security for the whole future. B imagines a forest nymph arriving in Copenhagen and choosing the hero; even B, quoting Donna Clara, calls her "the beautiful Preciosa".
- Marriage takes nothing from him or her and abolishes no difference. What it does is make the differences look accidental as long as marriage stays outside him; only when he gives the difference the expression of the universal is he secure in possessing it. "The ethical teaches him that the relationship is the absolute, for the relationship is the universal." It "deprives him of the vain joy of being uncommon in order to give him the true joy of being ordinary".
- Whether he gets the prodigy or a less gifted girl, he says essentially the same: "I am no different from any other married man, for the relationship is the absolute." He still values the difference: as there is no abstract vocation, there is no abstract marriage. "Ethics tells him simply that he must marry; it does not tell him with whom."
- Advantages of the ethical view of marriage: it highlights the universal, not the accidental; it shows how every married couple can become happy, not how two uncommon people could; it treats differences as tasks, not guarantees; it looks on love "with a view to its freedom" and understands historical beauty.
- In the event there was a misunderstanding with the nymph, and the hero married a girl like any other. He is glad: with a prodigy "the task would have been too much for him". He has the courage to believe the relationship can develop an ordinary girl into everything great and beautiful, and his wife humbly agrees.
- Time is a blessing to him. Had he kept the nymph without marrying, their love would have flared in single moments with vapid intervals between. The humble marriage, which made it a duty to see each other daily, "for richer or poorer", spread evenness over the relationship. "the prosaic marriage had concealed a poet".

### Woman and time (about pp. 577–582, PDF pp. 500–504)

- B confesses hours when he inwardly collapses: his melancholy temperament gains the upper hand, he feels old, and time drags. His wife is "on good terms with time". Watching her move about the room, always occupied, he finds himself back in time and the moment moves swiftly again.
- The story of the Dutch orientalist. He missed dinner because of a diacritic he had never seen in an excellent Dutch edition. His wife bent over him, said it was not worth wasting breath on, blew, and the dot vanished: it was a grain of snuff. He went happily to dinner, "happy that the vowel point had disappeared, even happier in his wife".
- The moral: unmarried he might have gone mad. As an aesthete he might have had the prodigy in a palace, visited her, charmed her, and come home at dawn to sit staring at the dot, with "not a wife who could blow the dot away".
- "Woman clarifies finiteness, man chases after infinity." Woman has "an innate talent and a primitive gift for clarifying finiteness". In B's retelling of the creation, man stood lord of all finiteness and did not know what to do with it; woman knew straightaway, and her humble comfort became life's richest joy. Her need, which she did not understand, was "to make the interval shorter".
- Woman bears children in pain, man conceives ideas in pain; the anxiety of doubt and the torment of despair are not for her. She has the idea at second hand. She is man's deepest life, but a hidden one, like a root.
- B says he hates all talk of the emancipation of women and calls it a seducer's most dangerous doctrine, since once a woman believes she could be more perfect "within the category of man" she is at his mercy. This is B's 1843 view, not a point Kraal endorses; know that B holds it.
- Because woman gives man finiteness "she is stronger than he". That is why Scripture says a man, not a maiden, shall leave father and mother and cleave to his wife. Woman is B's symbol of the congregation, and he says the congregation should be represented in church by a woman rather than a deacon or a bellringer.

### Friendship, and the duty to become revealed (about pp. 582–587, PDF pp. 504–508)

- The hero becomes B's friend, and B turns to friendship and its ethical validity, which A thinks falls entirely under aesthetic categories.
- B's observation: volatile natures form friendships in early youth, since their selves are currency from the start; deeper natures find themselves later and cannot wish for a friendship they cannot requite. "an observer is no friend." Two abnormal patterns: early friends who drop friendship when love arrives, and those burned by love too early who choose friendship instead.
- A gives opposite accounts of friendship, which shows that "sentimentality and callousness are one and the same". One moment it is the divine meeting of kindred souls; the next, "A friend … is an enigmatic thing; like fog, he can only be seen at a distance", noticed only once one is unhappy. A ends by calling friendship nonsense.
- "The absolute condition for friendship is full agreement in life-view." Then one does not build friendship on obscure emotions, and one does not have a friend one day and not the next. Consciousness saves true friendship from being infatuation.
- The shared life-view must be positive. B and his friend become serious when they look at each other; the Augurs laughed because their common life-view was negative. A's wish for "a kindred soul with whom you can laugh at it all" cannot ground a friendship, and B asks whether A has found his friends in the Symparanekromenoi, a society that esteems its members for hiding their madness.
- A positive life-view must contain an ethical factor. People with "a system in which there is no room for the ethical" have no life-view at all. "So really the ethical factor in the life-view is the starting-point for friendship."
- Friendship built on sympathy alone finds its fullest form in the lovebirds that die together, which is beautiful in nature and "ugly in the world of the spirit". Unity in life-view constitutes friendship: it endures if the friend dies, and ends if the unity ends though the friend lives.
- Aristotle made friendship the starting point of his ethical view, broadening justice until friendship and justice come to the same. B calls his category more perfect than the modern one that bases right on duty, "on the abstract categorical" (Kant), because it rests on the social, but imperfect because it makes the State the highest idea.
- B could say it is every man's duty to have a friend, but prefers a formula that covers friendship and everything else and separates the ethical from the aesthetic: "It is every man's duty to become revealed."
- Scripture says that after death comes the judgement, when everything is revealed. "Ethics says it is the meaning of life and reality that man be revealed." If a man is not, the revelation takes the form of punishment.
- The aesthete remains concealed, because however much he abandons himself to the world he always holds something back; doing it totally would be doing it ethically. Hide-and-seek avenges itself by making one a mystery to oneself. All mystics, who refuse reality's demand to be revealed, meet trials no one else knows: "The person who will not fight with reality gets phantoms to contend with."

## The close

### B's testimony, the uncommon man, and the farewell (about pp. 587–594, PDF pp. 508–514)

- B never meant to propound a theory of duty. He wanted to show that in "the mixed regions" the ethical imparts beauty to life. It gives peace, assurance and security, crying "*quod petis, hic est*" (what you seek is here). It teaches one to be happy in good fortune, which the aesthete cannot be, since good fortune is "an infinite relativity", and to be happy in misfortune.
- The letter is a trifle, "notes on Balle's textbook", yet it has authority. B has not acted as judge in his own case: he is not even the party opposed to A, and he does not speak for ethics. "I am nothing but a witness", and "a person who speaks of what he has experienced always has authority".
- His testimony: he serves as a judge and is glad of that vocation; he loves his wife and is happy in his home; his work matters to him and to others, in a measure he cannot determine; he loves his native country and his mother tongue. "Thus my life has importance for me." In the respiration of his earthly existence he sometimes inhales a higher life. "Thus I love existence because it is beautiful, and hope for one that is more beautiful still."
- The testimony's one property that A's life lacks is dependability. A can safely build on it.
- B's wife is fond of A and sees his weaknesses: "what you lack is some degree of womanliness", since A is too proud to give himself up to anyone. B pleads for A against her, saying A rejects people "in an infinite sense", out of restlessness for the infinite. So B's marriage has its conflict, and A is to blame.
- The question that would settle it: not whether A laughs when alone, but whether he finds that lonely laughter satisfying. If not, B has won.
- A's life is planned to an unusual standard; he will not follow the highways. One owes such a person the shout "Think of the end!", where the end means life, not death, since the real question is when one begins to live.
- The Middle Ages went into the monastery. The error was not the step but the conception that it made one uncommon, looking down on ordinary people. "the gods do not sell the unusual at a bargain price." Honest withdrawers would have counted themselves less perfect than others, not more.
- "The truly extraordinary man is the truly ordinary man. The more of the universally human an individual is able to realize in his life, the more extraordinary he is."
- Someone who cannot realize some part of the universal, blamelessly or not, first deliberates how far that is true, knowing that laziness and cowardice make people fancy it. Then he risks an experiment, and arranges it so that what wounds him is the universal, not the particular: he makes the particular into the universal, sets his desire on the enemy's side, and goes calmly to meet the pain.
- If the defeat comes he has emancipated himself from the universal on that point, with a clear explanation he could give if woken in the night. He sorrows deeply and frankly, and bears witness to the universal with his sorrow. He feels the responsibility of standing outside the universal, which is "a severe master" asking "Why do you want to be outside?", and trusts in a righteous rationality compassionate enough to punish wrongdoing. B calls the whole struggle "a purgatory".
- Such a person is reconciled with the universal through his sorrow and may become uncommon "in a nobler sense", gaining in intensity what he lost in compass. He sees "that in a sense everyone is an exception, and that it is equally true that everyone is the universally human and at the same time an exception". Even so, it would be more perfect still to take possession of the whole universal.
- The farewell. B hopes one day to call A his friend in the strict sense, sends his wife's greeting, and explains why he wrote rather than spoke: A hates having his inner history talked about. The letter stays a secret and should change nothing in A's relationship with B's family. B wishes him farewell as though they were far apart, and hopes to see him at his house as often as before.
