# PHIL 385 — Reading: *Either/Or*, "Equilibrium between the Aesthetic and the Ethical in the Development of Personality"

## What B asks A to choose

- Judge William, or B, is writing to A for the second and final time. In [the first letter](aesthetic-validity.md), he argued that a marriage gives first love a life beyond its beginning. Here he asks what it would take for A to commit to a way of life at all.
- A tries to keep his options open. A career can interest him for months and then lose its appeal as soon as something else comes along. B thinks each new interest still leaves A depending on a mood or circumstance. B calls that dependence **despair**, even when A feels fine.
- B asks A to face that dependence and **choose himself**. This means taking responsibility for the life he already has and deciding how to live it. A's abilities, interests and pleasures still belong to him. They now have a place in a life he chooses. That is the **equilibrium** the letter is about.

This letter is on PDF pp. 415–514 of Hannay’s abridged Penguin translation. The "Last Word" introducing the Jutland priest's sermon starts on PDF p. 515. Hannay's Penguin pages are approximate except for the passages marked with specific pages from lecture. Exam 4 covers both letters.

## The opening, and why choice cannot wait

### Why keeping options open is itself consequential (PDF pp. 415–423)

- A treats **either/or** as a joke. He tells people that whatever they do, they will regret it. One of his favourite stories offers a man a beating whether he picks up his hat or leaves it on the floor.
- B thinks A would give different advice to a gifted young person who trusted him. He would urge the young person to take their time and abilities seriously. B uses this to show that A already sees the importance of choice when he cares what happens to somebody.
- For B, a decision shapes the person who makes it. Think of a helmsman whose ship keeps moving while he decides whether to turn. If he waits long enough, the moment for turning passes and the ship's course is settled for him. A similarly weighs becoming a priest, an actor and then a lawyer, and spends a year and a half going nowhere.
- B wants A to take possession of himself before his moods and circumstances settle his life for him. The warning about the masquerade makes the same point. If A wears a different mask for every person, he may lose the ability to say who he is or reveal himself to anybody.

## The choice, and what philosophy cannot do

### The two choices in B's either/or (Hannay p. 480 and following, PDF pp. 423–424)

Imagine you damage a friend’s bike while borrowing it. You could tell them what happened or say it was already broken. B sees two choices here.

1. **The first either/or is how you will live.** You could treat the situation as a question of which story will work. Or you could let right and wrong matter in how you decide what to do, with this friend and across your life. B calls taking up that responsibility the **choice to will**.
2. **The absolute either/or is what you will do.** Once you have taken responsibility for living ethically, you can still tell the truth or lie. B calls that the choice between **good and evil**. Choosing to live ethically makes the distinction matter. It leaves you responsible for each act that follows.

- B calls the aesthetic position **indifference** to good and evil. He also calls an aesthetic choice no choice in the strict sense. It can follow an immediate attraction, or it can get lost in comparing possibilities. In the bike example, you could keep trying out stories in your head while letting which one feels persuasive decide for you.
- Someone who chooses seriously can discover that they chose badly and correct it. B calls the moment when a person has exhausted ways of avoiding the decision the **crossroads**. Kraal linked the first either/or to Nietzsche’s idea of going beyond good and evil. [Lecture 21](../lectures/21-equilibrium-hegels-framework-and-the-real-either-or.md)

### Thought, freedom and Hegel (PDF pp. 424–427)

- **Mediation** means understanding an opposition by showing how its sides belong to a wider whole. Looking back, a philosopher can explain how competing actions became part of one history. B accepts a place for this kind of thought in **logic, nature and history**, the spheres he associates with necessity. In history the mediation is relative because the story is still going on.
- A decision concerns a future one still has to act in. Two possible actions can both make sense to an observer. The person who acts still has to do one and give up the other. B calls this the sphere of **freedom**. The difference between the alternatives matters because the act settles what the person does.
- Kraal uses Hegel to explain the background. I am conscious both that I can step back from a given desire (**indeterminacy**) and that I can commit myself to a purpose (**determinacy**). Kraal connects this to Hegel's claim that what is rational is actual and what is actual is rational. If freedom is a rational account of what I am, it has to be recognized and realized in a shared life, through institutions such as family, civil society and the state. That helps explain why Hegel calls marriage an ethical relationship.
- B agrees that philosophy studies necessity. He thinks a person's inward commitment to act is a work of freedom. A historical explanation can show how an action entered the wider course of events. The agent still has to make the decision. Kraal contrasts Hegel's emphasis on objectivity with Kierkegaard's formula that truth is subjectivity. ### What changes when a person chooses themselves (PDF pp. 427–428)

- B gives two definitions. The **aesthetic factor** is how a person is immediately what they are, through the qualities and inclinations they find themselves with. The **ethical factor** is how a person becomes who they become, through what they choose and take responsibility for.
- B also calls choosing oneself **accepting oneself**. The individual takes up the life, character and history they already have. They can still love music, enjoy beauty and have moods. The change is that those things no longer decide how to live the whole of their life.

## Every aesthetic life is despair

### What the aesthetic life depends on (PDF pp. 428–436)

- The aesthetic view says that **one must enjoy life**. B adds a condition. Enjoyment depends on something outside the person, or on something in the person that they did not put there. A person can work at a talent, for example, but did not choose to be born with that aptitude.
  - **Health and beauty** are conditions found in oneself. B recalls the countess who still thinks her Ditlev is the handsomest man in the kingdom.
  - **Wealth, fame and rank** are outside conditions. So is the beloved for someone who says life has meaning only if that person loves them.
  - **Talent** is another condition found in oneself, whether it is an aptitude for business, mathematics or art.
  - A **life of desire** depends on satisfying one appetite after another. Nero has the means to do this and still gets bored. A asks whether Nero had enough of the artist in him to enjoy seeing Rome burn like Troy. B sees Nero's chase for stronger entertainment as a symptom of melancholy.
  - The **Epicurean** seeks to enjoy himself in whatever he enjoys. The **Cynic** takes pleasure in being able to discard each source of enjoyment. Both still need circumstances to supply something to enjoy or discard.
- B calls **melancholy** the **hysteria of the spirit**. A person senses a need to understand and direct their life as a whole, yet holds back from making any deeper commitment. Nero responds by looking for another amusement. They can feel weighed down without being able to name a loss. B treats that refusal to will seriously as a sin. Sorrow, by comparison, can have a definite cause and can come through no fault of the person who suffers it.

### Why the despair is there before a loss (Hannay pp. 501–502 on the argument, PDF pp. 436–443 for the section)

Imagine a musician who sees being able to perform as the one thing that makes their life worthwhile. Then an injury takes away their ability to perform.

1. **Before the injury**, they felt successful. Their sense of worth still depended on an ability that could be lost.
2. **After the injury**, they can feel how much depended on that ability. The injury exposed a dependence already there.
3. **B calls the dependence despair.** That is why he says that every aesthetic life-view is already despair. The person may feel happy while the condition holds.

- B’s own example is a painter who goes blind. Getting his sight back would end that particular despair. A has suffered no comparable loss. He already sees that wealth, admiration or love could stop satisfying him, yet keeps finding distractions. B calls A’s position **thought-despair**.
- When A gets absorbed in a project, he works intensely and then drops it. The thought that he could have continued lets him keep his options open. B grants that finite goods can be worth having and still fall short of what someone seeks when they want something eternal.

### What B means by “Despair!” (Hannay pp. 511–512, PDF pp. 445–446)

- The musician could go looking for another success to serve the same role. If they pin their whole worth on recovering or becoming successful at something else, B calls that **finite despair**. What the life depends on has changed. The dependence is still there.
- **Choosing despair** means deliberately facing that dependence. Think of the musician asking, “If I can no longer perform, how am I going to live this life?” B wants them to take responsibility for answering as the person they already are, with their history, relationships and abilities. That is what he means by **choosing oneself**.
- B calls despair **absolute** or **willed infinitely** when the person gives up making any finite loss or gain the ground of their whole life. He calls this **absolute self-surrender**. The musician can still work toward recovery or stay involved in music. Their responsibility for how they live reaches further than whether they can perform again (PDF pp. 447–449, 453–455).
- B tells A to **Despair!** because A already sees the limits of his distractions. A marriage or a job taken for the sake of distraction would repeat the pattern. Kraal connects B’s command to the **crossroads**, the point where A has to decide how he will live. B also illustrates the power of despair through a young man who discovers what he and the woman he loves share as human beings (Hannay p. 512). ## Choosing oneself

### Doubt, freedom and eternal validity (Hannay pp. 513–515 on doubt and despair, PDF pp. 447–453 for the section)

- B's formula is that **doubt is a despair of thought, and despair is a doubt of the personality**. Doubt concerns what a person can think or know. Despair asks the whole person what they will live for. B therefore makes despair the starting point for finding the absolute.
- **Choosing oneself in one's eternal validity** means taking oneself up as a free person responsible for their life. A talent, an income or a mood can change. In B's account, one's responsibility for choosing how to live goes deeper than any of those features. His first definition of the self one chooses is **freedom**.
- B calls the self abstract because he can speak of freedom apart from any particular trait. He also calls it concrete because the freedom belongs to this actual person, with these abilities, relationships and this past. The choice brings into being a way of living a life the person already has.
- This is the point of the paired formula about choosing the absolute that chooses him and positing the absolute that posits him. B actively takes up a self he also receives. He calls it choosing oneself.

### Why repentance belongs to the choice (PDF pp. 451–452)

- Choosing the whole self includes accepting responsibility for its past. B calls that movement **repentance**. A person faces guilt and the history that connects them to other people, through their family and ultimately to God.
- B calls repentance the one expression of the love of God because God loved the person first. He describes Christian repentance as reaching even to guilt inherited from one's family. He attributes the limit he sees in earlier Jewish piety to its inability, in his account, to choose the self absolutely.
- B illustrates this with his son. One day the boy may face the legacy of B's own guilt. B can care for him now, but making that choice will be the boy's own work.

### Why B says thought cannot establish good and evil (Hannay p. 524, PDF pp. 456–457)

- Go back to the broken bike. You can think through whether a lie will work, how much the repair costs, or how your friend might react. These are **relative differences**. Each answer depends on the comparison you are making.
- B says those comparisons cannot establish the **absolute difference** between good and evil. They still leave you with a decision. Will you take responsibility for acting on what you take to be right? He says that good and evil become a live issue for you when you choose to live ethically. This is what he means when he says that the good exists by virtue of his **willing** it.
- B still claims **absolute validity** for good and evil. Your choice is about taking responsibility for how you live. If you decide to lie, your preference for the easier story has no power to make it good.
- Kraal raised the possibility that a person can **intuit** the ethical, or see it directly through reason. In the example, suppose you see immediately that you should tell your friend what happened. Kraal’s reply on B’s behalf is that you still have to decide to trust that judgment and live by it. ## The ethical life-view

### Continuity through changing moods (PDF pp. 457–461)

- B compares aesthetic development to a plant growing according to what was already in it. An aesthete may develop different talents or tastes. Their development still follows the qualities and conditions they find themselves with. The ethical individual decides what place those qualities will have in their life in light of good and evil.
- Moods are still part of the ethical person's life. B calls their ability to stay responsible across changes of mood **continuity**. A gambler who can put off acting on a desire for an hour has begun to make space between feeling the urge and doing what it asks.
- B calls the change a **transfiguration**. A person's aesthetic qualities have a place in the ethical life because they can now be put to use within it.
- **Narcissus** shows a false way of choosing oneself. The man treats his self as something fixed and complete and gazes at it like Narcissus. B says he reaches eternity before his time. A free choice starts a person's work of becoming themselves and has to be realized in time.

### Sorrow, the Greek paragon and the mystic (PDF pp. 461–472)

- A sometimes treats sorrow as the meaning of life. B thinks this still depends on a condition that can change. Making grief the source of meaning repeats the problem of making pleasure the source of meaning. B allows room for sorrow and connects it with guilt, repentance and the hope of forgiveness.
- The **Greek paragon of virtue** works on personal virtues such as courage and temperance in isolation from civic life. B calls the choice abstract because it leaves out the person's real ties and obligations.
- The **mystic** devotes himself to a direct relation to God and withdraws from ordinary life. B has three objections.
  1. **Importunity.** He seeks God so insistently that he scorns the life God gave him.
  2. **Softness.** He keeps testing whether he feels his love of God and has difficulty trusting and living it.
  3. **Deceit.** His withdrawal neglects the people and relationships that have claims on him. B speaks here as a husband and father.
- B uses Ludvig Blackfeldt to illustrate the danger of religious or mystical isolation. B says the mystic repents **out of himself** and leaves the world behind. Ethical repentance brings the person **into themselves** and back to the concrete responsibilities of their life.

### Taking one's actual life as a task (PDF pp. 472–481)

- The ethical individual chooses a **concrete self**. This means taking responsibility for one's particular abilities, habits, relationships and circumstances. If a job disappoints, for example, B asks what a person can do with the situation that is actually theirs. The aesthete sees possibilities, and the ethical individual sees **tasks**.
- B calls the ethical individual **transparent to themselves**. They recognize what they are doing and take responsibility for it. Self-knowledge is therefore part of an action, which is why B says to choose oneself.
- The **accidental man** puts his value in what happens to make him different from others. The **universal man** realizes something shared by human beings, such as the capacity for fidelity, within his own particular life. The goal is to be a distinct individual while making the universally human real through one's actions.
- B still leaves space for things that are **ethically indifferent**. Choosing the ethical does not turn each small preference into a moral decision.

### What makes a duty mine (PDF pp. 475–487)

- B thinks a checklist of things imposed from outside misses how duty works. He uses the Danish **Pligt** and connects it to **paaligger**, what lies upon or is incumbent on a person. He distinguishes this from **Paalæg**, an imposition. The ethical person owns the responsibility and can act on it as their own.
- The universal becomes real in a particular action. When I say **my duty**, I mean what a shared obligation asks of me in my particular situation. That is why B calls duty the **unity of the universal and the particular**. Going through the outward motions of several duties can still leave one's actual responsibility unmet.
- When B was five, he had to learn ten lines from **Balle's textbook**. He tested himself at night and got up early to practise. The exercise shows what B calls the **intensity** of duty. One small task can make a person feel the full seriousness of taking responsibility for something.

## The aesthetic returns in the ethical

### What makes a life beautiful (PDF pp. 487–490)

- A defines the beautiful as having its **teleology** within itself. This means that something is worthwhile in its own right. He points to nature and art, which can be beautiful as we look at them.
- B asks what it means to live out an inner purpose. That takes movement and a history. A person realizes a purpose through decisions over time, as a spouse lives out fidelity day after day. B takes this to be a beauty available to any ethical individual, including someone whose work and circumstances seem ordinary.

### Work and vocation (PDF pp. 491–496)

- An aesthete advises a poor man to get money, the supposed condition of a good life. B says it is everyone's duty to work to live. He sees work both as a universal feature of life and as a way people exercise freedom by shaping the conditions they live in.
- **Everyone has a vocation**, or a particular calling in which they can take up the shared task of working. Talent varies. An exceptional talent can become a vocation, and someone with an ordinary aptitude has a vocation as well.
- **Accomplishment** depends partly on what happens outside one's control. Both a famous person and a worker with a modest role can do everything their work calls for. B compares this to a beech that grows its crown whether anybody comes to sit in its shade.

### Marriage and the uncommon girl (PDF pp. 496–500)

- For B, **it is every man's duty to marry** means that a person who marries realizes something universally human. He distinguishes that claim from calling everyone who remains single a sinner. The ethical can state the general duty. The individual must choose whom to love and marry.
- The hero hears that only an uncommon girl, like the forest nymph B imagines choosing him, can guarantee a good marriage. B says **the relationship is the absolute**. The spouse's gifts can enrich it, and the couple must still build a life together. Their individual differences become tasks the two can work with.
- The hero marries an ordinary girl and finds that seeing each other daily gives their love time to develop. B uses this as a further example of what he called inner history in the first letter.

### The Dutch orientalist and finiteness (PDF pp. 500–504)

- A scholar misses dinner because he is puzzled by a dot in a text. His wife leans over and blows it away. It was a grain of snuff. B uses this to illustrate his claim that **woman clarifies finiteness, man chases after infinity**. His wife brings him back to the immediate world of work, time and dinner.
- B uses the same idea when he says that watching his wife go about the room helps him when time drags. These are Judge William's claims about women and men.

### Friendship and becoming revealed (PDF pp. 504–508)

- B says friendship requires **full agreement in a positive life-view**. Sharing an outlook on how to live gives a friendship continuity. Enjoying the same joke about the world or feeling sympathy for someone can bring people together. B thinks lasting friendship requires a shared ethical basis.
- B says Aristotle made friendship the starting point of his ethics and related it to justice. He values the social basis of Aristotle's account and thinks it gives the state too high a place.
- B's broader formula is that **it is every man's duty to become revealed**. A person needs enough honesty about who they are to be open with others. A keeps something in reserve with everyone, and B thinks this makes A a mystery even to himself. B says that if a person will not become revealed freely, revelation takes the form of punishment.

## The close

### B as a witness and the meaning of being ordinary (PDF pp. 508–514)

- B presents himself as a **witness**. His marriage, his work as a judge and his love of his country and language give him something to testify about. His claim is that the ethical gives these parts of an ordinary life beauty and a dependability A is missing.
- **The truly extraordinary man is the truly ordinary man.** For B, what makes a person extraordinary is how fully they realize possibilities common to human beings. If someone cannot realize one of them, B asks them to examine that limit honestly, test it if possible, and acknowledge their loss without treating themselves as superior for being an exception.
- B hopes that he and A can become friends in the strict sense. He asks A to keep the letter private and still come to the house as often as before.
