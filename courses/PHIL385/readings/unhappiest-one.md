## The whole essay, start to finish
You read part 1 and then asked me to work through the rest (2026-09-16). This is the walkthrough,
in my words, with the Danish checked at tekster.kb.dk and the allusions against the Søren
Kierkegaard Research Centre's commentary. Short phrases in quotation marks are the wording worth
carrying into an exam.

### Who is speaking, and the heading
- The full heading is three lines: "The Unhappiest One", then "An enthusiastic address to
  Symparanekromenoi", then "Peroration in the Friday meetings". All three are printed in the book.
- A *peroration* is the closing part of a speech. So the essay presents itself as the last item at a
  regular Friday meeting of the club, not as an essay.
- The speaker is A, not Kierkegaard. The audience is the Symparanekromenoi, the fellowship of the
  already-dead: people who describe themselves as living "aphoristically", set apart and segregated,
  solitary birds in the night, believing in nothing but misfortune. The Greek and Latin words A uses
  for "set apart" are the ones Paul uses of himself in Romans 1:1.

### The grave, and where it came from
- A reports a grave somewhere in England marked only with the inscription "the unhappiest one". It
  was opened, and no body was found.
- The real source is a grave slab inscribed *Miserrimus* in Worcester Cathedral. Kierkegaard noted
  it in 1840, and the note already contains the whole idea: that someone might read the inscription,
  conclude nobody was buried there at all, and think the grave was meant for him.
- The empty grave is an Easter allusion, deliberately. So is the line near the end of the essay
  about the stone being rolled away. Both echo the women at Christ's tomb in Mark 16.
- The lines of verse A quotes about peace in the grave are from Christen Pram's epic *Stærkodder*
  of 1785.
- A poses two possibilities and takes the second: either the man has risen and wanders the world,
  or he simply has not been found yet. So the fellowship sets off west as crusaders, to the
  sorrowful grave in the unhappy west rather than the holy grave in the happy east.

### The rules of the competition
- **Who is shut out.** Anyone who counts death the greatest misfortune, and anyone who became
  unhappy because he feared death. The fellowship does not fear death; it knows a worse misfortune,
  which is living.
- **The man who cannot die is considered and rejected.** A raises the legend of the Wandering Jew:
  if such a man existed the grave would be explained and the contest over. Then he cancels it.
  Death is the common good fortune of all human beings, so the unhappiest must be sought inside
  that limit. On the way past he runs the old Greek ladder: happy the one who could die, happy the
  one who died old, happier the one who died young, happiest the one who died at birth, happiest of
  all the one never born.
- **Solon is the prize, not the rule.** Croesus, about to be burned, remembered Solon's warning that
  no one should be called happy while still alive, and cried out "Solon" three times. A inverts it:
  whoever is certain he is the unhappiest need never fear good fortune, because he will never have
  to make that cry. The story is from Herodotus.
- **The dead may compete**, because they have lived. A refuses to be sophistical about it.
- **Arm yourselves against the ear.** Before hearing the candidates, A tells the fellowship to
  strengthen their thought against the charm of the ear, because no voice is as ingratiating as an
  unhappy person talking about his own unhappiness, and the eloquence of sorrow is infinitely
  inventive.
- **The title goes to a class, not a person.** A states this before he starts: no single individual
  is the unhappiest, it is a class, and the representative of the class gets the name and the grave.
  That is why the candidates are types.

### The analysis
This is the part lecture 3 covered, so it is in
[03-losses-and-the-unhappy-consciousness.md](../lectures/03-losses-and-the-unhappy-consciousness.md)
in full. The short version, in order:

- The unhappy one has his ideal, his life's content, his real nature "in some way or other outside
  himself". He is "always absent from himself, never present to himself".
- You can only be absent in the past or the future, which maps the whole territory. A thanks Hegel
  for the boundary and takes nothing else from him.
- The grammar: philology knows tenses that are present in a past or a future time, but the pluperfect
  and the future perfect have no present in them at all. Those are the remembering and the hoping
  individuals.
- Merely hoping or merely remembering is not strict unhappiness, so long as you are present *in* the
  hope or the memory. Hence one blow, however heavy, can never produce the unhappiest.
- The hoper becomes strictly unhappy when he hopes for something he himself knows cannot be
  realised. The rememberer, when he remembers a past that never had reality for him.
- Memory is the proper element of the unhappy, and the unhappiest is always found among the
  rememberers, because hope's disappointments are the more gratifying kind.
- The combination: memory blocks his hope and hope blocks his memory. What he hopes for lies behind
  him, what he remembers lies ahead. His life is "back-to-front in two directions".

### The portrait, before the candidates
- He is taken for sane and knows he would be called mad if he explained himself. It is enough to
  drive a man mad and he does not go mad, and that is his misfortune.
- "He has come to the world too soon and is therefore constantly arriving too late."
- Two images for it. **Ancaeus**, king of Samos, was told by a seer that he would not live to drink
  the wine of the vineyard he had planted; he raised the cup to mock the seer, was called away to
  hunt a boar, and was killed by it — which is where the proverb about the slip between cup and lip
  comes from, and A's point is that his life is remembered for nothing else. And **Latona**, who was
  driven from country to country and could not give birth, and was perpetually about to.
- The world confronts him as a single "Thou", and that inseparable, importunate friend is
  **misunderstanding**.
- Then the four-part line: he cannot become old, for he was never young; cannot become young, for he
  is already old; in a way cannot die, for he has never lived; in a way cannot live, for he is
  already dead. And he cannot love, because love is always present and he has no present.

### The gallery — eight stations, in order
A turns from "the circumspect voice of deliberation" to "the eloquence of passion", and the
candidates are paraded. Learn the order and what disqualifies each one.

| # | Who | Why they do or do not win |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | An unnamed young girl whose lover was unfaithful | Dismissed in three lines. She can remember and grieve, so she is present in her memory. |
| 2 | **Niobe** | Boasted she was Leto's equal; Apollo and Artemis killed all twelve of her children and Zeus turned her to stone. She lost everything at one blow, and stands "petrified in memory". | 3 | **Antigone** | Of the outcast house of Oedipus, the blow passed down the generations to the last of them. She has turned her back on hope and traded its inconstancy for memory's loyalty. A wishes her a long life and the daily bitterness of grief in abundance. |

An *accessit* is the runner-up prize for a university prize essay. |
| 8 | The unhappiest one himself, unnamed | The emissary from the kingdom of sighs, the elected favourite of suffering, the apostle of sorrow, the silent friend of pain, and "the unhappy lover of memory" — confounded in his memory by hope's light, deceived in his hope by memory's shadows. A greets him as "great unknown, whose name I do not know". |

Two patterns run through it. Everyone who can still *grieve* is eliminated, because grief means
being present in memory. And the one who wins is the one for whom neither direction works.

### The award, the reversal, and the ending
- A tells the winner the stone is rolled away and the grave's shade is waiting — but the time is
  perhaps not yet come and the way perhaps long. That is why the grave is still empty: the contest
  is open and the winner has not been laid in it.
- Then two opposite wishes, and A offers the second in case the first is too easy. First: may no one
  understand you, no friend attach himself to you, no girl love you, no eye fathom your sorrow, no
  ear catch your sigh. Then, if his proud soul scorns that kind of pity: may the girls love you, may
  pregnant women in their fear come to you, may mothers hope in you, may the dying seek comfort from
  you, may the old lean on you as on a staff — may the whole world believe you can make it happy.
- **The reversal.** A corrects himself: he should have said the happiest, because this is a gift of
  fortune nobody can give himself. Language fails and thought is confounded, for who is the happiest
  except the unhappiest. Then four clauses to know: life is madness, faith is folly, hope is a
  reprieve, love is salt in the wound.
- He vanishes and the fellowship is standing at the empty grave again. A wishes him peace, rest,
  recovery, every good fortune, an early death, an eternal oblivion and no remembrance at all — so
  that not even the memory of him should make anyone else unhappy.
- The last line dismisses the meeting: the night has passed, and the day begins its untiring
  activity again, never tired of repeating itself for ever. The likely source is Ecclesiastes 1,
  where the sun rises and sets and there is nothing new under the sun. So the address ends with no
  winner installed, and the day starting over.

## Clarifications from our discussion (2026-09-16)

### Why grief disqualifies
- You can only grieve what you actually had. Niobe had twelve children and mourns them, so however
  destroyed she is, she has somewhere to stand: her past is real and she is living in it. A's rule is
  that whoever is present in his memory is not the unhappiest.
- Job's verdict is the same rule in one clause: he lost everything, but he had possessed it. Losing
  proves you had it. Having it means your past is real.
- The second girl gets closer than either of them because she has no ground in either direction. She
  cannot grieve, since perhaps he was not a deceiver; she cannot hope, since he was a riddle. She
  does not even know what she lost. Less happened to her than to Niobe, and she ranks higher.
- So the contest is not "who suffered most." It is "who has nothing real behind them and nothing
  real ahead." Grief is proof of something real.

### Presence over pain, and whether that is a stipulation
- A is not measuring how bad anyone feels. He is measuring whether they are anywhere at all. The
  unhappiest one is not in the most pain; A says he has no passion, "not because he lacks it, but
  because that same instant he has the opposite." His condition is cancellation, not intensity. He
  cannot even say what is wrong with him, which is why he would be declared mad if he tried.
- That is a definition, not an argument. A defines unhappiness as absence from yourself, borrows the
  boundary from Hegel, and never defends it. Define unhappiness as suffering instead and Job wins the
  grave. This is a fair objection and the essay has no answer to it.
- The insight underneath the stipulation is real, though: pain you can name is bearable in a way pain
  you cannot place is not. Compare the two girls. The first knows he cheated and can hate him and move
  on. The second cannot close the file. An ambiguous loss is worse than a clear one, even though
  "less happened." That is A's point with the theatre removed.

### Aesthete, not hedonist
- The course word for A is **aesthete**, and it is broader than hedonist. A hedonist wants pleasure. An
  aesthete wants experience: the interesting, the intense, the mood, misery included. A hedonist does
  not found a club to celebrate sorrow on Fridays. A does, and the Symparanekromenoi cultivate
  unhappiness as something to savour.
- The hedonist side of A shows up in the next reading, *Crop Rotation*, where boredom is the root of
  all evil and pleasures are rotated to stay ahead of it. Hedonism is one of A's moods, not his
  definition.

### The empty grave as a chair
- The grave has to stay empty for the essay to work. A says every unhappy visitor feels it was dug
  for him, and that only works if nobody is in it. It is a seat anyone can take, which is also why A
  said at the start that the title belongs to a class rather than a person.
- It also keeps the club's game going. These are aesthetes for whom misery is the Friday hobby; if
  someone were actually buried, the contest would be over. So A crowns a nameless winner, calls him
  the happiest, lets him vanish, and ends with the day starting again. The reversal is the club's
  joke about itself: being the unhappiest is the one prize luck can never take from you.

### Happiness versus presence, which is why this is an existentialism reading
- Your instinct measures a life by happiness. A measures it by whether you are present to your own
  existence. That gap is why this essay sits in an existentialism course: it is the first place the
  book asks "are you actually here?" rather than "are you happy?"
- Judge William picks up the same criterion in volume 2 and gives the answer A never gives: you
  become present by choosing, by committing to something. A diagnoses and walks off.

### "The happiest life is the one with the most experiences"
- That is the aesthetic life-view, stated cleanly, and it is the position the rest of the book puts on
  trial. A himself shows where it breaks in *Crop Rotation*: novelty wears out, every experience gets
  boring, so the pleasures have to be rotated faster and faster. The Symparanekromenoi are the end
  state, people who did experience everything and now count themselves dead.
- The Judge's diagnosis, later, is that a life built on new experiences has no self, only a sequence
  of moods, and depends on the outside world to deliver the next one. His counter is that presence
  comes from commitment, which does not need novelty; the married man, he says, has first love
  continuously rather than once.
- Hold the view if you hold it. Just know it is the claim the book exists to argue about.

## Clarifications on your part-1 notes
- **The title.** "The Unhappiest One. An inspired address to the Symparanekromenoi." The Symparanekromenoi are A's fictional club, the "fellowship of the dead": aesthetes who count themselves already dead and meet to celebrate unhappiness. A is giving them a speech.
- **"Why did he do that?"** The text does not say who opened the grave or why. Only what they found matters: nothing. A quotes the hymn line about peace in the grave to set up the irony. The grave is supposed to be the one place where the unhappy finally rest, and this one is empty. The unhappiest one does not even get that.
- **East and west.** The faithful go east on pilgrimage to the Holy Sepulchre, Christ's tomb in Jerusalem. The unhappy are drawn west to this grave in England. A calls the fellowship crusaders and proposes a pilgrimage to the sorrowful grave in the unhappy west rather than the holy grave in the happy east. Both tombs are empty, which makes the grave a dark mirror of the resurrection. Each unhappy person feels the grave was dug for him, which is how A pulls his listeners into the contest.
- **Who is excluded and why.** The happy, obviously. And anyone who fears death or counts it the greatest misfortune. The fellowship treats death as a rest and calls itself already dead, so someone who fears death still loves life too much to count as unhappy in their sense. Your instinct, that the one who fears death is unhappy, is the ordinary view. A overrides it on purpose: this is a club of people who have given up on life, and the rules are theirs.
- **Solon and Croesus.** From Herodotus. Croesus, the richest king alive, asked Solon who the happiest man was, expecting to hear his own name. Solon named ordinary men who had died well and told him to call no one happy before his death, because fortune can turn. Croesus remembered it too late, on the pyre, crying "Solon, Solon, Solon!" A does not draw the rule you would expect. He turns the saying into the prize: the reward for being certain you are the unhappiest is that you need never fear good fortune, and will never have to make that cry in your last hour. He also refuses to exclude the dead from the competition, on the ground that they have lived.
- **On your Solon line.** "Directly connects to existentialism through absurdity, the illusion of control" is a generic gloss, not something in the text. Keep A's narrow use: Solon gets him to "cannot die".
- **"Death is not the greatest misfortune."** A says this in his own voice. There are worse things. Kraal will test A's claim, not your agreement with it.
- **"The unhappiest one is the one who can't die."** This is the one thing in your notes to unlearn, and I had it wrong too. A raises it only to throw it away. If the legend of the Wandering Jew were true, he says, the empty grave would be explained and the contest settled. Then: but it is not so, death is the common good fortune of all human beings, so the unhappiest must be looked for inside that limit. The immortality answer is the easy answer A refuses.
- **Why the grave is empty, then.** Because the contest is still open and the winner has not yet been laid in it. When A finally hails the unhappiest one at the end, he says the stone is rolled away and the grave's shade awaits him, but the time is perhaps not yet come and the way perhaps long.
- **Where "cannot die" does come back.** In the middle of the essay, hedged, and about absence rather than immortality: he cannot in a way die, for he has not lived; he cannot in a way live, for he is already dead. That is about living in hope or recollection instead of the present, and it is not the answer to why the grave is empty. Part 2 is where it is explained.
