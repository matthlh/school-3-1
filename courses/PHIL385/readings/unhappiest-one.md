## The speech and the empty grave

### The speaker and his audience

- The speaker is A, a fictional aesthete in *Either/Or*. An **aesthete** seeks interesting or intense experiences, including misery. A **hedonist** seeks pleasure. A's club celebrates sorrow, and the emphasis on pleasure is one part of his outlook that appears in *Crop Rotation*.
- A addresses the **Symparanekromenoi**, the fellowship of the already-dead. Its members live "aphoristically", think of themselves as "set apart" and "set apart and segregated", compare themselves to solitary birds in the night and believe only in misfortune.
- One heading recorded here has three printed lines, "The Unhappiest One", "An enthusiastic address to Symparanekromenoi", and "Peroration in the Friday meetings". A **peroration** closes a speech. The framing makes this the last item at the club's regular Friday meeting.
- The original clarifications also give the heading as "The Unhappiest One. An inspired address to the Symparanekromenoi." The two accounts differ in their wording and in whether they include the third line.

### Why the grave matters

- A says a grave somewhere in England bore only the inscription "the unhappiest one". When it was opened, there was no body. The text gives no name or reason for the person who opened it.
- The source is a grave slab marked *Miserrimus* in Worcester Cathedral. Kierkegaard noted it in 1840. The note already imagines someone reading the inscription, concluding the grave is empty and thinking it was meant for him.
- A quotes Christen Pram's *Stærkodder* (1785) on peace in the grave. The empty grave raises the possibility that the unhappiest person cannot even rest in death.
- The empty grave and the stone rolled away near the end echo the women at Christ's tomb in Mark 16. The faithful travel east to the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem. A calls his fellowship crusaders and sends them west to the empty grave in England, in what he calls the unhappy west.
- A considers two explanations. The man might have risen and still be wandering, or he might be someone the fellowship has yet to find. A takes the second possibility. Each unhappy visitor feels the grave was dug for him.

## What qualifies someone for the grave

### Rules of the contest

- The happy cannot win. A also excludes anyone who counts death the greatest misfortune or becomes unhappy from fear of death. The fellowship views death as rest and living as the worse misfortune.
- The dead may compete because they have lived. A refuses to treat their eligibility as a technicality.
- Before hearing candidates, A warns the members against being persuaded by a speaker's voice. He says an unhappy person describing his own sorrow is especially persuasive.
- A awards the title to a class of people, represented by whoever gets the name and the grave. Each candidate illustrates a type of unhappiness.

### Death, the Wandering Jew and Solon

- A considers the Wandering Jew, who would explain the grave if a person could live forever. A rejects the explanation because death is the common good fortune of all human beings. He seeks the unhappiest person among people who can die.
- In the old Greek ranking, it is fortunate to be able to die. Dying old is fortunate, dying young is more fortunate, dying at birth is happier still and never being born is happiest of all.
- In Herodotus, Croesus, the richest king alive, asks Solon who is happiest and expects to hear his own name. Solon names ordinary men who died well and tells him to call no one happy before death, because fortune can change.
- About to be burned, Croesus recalls the warning and cries "Solon" three times, or "Solon, Solon, Solon!" in the allusion list.
- A makes certainty of being the unhappiest the prize. A person certain of that rank need never fear good fortune and never has to make Croesus's cry. The story leads into the question of a person who "cannot die". A's use of the story is narrower than the notes' reading of it as "Directly connects to existentialism through absurdity, the illusion of control".

## What A means by unhappiness

### Absence from the present

- The unhappy person's ideal, life's content or real nature is "in some way or other outside himself". He is "always absent from himself, never present to himself".
- A takes one boundary from Hegel. A person absent from the present can be in the past or the future.
- Philology has tenses with a present relative to the past or future. The pluperfect and future perfect have no present in A's analogy. He uses them for the remembering and hoping individuals.
- Someone can hope or remember and still be present in the hope or the memory. Even one devastating loss cannot by itself produce the unhappiest person.
- The hoper becomes strictly unhappy when he hopes for something he knows cannot happen. The rememberer becomes strictly unhappy when he remembers a past that never had reality for him.
- A calls memory the proper element of the unhappy. Hope's disappointments are more gratifying, so the unhappiest person is found among rememberers.
- In the most unhappy person, memory blocks hope and hope blocks memory. What he hopes for is behind him. What he remembers is ahead of him. His life is "back-to-front in two directions".

### How A describes the unhappiest person

- Others take him for sane. He knows they would call him mad if he could explain himself. A says his condition is enough to drive him mad, and his misfortune is that it does not.
- A says, "He has come to the world too soon and is therefore constantly arriving too late."
- A's first example is **Ancaeus**, king of Samos. A seer told him he would never drink the wine from his own vineyard. Ancaeus lifted the cup to mock the seer, was called away to hunt a boar and was killed. His life is remembered for that incident, the origin of the proverb about the slip between cup and lip.
- His second example is **Latona**, also called Leto. Hera drove her from land to land, leaving her perpetually about to give birth and unable to do so.
- The world confronts the unhappiest person as a single "Thou". The companion he cannot escape is misunderstanding.
- He cannot become old because he was never young. He cannot become young because he is already old.
- In a sense, he cannot die because he has never lived. In a sense, he cannot live because he is already dead. These claims describe absence from the present. The story of the immortal wanderer gave a literal version of "cannot die" that A rejects.
- He cannot love because love takes place in the present, which he lacks.

## The award and the reversal

- The stone is rolled away and the grave is waiting. A says the time may still be far off and the way may be long. The grave stays empty because the winner has yet to be laid in it.
- A first wishes that no one will understand the winner, no friend will attach himself to him, no girl will love him, no eye will see his sorrow and no ear will catch his sigh.
- If the winner's proud soul rejects that pity, A offers the opposite wish. He hopes girls love him, pregnant women come to him in fear, mothers put their hope in him, the dying seek his comfort and the old lean on him. He wishes that everyone will think the winner can make them happy.
- A calls the winner the happiest. The title is a gift of fortune he cannot give himself. Language fails and thought is confounded when A asks who is the happiest except the unhappiest.
- A's four closing claims are that life is madness, faith is folly, hope is a reprieve and love is salt in the wound.
- The winner vanishes. Back at the grave, A wishes him peace, rest, recovery, good fortune, early death and eternal oblivion. He wants nobody to remember him and become unhappy.
- The night ends and the day returns to its untiring activity. It repeats itself forever, likely echoing Ecclesiastes 1, where the sun rises and sets and there is nothing new under the sun.
- The grave stays available to represent a class. Burying an actual person there would end the club's contest. The nameless winner receives a prize that good luck can never take away.

## Clarifications from our discussion (2026-09-16)

### Why grief disqualifies

- For A, you can grieve something you actually had. Niobe can be present in her past because her twelve children were real. Job's loss likewise proves he possessed what he lost.
- The second girl ranks higher because her loss is ambiguous. She cannot decide what she lost or whether her lover deceived her. The first girl knows he cheated and can grieve, hate him and move on.
- A ranks absence from oneself ahead of the intensity of pain. The unhappiest person has no passion, "not because he lacks it, but because that same instant he has the opposite." His feelings cancel each other, leaving him unable to say what is wrong.
- This ranking follows A's definition. A takes the boundary from Hegel without arguing for it. If the contest measured suffering, Job would win. The comparison between the two girls illustrates why an uncertain loss can be harder to bear even when "less happened."

### Presence and commitment

- In the discussion, the notes measure a life by happiness. A asks "are you actually here?" in place of "are you happy?" He measures whether a person is present to their own existence.
- "The happiest life is the one with the most experiences" states the aesthetic view that the rest of the book examines. In *Crop Rotation*, A calls boredom the root of all evil and rotates pleasures to stay ahead of it. Novelty wears off, so the rotations speed up. The Symparanekromenoi have experienced everything and count themselves dead.
- In volume 2, Judge William takes up presence and connects it to choosing and committing. A diagnoses absence from oneself and offers no practical answer.
- Judge William says a life built on new experiences depends on the outside world to supply the next mood and gives a person no stable self. Commitment provides continuity without needing novelty. In his example, the married man experiences first love continuously.

## Clarifications on your part-1 notes

- The "fellowship of the dead" is A's fictional club. Its speech frames the contest around an empty grave.
- "Why did he do that?" The text leaves the opening of the grave unexplained. The empty grave reverses the expectation that the unhappy can finally rest there.
- "Death is not the greatest misfortune." This is A's position. The fellowship's rule treats fear of death as a sign that someone still values life. In the ordinary sense, a person who fears death can still be unhappy.
- "The unhappiest one is the one who can't die." This note gives the literal answer that A considers and rejects. In part 2, he says in a qualified sense that the unhappiest person cannot die because he has never lived.
