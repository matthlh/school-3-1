## The speech and the empty grave

### The speaker and his audience

- The speaker is A, not Kierkegaard.
- The full heading is three lines, and all three are printed in the book. They are "The Unhappiest One", then "An enthusiastic address to Symparanekromenoi", then "Peroration in the Friday meetings".
- The title is also rendered "The Unhappiest One. An inspired address to the Symparanekromenoi."
- A **peroration** is the closing part of a speech. So the piece presents itself as the last item at a regular Friday meeting of the club, not as an essay.
- A addresses the **Symparanekromenoi**, the fellowship of the already-dead. They are A's fictional club of aesthetes who count themselves already dead and meet to celebrate unhappiness.
- The members describe themselves as living "aphoristically", set apart and segregated, solitary birds in the night, believing in nothing but misfortune.
- The Greek and Latin words A uses for "set apart" are the ones Paul uses of himself in Romans 1:1.

### The grave and its source

- A reports a grave somewhere in England marked only with the inscription "the unhappiest one". It was opened, and no body was found.
- The text does not say who opened the grave or why. Only what they found matters, which is nothing.
- The real source is a grave slab inscribed *Miserrimus* in Worcester Cathedral. Kierkegaard noted it in 1840.
- Kierkegaard's note already contains the whole idea. Someone might read the inscription, conclude nobody was buried there at all, and think the grave was meant for him.
- The lines of verse A quotes about peace in the grave are from Christen Pram's epic *Stærkodder* of 1785.
- A quotes them to set up the irony. The grave is supposed to be the one place where the unhappy finally rest, and this one is empty, so the unhappiest one does not even get that.
- The empty grave is a deliberate Easter allusion. So is the line near the end of the essay about the stone being rolled away. Both echo the women at Christ's tomb in Mark 16.

### East and west

- A poses two possibilities and takes the second. Either the man has risen and wanders the world, or he simply has not been found yet. So the fellowship sets off west as crusaders.
- The faithful go east on pilgrimage to the Holy Sepulchre, Christ's tomb in Jerusalem. The unhappy are drawn west to this grave in England.
- A calls the fellowship crusaders. He proposes a pilgrimage to the sorrowful grave in the unhappy west rather than the holy grave in the happy east.
- Both tombs are empty, which makes the grave a dark mirror of the resurrection.
- Each unhappy person feels the grave was dug for him, which is how A pulls his listeners into the contest.

## The rules of the contest

### Who is shut out

- The happy are shut out.
- So is anyone who counts death the greatest misfortune, and anyone who became unhappy because he feared death.
- The fellowship does not fear death. It treats death as a rest, calls itself already dead, and knows a worse misfortune, which is living.
- So someone who fears death still loves life too much to count as unhappy in the fellowship's sense.

### The man who cannot die is considered and rejected

- A raises the legend of the Wandering Jew. If such a man existed, the grave would be explained and the contest over.
- Then A cancels it. Death is the common good fortune of all human beings, so the unhappiest must be sought inside that limit.
- On the way past, A runs the old Greek ladder.
    - Happy is the one who could die.
    - Happy is the one who died old.
    - Happier is the one who died young.
    - Happiest is the one who died at birth.
    - Happiest of all is the one never born.

### Solon is the prize, not the rule

- The story is from Herodotus. Croesus, the richest king alive, asked Solon who the happiest man was, expecting to hear his own name.
- Solon named ordinary men who had died well. He told Croesus that no one should be called happy while still alive, because fortune can turn.
- Croesus remembered the warning too late. About to be burned on the pyre, he cried out "Solon, Solon, Solon!"
- A inverts the saying and turns it into the prize. Whoever is certain he is the unhappiest need never fear good fortune, because he will never have to make that cry in his last hour.

### The other rules

- **The dead may compete**, because they have lived. A refuses to be sophistical about it.
- **Arm yourselves against the ear.** Before hearing the candidates, A tells the fellowship to strengthen their thought against the charm of the ear.
- A's reason is that no voice is as ingratiating as an unhappy person talking about his own unhappiness, and the eloquence of sorrow is infinitely inventive.
- **The title goes to a class, not a person.** A states this before he starts. No single individual is the unhappiest, because it is a class, and the representative of the class gets the name and the grave.
- That is why the candidates are types.

## What A means by unhappiness

Lecture 3 covered this part, and the [lecture 3 page](../lectures/03-losses-and-the-unhappy-consciousness.md) has it in full. These are the steps in order.

### Absence from the present

- The unhappy one has his ideal, his life's content, his real nature "in some way or other outside himself". He is "always absent from himself, never present to himself".
- You can only be absent in the past or the future, and those two map the whole territory.
- A thanks Hegel for that boundary and takes nothing else from him.
- Philology knows tenses that are present in a past or a future time. The pluperfect and the future perfect have no present in them at all. Those two are the remembering and the hoping individuals.
- Merely hoping or merely remembering is not strict unhappiness, so long as you are present *in* the hope or the memory.
- Hence one blow, however heavy, can never produce the unhappiest.
- The hoper becomes strictly unhappy when he hopes for something he himself knows cannot be realised.
- The rememberer becomes strictly unhappy when he remembers a past that never had reality for him.
- Memory is the proper element of the unhappy. The unhappiest is always found among the rememberers, because hope's disappointments are the more gratifying kind.
- In the combination, memory blocks his hope and hope blocks his memory. What he hopes for lies behind him, and what he remembers lies ahead. His life is "back-to-front in two directions".

### The portrait, before the candidates

- He is taken for sane, and he knows he would be called mad if he explained himself.
- It is enough to drive a man mad, and he does not go mad. That is his misfortune.
- A says, "He has come to the world too soon and is therefore constantly arriving too late."
- A gives two images for it, Ancaeus and Latona.
- **Ancaeus**, king of Samos, was told by a seer that he would not live to drink the wine of the vineyard he had planted. He raised the cup to mock the seer, was called away to hunt a boar, and was killed by it.
- The proverb about the slip between cup and lip comes from Ancaeus. A's point is that his life is remembered for nothing else.
- **Latona** was driven from country to country and could not give birth. She was perpetually about to.
- The world confronts him as a single "Thou". That inseparable, importunate friend is **misunderstanding**.
- He cannot become old, for he was never young. He cannot become young, for he is already old.
- In a way he cannot die, for he has never lived. In a way he cannot live, for he is already dead.
- He cannot love, because love is always present and he has no present.

Two patterns run through the gallery.

- Everyone who can still *grieve* is eliminated, because grief means being present in memory.
- The one who wins is the one for whom neither direction works.

## The award, the reversal and the ending

- A tells the winner the stone is rolled away and the grave's shade is waiting. But the time is perhaps not yet come, and the way is perhaps long.
- That is why the grave is still empty. The contest is open, and the winner has not been laid in it.
- Then A makes two opposite wishes, and he offers the second in case the first is too easy.
- In the first wish, A asks that no one understand the winner, no friend attach himself to him, no girl love him, no eye fathom his sorrow and no ear catch his sigh.
- The second wish is for the case where the winner's proud soul scorns that kind of pity. A asks that the girls love him, pregnant women in their fear come to him, mothers hope in him, the dying seek comfort from him and the old lean on him as on a staff. He asks that the whole world believe the winner can make it happy.
- **The reversal.** A corrects himself and says he should have called the winner the happiest, because this is a gift of fortune nobody can give himself.
- Language fails and thought is confounded, for who is the happiest except the unhappiest.
- Then come four clauses to know. Life is madness, faith is folly, hope is a reprieve and love is salt in the wound.
- The winner vanishes, and the fellowship is standing at the empty grave again.
- A wishes him peace, rest, recovery, every good fortune, an early death, an eternal oblivion and no remembrance at all. He wishes this so that not even the memory of him should make anyone else unhappy.
- The last line dismisses the meeting. The night has passed, and the day begins its untiring activity again, never tired of repeating itself for ever.
- The likely source is Ecclesiastes 1, where the sun rises and sets and there is nothing new under the sun.
- So the address ends with no winner installed and the day starting over.

## Clarifications from our discussion (2026-09-16)

### Why grief disqualifies

- You can only grieve what you actually had.
- Niobe had twelve children and mourns them. However destroyed she is, she has somewhere to stand, because her past is real and she is living in it.
- A's rule is that whoever is present in his memory is not the unhappiest.
- Job's verdict is the same rule in one clause: he lost everything, but he had possessed it. Losing proves you had it, and having it means your past is real.
- The second girl gets closer than either of them because she has no ground in either direction. She cannot grieve, since perhaps he was not a deceiver. She cannot hope, since he was a riddle.
- The second girl does not even know what she lost. Less happened to her than to Niobe, and she ranks higher.
- So the contest is not "who suffered most." It is "who has nothing real behind them and nothing real ahead." Grief is proof of something real.

### Presence over pain, and whether that is a stipulation

- A is not measuring how bad anyone feels. He is measuring whether they are anywhere at all.
- The unhappiest one is not in the most pain. A says he has no passion, "not because he lacks it, but because that same instant he has the opposite."
- His condition is cancellation, not intensity. He cannot even say what is wrong with him, which is why he would be declared mad if he tried.
- That is a definition, not an argument. A defines unhappiness as absence from yourself, borrows the boundary from Hegel and never defends it.
- If you define unhappiness as suffering instead, Job wins the grave. This is a fair objection, and the essay has no answer to it.
- The insight underneath the stipulation is real, though. Pain you can name is bearable in a way that pain you cannot place is not.
- Compare the two girls. The first knows he cheated and can hate him and move on. The second cannot close the file.
- An ambiguous loss is worse than a clear one, even though "less happened." That is A's point with the theatre removed.

### Aesthete, not hedonist

- The course word for A is **aesthete**, and it is broader than hedonist.
- A hedonist wants pleasure. An aesthete wants experience, meaning the interesting, the intense and the mood, misery included.
- A hedonist does not found a club to celebrate sorrow on Fridays. A does, and the Symparanekromenoi cultivate unhappiness as something to savour.
- The hedonist side of A shows up in the next reading, *Crop Rotation*, where boredom is the root of all evil and pleasures are rotated to stay ahead of it.
- Hedonism is one of A's moods, not his definition.

### The empty grave as a chair

- The grave has to stay empty for the essay to work. A says every unhappy visitor feels it was dug for him, and that only works if nobody is in it.
- The grave is a seat anyone can take. That is also why A said at the start that the title belongs to a class rather than a person.
- The empty grave also keeps the club's game going. These are aesthetes for whom misery is the Friday hobby, and if someone were actually buried, the contest would be over.
- So A crowns a nameless winner, calls him the happiest, lets him vanish, and ends with the day starting again.
- The reversal is the club's joke about itself. Being the unhappiest is the one prize luck can never take from you.

### Happiness versus presence, which is why this is an existentialism reading

- Your instinct measures a life by happiness. A measures it by whether you are present to your own existence.
- That gap is why this essay sits in an existentialism course. It is the first place the book asks "are you actually here?" rather than "are you happy?"
- Judge William picks up the same criterion in volume 2. He gives the answer A never gives, which is that you become present by choosing, by committing to something.
- A diagnoses and walks off.

### "The happiest life is the one with the most experiences"

- That is the aesthetic life-view, stated cleanly, and it is the position the rest of the book puts on trial.
- A himself shows where it breaks in *Crop Rotation*. Novelty wears out and every experience gets boring, so the pleasures have to be rotated faster and faster.
- The Symparanekromenoi are the end state. They are people who did experience everything and now count themselves dead.
- The Judge's diagnosis, later, is that a life built on new experiences has no self, only a sequence of moods, and depends on the outside world to deliver the next one.
- His counter is that presence comes from commitment, which does not need novelty. The married man, he says, has first love continuously rather than once.

## Clarifications on your part-1 notes

- **"Why did he do that?"** The text does not say who opened the grave or why. Only what they found matters, which is nothing.
- **Who is excluded and why.** Your instinct, that the one who fears death is unhappy, is the ordinary view. A overrides it on purpose, because this is a club of people who have given up on life, and the rules are theirs.
- **On your Solon line.** "Directly connects to existentialism through absurdity, the illusion of control" is a generic gloss, not something in the text. Keep A's narrow use, which is that Solon gets him to "cannot die".
- **"Death is not the greatest misfortune."** A says this in his own voice, and there are worse things. Kraal will test A's claim, not your agreement with it.
- **"The unhappiest one is the one who can't die."** This is the one thing in your notes to unlearn. A raises it only to throw it away, and the immortality answer is the easy answer A refuses.
- **Where "cannot die" does come back.** It comes back in the middle of the essay, hedged, and about absence rather than immortality. In a way he cannot die, for he has not lived, and in a way he cannot live, for he is already dead.
- That passage is about living in hope or recollection instead of the present. It is not the answer to why the grave is empty, and part 2 is where it is explained.
