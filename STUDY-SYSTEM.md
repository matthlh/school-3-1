# The process

Answering your question directly: **preview → notes → package is the wrong shape.** Two of
those three steps are low-return. Here's what the evidence supports, and what I've replaced
them with.

## What the research actually says

Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated ten common study techniques. Only **two** came out "high utility":
**practice testing** and **distributed practice**. A later meta-analysis over 242 studies and
~169,000 participants reached the same two. Rated *low* utility: **rereading, highlighting,
and summarization** — which is exactly what most note-taking is.

Specific numbers worth knowing:
- **Retrieval beats rereading.** Roediger & Karpicke: 80% retained after a week with recall
  practice vs. 34% with rereading.
- **Interleaving beats blocking**, hugely, on delayed tests: 63% vs. 20% at one week
  (d = 1.34); 61% vs. 38% at one month in a ~800-student trial. Note the catch — interleaving
  feels *worse* during practice (60% vs 89% accuracy). It's supposed to. Don't bail on it.
- **Passive previewing doesn't work.** Students who read preview material before a lecture did
  not outperform those who didn't — but they *believed* they had. **Pre-testing does work**,
  even when the pre-test gives no answers.

So: previewing survives only if you convert it into pre-testing. Note-taking survives only if
the notes are questions. Packaging doesn't survive at all — replace it with spaced retrieval.

## The loop

### 1. Pre-lecture — 90 seconds
Open the slides. Read **titles only**. Write 3 questions you can't yet answer at the top of
your page. Don't read the content. You're setting up a pre-test, not previewing.

### 2. In lecture — your half page, restructured
Same volume you planned. But split the page into two columns:

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|

Every line is something you could be asked. `"Connascence of name vs. type — what's the
difference and which is weaker?"` not `"Connascence: types are name, type, meaning..."`

Same ink, same time. The difference is that a question page is a self-test and a statement
page is a reread. This is the single highest-leverage change in this whole document.

### 3. Same day — 10 minutes
Photograph or type the page into `courses/<CODE>/lectures/`. Cover the answer column, test
yourself once, mark each line `X` / `~` / `O`. Then tell me **"log CPSC310 lec 7"** — I pull
your questions into the course bank and the ledger, and the spacing starts.

### 4. Friday — one retrieval block, ~2 hours
I quiz you across all courses, interleaved, weighted toward what's overdue and what you've
missed before. This is where the actual learning happens. Everything above just feeds it.

### 5. Sunday — 20 min, bolted onto your existing Weekly Review
Update the ledger, look at the week's due topics, adjust.

## Weekly time budget

Four courses (321 dropped):

| Block | Hours |
|---|---|
| Lecture close-outs (9 lectures × 10 min) | 1.5 |
| STAT 251 — timed problem redo | 1.5 |
| CPSC 310 — deliverable work | 3.5 |
| ASIA 250 + PHIL 385 — reading w/ argument mapping | 1.5 |
| Friday retrieval block | 2.0 |
| Sunday ledger + planning | 0.5 |
| Saturday flex (deliverable & essay weeks only) | 2.5 |
| **Total outside class** | **~13** |

Plus ~15 h of class = **~28 h/week**. With five courses add ~6 h → ~34 h.

## Course-specific tactics

**CPSC 310.** The 26W1 grading is 5% participation / 10% labs / **20% project** / **25% midterm /
40% final**. That is 65% exams — and it is *not* what the course's reputation says. Students who
took it under the old project-heavy scheme will tell you the project is everything. For your
term that advice is wrong and following it will cost you the grade. Both exams are closed-book,
no notes, no devices, and ask you to *justify* answers. Past finals were described as heavy on
memorized specifics and trick questions. Treat the vocabulary like a language exam.

**STAT 251.** Students consistently report the final is fair but *long*. Redoing the WeBWorK
problems is the highest-rated tactic. Redo them from a blank page with a timer — you are
training speed, not comprehension.

**PHIL 385 / ASIA 250.** The best advice from a UBC philosophy student: most of their studying
was walking and mentally reconstructing each argument, not reading notes. They could usually
predict 1–2 exam questions from what the prof emphasized in class. So: track prof emphasis
explicitly, and rehearse arguments out loud. ASIA 250 has no exams — 22% quizzes on lectures *and*
readings, two SWRs, and a 47% paper on the novel. A third of Shooklyn's 2025W class scored 90+;
the cheap points are real, bank them.
